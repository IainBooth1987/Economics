Confidence intervals made simple

A survey gives you a range, not one exact number. Learn to read that range and use it to make a decision.

STEP 1 · THE PROBLEM

A business can't ask everyone

Brew Box is a coffee subscription company in Leeds with 20,000 customers. It wants to launch oat-milk lattes.

It can't ask all 20,000 customers, because that would take too long and cost too much. So it asks 400 of them. This smaller group is called a sample.

Of the 400 customers asked, 58% said they would buy.

Does this mean exactly 58% of all 20,000 customers will buy?

So we need a way of saying "about 58%, give or take a bit". That's what a confidence interval does.

This is the whole idea behind confidence intervals.

STEP 3 · KEY WORD 1

Confidence interval = the range

Confidence interval: the range of values that the real answer is likely to be in.

Brew Box's survey said 58%. The interval might be 53% to 63%. The real figure for all 20,000 customers is probably somewhere in that range.

The dot is the survey result (the best guess). The green box is the confidence interval.

A report says: "The real figure is likely to be between 48% and 56%." What is "48% to 56%"?

STEP 4 · KEY WORD 2

Confidence level = how reliable the range is

Confidence level: how reliable the method for working out the range is. It's usually 90%, 95% or 99%.

The simple version: a 95% confidence level means we've used a very reliable method. It gives a range that catches the real answer most of the time.

The accurate version (learn this one): if we took lots of different samples and worked out a range from each one, about 95% of those ranges would contain the real answer.

Confidence levelIn plain English
90%About 9 in every 10 ranges worked out this way would catch the real answer.
95%About 19 in every 20 ranges would catch it. This is the one used most often.
99%About 99 in every 100 ranges would catch it.

See it for yourself. The real figure is 50% (the dashed line). Each bar is the 95% range from a different survey of 200 people.

STEP 5 · SAY IT PROPERLY

The magic sentence

Learn this sentence. It earns marks every time you use it in an exam:

"We are __% confident that the true figure is between __ and __."

STEP 6 · CAN YOU CALCULATE IT?

Working out the range from a margin of error

Sometimes you're given the best guess and a margin of error (the "give or take" amount, written with a ± sign), and you have to work out the range yourself.

Lower limit = best guess − margin of error
Upper limit = best guess + margin of error

Worked example

A supermarket surveys 100 customers. Average spending is £40, with a margin of error of ±£3 (95% confidence).

Lower limit = 40 − 3 = £37
Upper limit = 40 + 3 = £43
Confidence interval = £37 to £43

What it means: we are 95% confident that the average spend of all the supermarket's customers is between £37 and £43. It can plan on an average of roughly £37 to £43 per customer.

Watch out: this is about the average customer. Some individual customers will spend £5 and others £150.

STEP 9 · THE TRADE-OFF

Being sure AND precise costs money

Every business wants a range that is both narrow (useful) and very sure (95% or 99%). The only way to get both is to ask more people.

What the business wantsWhat it has to doThe catch
A narrower rangeSurvey more peopleCosts more money and takes more time
To be more sureUse a higher confidence levelThe range gets wider (less useful)
BothSurvey a lot more peopleThe most expensive option
It may not have the money or time for a big survey. A rough idea from a small sample is better than no research at all, as long as it knows the result is uncertain.
If it's about to spend millions on a launch, getting it wrong is very expensive. Paying extra for a precise result is worth it to reduce the risk.
STEP 10 · USE IT TO DECIDE

The traffic-light rule

A business usually has a target line, the % of customers it needs to buy for the idea to be worth it (for example, to break even). Compare the whole range with that line:

GREEN: the whole range is above the line.
Even the bottom of the range is good enough, so the evidence supports going ahead.
AMBER: the line is inside the range.
It could go either way. It's risky, so do more research or be cautious.
RED: the whole range is below the line.
Even the best case isn't good enough, so don't do it.
Green is not a guarantee. It means the research evidence is good, not that the launch will definitely succeed. The real answer could still be outside the range, the sample could be biased (for example, only loyal customers were asked), people don't always do what they say in a survey, and things can change (a new competitor, or a recession).

STEP 11 · FORECAST GRAPHS

Reading a sales forecast graph

AQA often shows confidence intervals on a sales forecast graph. It looks complicated, but there are only three parts:

1. The solid line
Real sales from past years. These figures are known.
2. The fan
The forecast for next year. It spreads out because the future is uncertain.
3. Top and bottom of the fan
The two ends of the confidence interval.
Read the top and bottom of the fan, then use the magic sentence:
"We are 90% confident that sales in 2027 will be between £260,000 and £320,000."
Worked example: the % calculation AQA loves
Calculate the maximum % increase in sales between 2026 and 2027.
Step 1: Last real figure (2026) = £280k. Highest forecast (2027) = £320k.
Step 2: Change = 320 − 280 = 40
Step 3: 40 ÷ 280 × 100 = 14.3%
For the minimum change, use the bottom of the fan instead: (260 − 280) ÷ 280 × 100 = −7.1%. That's a fall!
The further ahead you forecast, the more things can change (the economy, competitors, customer tastes), so the business is less sure, and the range has to be wider to stay 90% confident.
So it can plan for the worst case as well as the best case. For example, it can check it still has enough cash if sales only reach the bottom of the fan.

STEP 13 · EXAM PRACTICE

A 6-mark Analyse question

Brew Box surveyed 400 customers. 58% said they would buy oat-milk lattes. At the 95% confidence level, the interval is 53% to 63%. Brew Box needs at least 50% of customers to buy for the launch to break even.

Analyse why the confidence interval might help Brew Box decide whether to launch oat-milk lattes. [6 marks]

Build one chain of reasoning. Fill in each box:

Point
Because
This means
So

Model answer:

The confidence interval shows Brew Box that we are 95% confident the true proportion of customers who would buy oat-milk lattes is between 53% and 63%. Because even the bottom of this range (53%) is above the 50% break-even level, Brew Box can be fairly sure the launch would at least break even. This means the risk of losing money on the launch is low, as the result is very unlikely to be a fluke of the sample. So Brew Box can launch with more confidence, and is less likely to waste money on stock and marketing for a product that doesn't sell, which helps protect its profit.

However, the survey only reflects what customers say they will do. If the sample was not representative (for example, only loyal customers replied), the interval could be misleading.

STEP 14 · SUMMARY

Six things to remember

  1. A survey result is a best guess, not an exact answer.
  2. Confidence interval = the range the real answer is likely to be in.
  3. Confidence level = how reliable the method is. At 95%, about 19 out of every 20 ranges made this way would contain the real answer.
  4. Lower limit = best guess − margin of error. Upper limit = best guess + margin of error.
  5. More people asked means a narrower range. More sure means a wider range. Narrow and sure costs more money.
  6. Compare the whole range with the target: green = good evidence to go ahead (not a guarantee), amber = risky, red = don't.
