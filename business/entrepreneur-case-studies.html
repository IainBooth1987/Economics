The spec in a nutshell

▸

Reasons for setting up in business split into two types. Financial reasons are about money: earning an income, building wealth, achieving financial security. Non-financial reasons are everything else: independence, flexibility, pursuing a passion, supporting a community, proving something to yourself. Most real entrepreneurs have a mix of both — the case studies in the other tabs show this clearly.

Characteristics of entrepreneurs are the personal qualities that tend to show up again and again: being a risk taker (willing to risk time, money or security on an unproven idea), resilient (bouncing back from setbacks and rejection), focused (staying on track towards a clear goal), passionate (genuinely caring about the product or cause), innovative (coming up with original ideas or approaches) and adaptable (changing plans as circumstances change).

Challenges of setting up in business commonly include: getting access to finance, managing cash flow in the early months, meeting legal and regulatory requirements, having enough time and skills to cover every part of the business, and competing against established rivals.

How the entrepreneur's role changes as a business grows: most founders start out doing everything themselves — production, sales, finance, marketing — hands-on and close to the product. As a business grows, the founder typically has to hand over day-to-day tasks, bring in specialist staff or managers, and shift towards a more strategic role: planning, leading and representing the brand rather than doing every job personally.

Legal and regulatory requirements
Economies of scale

Answer: Legal and regulatory requirements — Registering the business, understanding tax obligations and meeting health & safety rules are all legal and regulatory requirements a new business has to sort out before it can trade properly.

Limited skills and time
Access to finance
Brand loyalty

Answer: Limited skills and time — Many start-ups rely on one or two people covering every business function — marketing, finance, hiring, production — which limits how much can realistically get done.

5. A new café opens near two well-established coffee shops with loyal regulars, and struggles to attract customers in its first year. This illustrates the challenge of:

Competition from established businesses
Protecting intellectual property
Currency risk
Diseconomies of scale

Answer: Competition from established businesses — New businesses often have to fight for customers against rivals who already have loyal, established customer bases — a genuine barrier to gaining market share.

Protecting the business idea

1

Grandma's recipe, a rucksack and Notting Hill Carnival

Late 1970s–2006 ▸

Levi Roots — born Keith Valentine Graham in Jamaica — moved to the UK as a young man, bringing with him a hot pepper sauce recipe he said had been passed down from his grandmother. Music came first for years: he performed as a reggae singer around London and released several albums. But the sauce never left him. He began cooking it in his own kitchen and selling it, bottle by bottle, from a stall at Notting Hill Carnival and local markets, carrying it out on his back and later with his children helping him bottle it at home.

He wasn't chasing a food empire at this point. He was combining two things he already loved — music and Caribbean cooking — and building a loyal following one carnival stall at a time. When he first tried expanding into shops in Brixton, it flopped: locals there already knew how to make their own hot sauce. It was mainstream shoppers outside that community, drawn to the specific flavour of his sauce, who kept buying.

He equates his early success to "putting himself into the product" — his personality and heritage were never separate from what he was selling.leviroots.com, company history

2

Knocking on doors that wouldn't open

Before 2007 ▸

To grow beyond his home kitchen, Levi needed proper premises, equipment and start-up capital — so he approached banks and other businesses for finance. He came away empty-handed every time. He later described feeling that he'd been judged for who he was rather than the strength of his product, using the phrase "too black" to sum up how the response felt to him. Whether it was prejudice, general caution about lending to unproven food businesses, or both, the practical result was the same: he stayed stuck producing at kitchen-scale for years, unable to meet the demand he already had.

The turning point came almost by chance: a television producer spotted him at a food trade show and invited him to pitch on a BBC programme he'd never actually watched.

3

Guitar in hand, into the Den

2007 ▸

Levi's own children begged him not to go on Dragons' Den, warning him he'd be torn apart. He went anyway — guitar included — and opened his pitch by singing a jingle for his sauce rather than starting with a spreadsheet. It worked as theatre: the Dragons tasted the sauce and warmed to him immediately.

But when the questions turned to numbers, it went wrong. Asked how he'd handle a large supermarket order, he mixed up his quantities badly, at one point describing an order of 2.5 million litres when the real figure was 2,500 kilograms. Several Dragons dropped out on the spot, unconvinced he could handle scale. Two, Peter Jones and Richard Farleigh, stayed in and offered him the full £50,000 he'd asked for — but for a 40% stake, double the 20% he'd originally offered, and a much bigger slice of the business than he might have given up if he'd known his figures cold.

He's said he'd never even watched the show before his own producer-arranged appearance, and had to go home and ask his own children what Dragons' Den actually was.reported in Levi Roots profiles, including Time Out London, paraphrased

4

From kitchen table to 600 supermarkets

2007–2008 ▸

The £50,000 investment let Levi move out of his home kitchen for the first time: proper premises, proper equipment, and manufacturing at a scale a domestic kitchen could never have reached. Peter Jones used his contacts to get Reggae Reggae Sauce listed in Sainsbury's, and within weeks of the broadcast it was on shelves in 600 stores — reportedly outselling even Heinz Tomato Ketchup at points.

None of this happened because the recipe changed. It happened because Levi suddenly had to solve problems he'd never faced as a solo kitchen producer: hitting supermarket order volumes, meeting food safety and quality standards at scale, and keeping a consistent product as demand exploded almost overnight.

5

A brand built on a story — and a story that got tested in court

2008–today ▸

Reggae Reggae Sauce is no longer one product — it's a brand with dozens of lines, including marinades, ready meals and snacks, with Levi Roots' own wealth estimated by the Sunday Times Rich List at around £30 million. Levi Roots hasn't spent that growth in a kitchen. His time now goes on brand partnerships, media appearances, mentoring other entrepreneurs, cookbooks, and speaking to businesses about scaling up — a long way from bottling sauce with his children at the table.

But in 2011, the brand's founding story was tested in the High Court. Tony Bailey, a friend from Levi's Brixton years, sued for a share of the profits — reportedly over £600,000 — claiming he, not Levi's grandmother, had actually developed the recipe. Under oath, Levi admitted that the "grandmother's recipe" story he'd used on Dragons' Den and in years of marketing was not true. Even so, the judge dismissed Bailey's claim, ruling it "a dishonest claim, dishonestly advanced" and finding no evidence Bailey had invented the sauce either. Lawyers estimated the case had cost more than £1 million between them, with Levi entitled to have his own costs paid.

What's interesting is what stayed the same afterwards: Levi remained the public face of the brand, and it kept growing. He's said in interviews that his personal story is central to the brand's identity — even after a court case had shown that one central part of that story wasn't literally true.

The judge's ruling on Bailey's claim was blunt: "This was a dishonest claim, dishonestly advanced."Reported by the Guardian and BBC News following the 2011 High Court judgment

1

A damp shop, £4,000, and two kids to feed

1976 ▸

Anita Roddick opened the first Body Shop in Brighton in March 1976 with no training, no business experience, and a straightforward reason: her husband Gordon had set off on a two-year horseback trek across the Americas, and she needed a livelihood for herself and their two young daughters. Years of travelling — Tahiti, South Africa, India — had shown her how different cultures used natural, locally sourced ingredients for skin and hair care, and that became the idea behind her small range of products.

Getting the money wasn't simple. She asked a Barclays bank manager for a £4,000 loan and, by her own account, he wouldn't lend it to her directly — only once Gordon appeared alongside her, in a pin-striped suit, with a profit and loss sheet neither of them fully understood, did the manager hand over the money, and even then to Gordon rather than to her. She painted the shop's damp, patchy walls green simply because it was the only colour that covered the marks — it later became the brand's signature colour.

She told the story herself years later: the bank manager wouldn't hand her the money directly, so Gordon came with her in a suit and the loan went to him instead — "not me," as she put it.Anita Roddick, recorded interview transcript (New Media Rights)
She later put it bluntly: "Nobody talks of entrepreneurship as survival, but that's exactly what it is."Anita Roddick, widely quoted remark

2

A second shop, and a franchise model nobody had a name for

1976–1984 ▸

The first shop was such an immediate success that Anita wanted a second one before her first year of trading was even up. The bank turned her down again, so a friend introduced her to Ian McGlinn, a local car dealer, who agreed to put in the same £4,000 in exchange for a full half-share of the business — a deal struck before Gordon, away on his trek, could write back with his objections. When Gordon returned from his trek, he suggested a way to keep opening new shops without going back to the bank each time: get new shop owners to fund their own stores under the Body Shop name. Neither of them knew the word "franchising" at the time — they built the system as they went, starting with friends and acquaintances who believed in the idea.

McGlinn's £4,000 stake would later be worth well over £100 million as the company grew.Widely reported, including in contemporary UK press profiles of McGlinn

The selection process stayed deliberately unconventional as it grew: Anita has said she didn't want franchisees who were purely business-minded, and recruited teachers and activists instead, reportedly asking prospective franchisees unusual personal questions rather than just checking their finances. By 1984, the company had 138 shops, the majority of them outside the UK, and it went public on the London Stock Exchange that same year.

3

Refills, causes, and a shop with a conscience

1980s–1990s ▸

Some of the Body Shop's most distinctive features started as practical fixes rather than grand plans. Anita has said she offered a discount when customers brought their bottles back for refilling mainly because she simply didn't have enough new bottles to go around — not out of an early environmental masterplan. But it fitted perfectly with what the brand became: products against animal testing, campaigns for human rights and the environment, and a refusal to advertise in the conventional way, relying instead on word of mouth and each shop's window displays to spread campaign messages.

Roddick was open that this focus on causes sometimes came at a commercial cost. She kept campaigning hard on issues she personally cared about even when it didn't obviously help sales, because she believed — and said publicly — that a business should be about more than making money.

She summed up her philosophy directly: "The business of business should not just be about money, it should be about responsibility."Roddick, Business As Unusual (2001)

4

"We're having to grow up"

1990s ▸

By the mid-1990s, the cosmetics market had caught up: bigger competitors were copying the natural-ingredients, ethical-brand approach that had once made the Body Shop unique, and growth slowed. Journalists also began questioning whether some of the company's ethical claims held up under scrutiny, putting its reputation under pressure for the first time. Roddick admitted in a Fortune magazine interview that the loose, friendship-based way the business had been run couldn't keep working at this size: formal management processes and a proper hierarchy had to be introduced, something she found difficult given how the company had grown up.

This is the point where Anita Roddick, the entrepreneur who once ran her business on instinct and personal relationships, had to become something closer to the chief executive of a large public company, answering to shareholders and managing layers of staff rather than a handful of friends.

5

Walking away from the company she built

2001–2007 ▸

In 2001, Anita and Gordon Roddick resigned from the Body Shop's board. By then the company had around 1,700 stores in 46 countries — a scale most founders would call total success — but Roddick reportedly described what it had become as something closer to a "dysfunctional coffin," feeling it had drifted from the values she'd built it on in order to satisfy market demands. Rather than stay and keep steering the company commercially, she redirected her energy into campaigning full-time: she launched her own publishing company for human rights and environmental causes, describing its output as "weapons of mass instruction."

In 2006 the family sold the Body Shop to L'Oréal for £652 million; Roddick personally received around £130 million while keeping a stake in the company. The sale was controversial with many of the ethically-minded customers the brand had built its identity around, since L'Oréal had a very different reputation on issues like animal testing. Roddick continued her activism and writing until her death in 2007.

She was blunt about how far the company had moved from her original vision, calling it "a dysfunctional coffin" that had sacrificed its values to market demands.Widely reported, including in her NZ Herald obituary (2007)

Compare the two founders

Both entrepreneurs show every characteristic on the spec, but their stories pull in different directions on change in role: Levi Roots' role changed but he chose to stay as the permanent face of the brand, because his personal story is the brand. Same spec point, two opposite endings — good material for a "to what extent" or evaluative answer.

1

A villa in Majorca and 6 million followers

2019–2020 ▸

Molly-Mae Hague entered the reality TV show Love Island in 2019 as a 19-year-old and finished runner-up. She didn't win the show's £50,000 prize, but she left with something arguably more valuable to a future business: a huge, engaged social media following, built almost entirely by being watched and talked about for weeks on end. Within a year she had millions of Instagram followers and a growing YouTube channel, and brands were paying to be associated with her rather than the other way round.

This is the starting point that makes influencer-led enterprise genuinely different from the other case studies here: her first real business asset wasn't a product, a recipe, or a shop — it was an audience that trusted her opinion and wanted to copy her choices.

2

From ambassador to Creative Director

2020–2021 ▸

Hague first worked with fast-fashion retailer PrettyLittleThing as a paid brand ambassador — fronting campaigns for a company she had no ownership or design input into. In August 2021 that changed: she was hired as the brand's creative director, in a deal reported to be worth seven figures. Rather than simply lending her name to a collection designed by others, she has said she was involved in every stage of the design process, and took a collection to London Fashion Week within months of starting.

This is a meaningfully different role from being an ambassador: she now had genuine creative input and public accountability for the brand's output, even though she still didn't own any part of the company itself.

Announcing the role on Instagram, she called it "the biggest move in my career so far," adding that PLT had "had faith in me from the VERY start."Molly-Mae Hague, Instagram announcement, August 2021 — reported by Capital FM and The Tab

3

When the personal brand becomes the problem

2021–2022 ▸

Being the human face of a brand cuts both ways. An Instagram post from October 2021 promoting a PrettyLittleThing dress was later found by the Advertising Standards Authority to have breached ad-disclosure rules, since it gave no indication it was a paid promotion — a compliance failure specific to influencer marketing that a traditional retailer simply wouldn't run into in the same way. Then in January 2022, comments Hague made on Steven Bartlett's "Diary of a CEO" podcast about hard work and privilege — including the line that everyone has "the same 24 hours in a day" — triggered a much larger backlash, with critics calling her out of touch and some calling for her to be removed from the creative director role entirely.

She didn't step down. She publicly defended her work ethic, pointing to long hours and months of preparation behind each collection, and continued in the role for two more years.

4

Stepping back, then building her own

2023–2024 ▸

In 2023, after becoming a mother, Hague stepped back from the PrettyLittleThing creative director role, saying she wanted time with her daughter that she wouldn't get back, and that the decision was made jointly with the company. Rather than stepping away from fashion altogether, she spent the next period working, largely out of public view, on a brand that would be entirely her own: Maebe, a womenswear label she said she'd been developing for several years.

Before Maebe's online store had even opened, the brand's Instagram account had reportedly gathered hundreds of thousands of followers within a single day of being revealed. That's a different kind of market research and pre-launch marketing to anything available to a founder without an existing audience — she could effectively test demand before spending heavily on stock or a public launch.

5

Owning the label — and owning the problems too

2024–today ▸

Maebe launched in September 2024, positioned as "accessible luxury" with most pieces priced under £100. Unlike the PrettyLittleThing role, this business is hers — which means the successes and the failures both land on her directly. When customers complained that a £140 blazer had bobbled after a single wear, a behind-the-scenes documentary series showed Hague visibly affected, attributing the fault to a factory that had changed the fabric without her approval. In a separate interview with Vogue, she said she was unable to answer detailed questions about how the clothes were actually made in the brand's factories in Turkey and China.

That gap — being the passionate, hands-on face of a brand while relying on manufacturers she doesn't personally oversee day to day — is a genuine tension for influencer-led businesses that scale quickly. It's a different flavour of "growing pains" to a traditional retailer's, but it's recognisably the same spec point: the entrepreneur's role has to change to cope with a supply chain and production process they didn't build themselves.

What's genuinely different about an influencer-led business?

Levi Roots and Anita Roddick both started with a product and had to build an audience for it. Molly-Mae Hague started with the audience and worked backwards to a product — which changes where the risks sit. Her biggest challenges aren't about getting a bank to say yes; they're about advertising regulation and keeping control of a supply chain she's several steps removed from, because her personal following is the business's main asset and its biggest liability at the same time. Good material for comparing "traditional" and "modern" routes into enterprise, and for questioning whether every characteristic on the spec applies in exactly the same way to each.

1

Five workers, a grandmother's plot of land, no jobs for miles

2004–2005 ▸

Bethlehem Tilahun Alemu grew up in Zenabwork, one of the most impoverished and marginalised communities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. After studying at Unity University and working in the leather and apparel sector to learn the industry, she noticed that her community was full of skilled artisans with no work: chronic unemployment, poverty and stigma meant many talented people couldn't get even simple jobs. Rather than move away or found a charity, she decided the answer was a genuine global business, run and owned locally, that could compete on quality rather than rely on sympathy or aid.

In early 2005, fresh out of college, she started soleRebels with five workers in a workshop on her grandmother's plot of land. Reported figures for the start-up funding vary — Wikipedia cites a bank loan of 580,000 Ethiopian Birr (around $33,000 at the time), while Alemu herself has described the early days more simply as self-financed with family support. Either way, it was a serious sum to risk in that setting, with no guarantee a shoe brand from a slum in Addis Ababa could ever reach international shelves.

She's explained the reasoning directly: many people in her community had real artisan talent but, because of "extreme poverty, stigma, and marginalization," couldn't even get simple jobs — and she wanted to change that through trade, not aid.Bethlehem Tilahun Alemu, quoted by Sustainable Brands / International Labour Organization

Reinventing a rebel's shoe

2004–2006 ▸

Rather than copy an existing Western shoe design, Alemu built the business around a traditional Ethiopian shoe called the "selate" or "barabasso" — a recycled car-tyre-soled sandal that, according to the brand's own account, was once worn by Ethiopian fighters who helped keep Ethiopia the only African nation never colonised. She combined this age-old recycling tradition with local artisan techniques — hand-spun cotton, hand-loomed fabric — and modern design sensibilities, deliberately aiming for a product that could compete internationally on style and quality, not just as a novelty.

She was explicit that this wasn't about selling a "worthy" product people bought out of sympathy: the goal from day one was footwear good enough that customers the world over would choose it on its own merits.

It's been widely reported that she designed soleRebels' products to compete as "the best of the best in the world," not wanting a single customer to buy "simply as an act of charity or sympathy."Reported by Asaase Radio, among other profiles

3

Convincing a market that doubted the label

2006–2010 ▸

Breaking into international retail meant fighting a widely held assumption: that products made in Africa were low quality or simply "not cool," regardless of how good they actually were. Alemu has spoken publicly about this perception being one of the biggest barriers facing African entrepreneurs trying to sell into Western markets, on top of the practical challenges of financing, logistics and export from a landlocked, developing economy. She's also been vocal about a second, related challenge: the lack of a strong, enforceable intellectual property system in much of Africa, which makes it harder for entrepreneurs to protect original designs as they grow.

Despite this, soleRebels eventually secured listings with major international retailers including Whole Foods, Urban Outfitters and Amazon — retailers whose standards gave the brand credibility it couldn't have argued its way into on reputation alone.

From one workshop to fifty countries

2010–2016 ▸

soleRebels grew from five workers to around 300 employees, and by the mid-2010s the company said it had become the world's first Fair Trade-certified footwear company (a claim tied to its certification from the World Fair Trade Organization). Its shoes reached more than 50 countries, with standalone stores opening from Addis Ababa to Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan and California. Alemu built early, direct e-commerce partnerships with major online retailers well before this was standard practice for a business based in a developing economy, effectively letting the brand sell everywhere at once rather than country by country.

None of this could run the way the original five-person workshop did. Managing a global retail footprint, export logistics, franchising and e-commerce meant Alemu's day-to-day work shifted from personally organising artisans to building distribution partnerships, managing an international brand, and speaking at global forums such as the World Economic Forum about African entrepreneurship.

5

From founder to figurehead for a continent's businesses

2015–today ▸

Alemu didn't stop at one brand. She went on to launch Garden of Coffee, an Ethiopian coffee brand, and Republic of Leather, a bespoke luxury leatherwear label — each one built on the same idea of turning local artisan skill into globally competitive brands. Alongside running these businesses, she has become a prominent public voice for African entrepreneurship, speaking at international conferences and forums about funding, intellectual property and what she calls prosperity built by local Africans rather than poverty alleviation "orchestrated by external actors."

Her role has moved a long way from hands-on workshop organiser: she now operates as the head of a small group of businesses and a public advocate, using her own company's success as evidence for a much bigger argument about how African economies can grow.

She's framed her wider goal as proving that Africa can prosper through trade and global brands built on local skill, rather than through aid or donor projects.Reflects statements reported across multiple profiles, including Asaase Radio and Knowledge at Wharton

Four founders, one spec

Across all four case studies, the same six characteristics and the same four spec points show up again and again — but the details of how vary hugely with context. Levi Roots and Anita Roddick both faced a British bank manager who wouldn't lend to them for reasons tangled up with who they were; Bethlehem Tilahun Alemu faced that same access-to-finance barrier but layered with the extra challenge of exporting from a developing economy and fighting assumptions about where her product was made. Molly-Mae Hague's biggest risk wasn't ever about getting a loan — it was about what happens when your own personal following is both your greatest asset and your biggest liability. Good exam technique: don't just spot the characteristic, ask what's specific about this founder's context that shaped how it showed up.

Your answer should include:

Explain one challenge that is particularly linked to businesses built around a social media following, using Molly-Mae Hague as your example.

Your answer should include:

Analyse

ANALYSE

Analyse how Bethlehem Tilahun Alemu's role changed as soleRebels grew from a five-person workshop into a global brand.

A strong answer builds a chain, e.g:

  • Start point: describe her hands-on role organising five artisans on her grandmother's land
  • First link: as the business scaled to around 300 employees and shoes reaching more than 50 countries, she couldn't personally oversee everything, so her role shifted towards building international retail and e-commerce partnerships
  • Second link: she went on to launch further businesses (Garden of Coffee, Republic of Leather), showing the role evolved again into leading a small group of companies rather than running one workshop
  • Conclusion: her role moved from local, hands-on operator to global brand-builder and industry advocate
ANALYSE

Analyse the impact of taking a significant financial risk on Levi Roots' business.

A strong answer builds a chain, e.g:

  • Identify the risk: going into the Dragons' Den pitch without knowing his figures cold, which meant giving up 40% equity rather than a smaller share
  • First consequence: this cost him a larger share of ownership, and long-term profit, than he might otherwise have given up
  • Second consequence: but the investment and Peter Jones's contacts gave him access to Sainsbury's and the capital to scale production — something he couldn't achieve alone after years of bank rejections
  • Conclusion: weigh whether the short-term cost (equity) was outweighed by the long-term gain (national retail access and rapid growth)
ANALYSE

Analyse why access to finance can be a bigger barrier for some entrepreneurs than others, using evidence from two of the case studies.

A strong answer compares across founders, e.g:

  • Levi Roots and Anita Roddick both faced reluctant UK bank managers, in ways they linked to how they were perceived rather than the strength of their idea
  • Bethlehem Tilahun Alemu faced an extra layer: financing an export business from a developing economy, plus a broader assumption that African-made products were lower quality
  • Mechanism: lenders and investors judge risk partly on perceived credibility, so unfamiliar founders or products can be seen as higher risk regardless of actual quality
  • Consequence: this can mean worse terms, more equity given up, or slower growth
  • Conclusion: a comparative judgement on which founder faced the bigger barrier, and why

Assess / Evaluate

ASSESS

"The most important characteristic for a successful entrepreneur is being a risk taker." Assess this statement, using evidence from at least two case studies.

A strong answer weighs both sides, e.g:

  • For: risk-taking evidence that paid off — Roots' pitch risk led to the Sainsbury's deal; Hague's risk leaving PLT led to full ownership of Maebe
  • Against: evidence that other characteristics mattered just as much — Roddick's resilience through repeated bank rejection; Alemu's focus on quality over sympathy sales
  • Context: consider whether different situations reward different characteristics more
  • Conclusion: a justified judgement — e.g. risk-taking may be necessary but rarely sufficient on its own; it tends to need pairing with resilience or focus to actually succeed
EVALUATE

To what extent does an entrepreneur's role always change in the same way as their business grows? Use evidence from at least two case studies to support your answer.

A strong answer weighs both sides, e.g:

  • One pattern: several founders shift from hands-on producer to strategist or figurehead while staying with the business — Roots remains the face of his brand; Alemu becomes a global advocate
  • A different pattern: Roddick's role changed all the way to leaving the business entirely, because she felt it had drifted from her founding values under commercial pressure
  • What causes the difference: whether the founder's personal identity stays tied to the brand, versus whether their values start to conflict with the commercial direction of the business
  • Conclusion: a justified judgement on how far "role change" follows one single, predictable pattern across different founders
