Understanding Dividends

What dividends are, why they matter to shareholders, why some PLCs choose not to pay them — and how to calculate dividend per share and dividend yield.

What is a dividend?

When a company makes a profit, its directors decide what to do with it. Some profit is usually kept in the business as retained profit — used to fund growth, pay off debt, or build up reserves. The rest can be paid out to shareholders as a dividend: a cash payment, made per share owned, as a reward for investing in the company.

A few things worth knowing

Dividends are payments to shareholders — so only companies with shares can pay them. That includes both private limited companies (Ltds) and public limited companies (PLCs); sole traders and partnerships don't have shareholders to pay. This resource focuses on PLCs specifically, since their shares are traded on a public stock market and so have a constantly changing, publicly known share price — which is what makes dividend yield a meaningful, calculable figure.

Dividends are usually paid twice a year: an "interim" dividend partway through the financial year, and a larger "final" dividend once annual profits are confirmed.

Dividends are not guaranteed. Unlike interest on a loan, a company has no legal obligation to pay a dividend, and the amount can rise, fall, or disappear entirely depending on how profitable the business is and what its directors decide.

Why dividends incentivise shareholders

Shareholders can make money from owning shares in two ways: through dividends (income, paid out regularly) and through capital growth (the share price itself rising, so the shares are worth more if sold). Dividends specifically appeal to shareholders because they offer:

A reliable, ongoing income

Unlike capital growth, which only becomes real money when shares are sold, a dividend is cash paid directly to the shareholder while they continue to hold the shares. This particularly appeals to investors who want a steady income stream — for example, pension funds, or individuals investing for retirement.

A signal of financial health

A company that can afford to pay — and especially one that can afford to increase — its dividend year after year is signalling to the market that it's generating strong, stable profit. This can boost investor confidence and make the shares more attractive, even to investors who don't especially want the income itself.

A reason to hold rather than sell

Regular dividend payments give shareholders a reason to keep holding shares through short-term price dips, rather than selling at the first sign of a falling share price — which can help keep a company's share price and ownership more stable.

Why some PLCs choose not to pay dividends

Not every successful PLC pays a dividend, and a lower (or zero) dividend doesn't automatically make a company a less attractive investment. It depends on what stage of growth the business is at, and what shareholders are actually looking for.

Growth-focused companies

Often young, fast-expanding businesses

  • Reinvest profit into expansion, research, or new markets instead of paying it out
  • Shareholders are betting on strong capital growth — a rising share price — rather than income
  • Can still attract huge demand for shares if investors believe future growth will be worth far more than a dividend today
  • Cutting or skipping dividends can free up cash for opportunities a competitor paying out profit might not be able to afford

Income-focused companies

Often larger, more established businesses

  • Operate in mature markets with fewer big growth opportunities left to reinvest in
  • Pay a stable or rising dividend to reward shareholders directly, since rapid share price growth is less likely
  • Attract investors who specifically want reliable income, such as pension and income funds
  • A dividend cut can badly damage investor confidence, since it's often read as a sign of financial trouble

So why would investors accept a low dividend yield?

A low dividend yield doesn't necessarily mean low demand for the shares. Investors may still want to buy in even with a small (or zero) dividend if:

• They expect strong future capital growth to more than make up for the lack of income now.

• The company has a strong brand, market position, or reputation that makes it a safer long-term bet.

• They're reinvesting for the long term and don't need income right now, so retained profit funding growth suits them just as well as a dividend would.

• A rising share price, driven by reinvested profit, can ultimately deliver a bigger overall return than dividends would have.

Calculating dividend per share (DPS)

Dividend per share tells you exactly how much cash a shareholder receives for each individual share they own.

Formula

Dividend per share = Total dividend paid ÷ Number of issued shares

"Issued shares" means the total number of ordinary shares the company has in circulation, not just the shares one investor owns.

Worked example

Riverstone plc pays a total annual dividend of £4,500,000 to its ordinary shareholders.

The company has 30,000,000 issued shares.

Dividend per share = £4,500,000 ÷ 30,000,000 = £0.15

Dividend per share = 15 pence (£0.15) per share

Calculating dividend yield

Dividend yield shows the dividend as a percentage of the current share price — it tells an investor what return they're getting on the money they'd need to invest today, based purely on dividend income.

Formula

Dividend yield (%) = (Dividend per share ÷ Share price) × 100

Make sure dividend per share and share price are in the same units (both in pence, or both in £) before dividing.

Worked example

Using Riverstone plc's dividend per share of 15p from the example above.

Riverstone plc's current share price is 250p (£2.50).

Dividend yield = (15 ÷ 250) × 100 = 6%

Dividend yield = 6%

Reading the direction of change

• If the share price rises but the dividend stays the same, dividend yield falls.

• If the dividend increases but the share price stays the same, dividend yield rises.

• A dividend yield can look artificially high simply because a company's share price has fallen — often a warning sign, not necessarily a bargain.

Practice questions

Work through each question, then check your answer against the working shown below it.

1

Hazelmere plc pays a total dividend of £2,000,000 and has 10,000,000 issued shares. Calculate the dividend per share (in pence).

Answer: DPS = £2,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = £0.20 = 20p

2

Corvina plc's dividend per share is 18p and its share price is 360p. Calculate the dividend yield (%).

Answer: Yield = (18 ÷ 360) × 100 = 5%

3

Wrenfield plc pays a total dividend of £900,000 to 6,000,000 issued shares. Calculate the dividend per share (in pence).

Answer: DPS = £900,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = £0.15 = 15p

4

Oakthorn plc's dividend per share is 8p and its share price is 800p. Calculate the dividend yield (%).

Answer: Yield = (8 ÷ 800) × 100 = 1%

5

Two-step question. Bramfield plc pays a total dividend of £1,200,000 across 4,000,000 issued shares. Its share price is 150p. First calculate the dividend per share (in pence), then use it to calculate the dividend yield (%).

Answer: DPS = £1,200,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = £0.30 = 30p. Yield = (30 ÷ 150) × 100 = 20%

6

Elmcourt plc's share price rises from 200p to 400p, but its dividend per share stays at 10p. What happens to the dividend yield? Give the new yield (%).

Answer: New yield = (10 ÷ 400) × 100 = 2.5% — down from 5% before the price rise. The yield falls even though the dividend hasn't changed, because the share price has gone up.
