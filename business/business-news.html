18 September 2026 Big Story — Cost of Finance

BoE holds at 3.75% as gilt yields hit a 28-year high — the cost of business finance keeps climbing

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% for a second consecutive meeting on 18 September, splitting 6-3, even after inflation rose to 3.1% — its highest since February 2024. At almost the same time, 30-year gilt yields pushed above 5.9%, their highest level since the late 1990s, as investors demanded a bigger premium to hold long-dated UK debt ahead of Chancellor Healey's November Budget. Because gilt yields act as the benchmark against which corporate borrowing is priced, the combination of a held policy rate and rising long-term yields means the cost of both short- and long-term business finance is now moving in the wrong direction for firms planning to invest.

Exam link Investment appraisal and the cost of finance — a rise in the benchmark gilt yield typically raises the discount rate firms should use in net present value calculations, making some previously-viable investment projects marginal or unviable. Link to the 6-3 MPC vote from a "should we raise rates" perspective too: a split committee signals genuine uncertainty about the future path of rates, making it harder for finance directors to plan with confidence. Evaluate: should firms lock in fixed-rate borrowing now, betting yields stay elevated or rise further, or wait in the hope rates ease once inflation is brought back under control — and what does each choice do to a firm's financial risk profile?
Why does a rise in gilt yields raise the cost of finance even for a firm that has never borrowed from the government? Because gilt yields act as the "risk-free" benchmark that lenders price corporate debt above. When the government's own borrowing costs rise, banks and bond markets typically demand a higher return on business lending too — so the cost of a business loan or corporate bond tends to rise alongside gilt yields, even though the borrower has no direct link to government debt.
September 2026 Strategy & Market Conditions

Next reports resilient summer trading as shoppers keep spending selectively

Retailer Next reported like-for-like sales growth of around 3.2% for the summer trading period, ahead of analyst expectations, as shoppers continued to spend on clothing and homeware despite wider cost-of-living pressure. Management pointed to disciplined stock management, strong online growth and a loyal customer base as key drivers, while cautioning that the broader consumer backdrop — weakening confidence, rising mortgage costs, a softer labour market — remained challenging heading into the autumn.

Exam link Competitive strategy in a squeezed market — Next's resilience is a useful counter-example to the "consumers are cutting back everywhere" narrative, showing that strong brand positioning, inventory discipline and a well-developed online channel can help a firm outperform the wider sector even in a difficult external environment. Link to Porter's generic strategies: Next's combination of quality, convenience (online plus stores) and brand trust supports a differentiation strategy rather than competing purely on price. Evaluate: how much of Next's outperformance reflects genuinely superior strategy versus simply serving a more resilient, higher-income customer base than discount or value retailers?
Why can one retailer report resilient trading while the wider sector talks about a consumer squeeze? Because aggregate figures like consumer confidence or retail sales hide a lot of variation between individual firms. A retailer with strong brand loyalty, disciplined stock control and a wealthier customer base can outperform the average even while overall consumer spending is weak — the sector-wide squeeze doesn't hit every firm equally.
September 2026 M&A and Competition Policy

CMA moves the McCormick/Unilever foods deal to a full Phase 2 investigation

After extending its initial review deadline earlier in the month, the Competition and Markets Authority referred McCormick & Company's proposed acquisition of Unilever's food business for a full, in-depth Phase 2 investigation, concluding that the deal raises competition concerns serious enough to require deeper scrutiny. The move means the deal now faces a longer statutory timeline before any final decision, with the CMA required to examine market shares, retailer bargaining power and the likely impact on consumers in the affected packaged-food categories in much greater detail.

Exam link Regulatory risk in M&A strategy — escalation to Phase 2 is a significant strategic setback for the merging firms: it delays any synergies, increases advisory and compliance costs, and creates a real (if still uncertain) risk the deal could ultimately be blocked or require remedies such as divestment of specific brands. Link to Ansoff's Matrix: this continues the market development story from July/August, showing that inorganic growth strategies carry regulatory execution risk that organic growth does not. Evaluate: should firms build the possibility of a Phase 2 referral into their original deal valuation and timeline, given how common escalation has become in concentrated consumer-goods markets?
Why is being referred to a Phase 2 investigation worse for a firm than simply waiting longer for a Phase 1 decision? Because Phase 2 isn't just a longer wait — it signals the regulator has genuine competition concerns serious enough to need deep scrutiny, which increases the real risk the deal is blocked or has to be restructured (for example, through forced divestment of certain brands), on top of the extra time and advisory cost involved.

September 2026 Employment Rights Act 2025

The October compliance wave approaches: harassment duty and extended tribunal limits both go live within weeks

With October fast approaching, HR and compliance teams spent September finalising preparations for two Employment Rights Act deadlines landing in quick succession: extended employment tribunal time limits from 1 October, and the new employer duty to take "all reasonable steps" to prevent sexual harassment — including from third parties such as customers — from 30 October. Employment lawyers reported a surge in demand for policy reviews and staff training in the final weeks of September, as firms without existing anti-harassment procedures scrambled to put something in place before the duty bites.

Exam link HR planning and legal compliance — a "reasonable steps" duty is deliberately open-ended, meaning firms cannot simply tick a box; they must demonstrate genuine risk assessment, policy and training appropriate to their own workplace, which is a bigger undertaking for large customer-facing employers (retail, hospitality) than for small office-based firms. Link to organisational culture: a compliance-driven policy rollout is less effective than one genuinely embedded in workplace culture and leadership behaviour. Evaluate: is a legal deadline like this best treated as a compliance cost to minimise, or an opportunity to genuinely improve workplace culture and reduce staff turnover linked to harassment and poor conduct?
Why might a "reasonable steps" legal duty actually be harder for a business to comply with than a duty with a precise checklist? Because a precise checklist tells a firm exactly what to do, while a "reasonable steps" duty requires judgement about what's proportionate for that specific workplace — a large customer-facing retailer and a small office-based firm face very different expectations, so businesses can't just copy a generic template and assume they're compliant.
September 2026 Recruitment & Workforce Planning

UK job vacancies keep falling as hiring intentions weaken further

Vacancy data published in September showed job openings falling for a further consecutive quarter, continuing a trend that has now run for well over a year, as employers facing higher borrowing costs, a softer consumer backdrop and rising employment costs turned more cautious about hiring. Surveys of employer hiring intentions pointed to a similar picture, with a growing share of firms planning to hold headcount flat or reduce it over the coming quarter rather than expand.

Exam link Workforce planning under uncertainty — falling vacancies alongside weak hiring intentions is a leading indicator that firms are moving from growth-mode to cost-control mode, often before any formal redundancy announcements appear in the headline unemployment figures. Link to human resource flow models: a hiring freeze is a "soft" workforce reduction tool, avoiding the direct cost and reputational risk of redundancy while still reducing headcount growth. Evaluate: is a prolonged hiring freeze a prudent response to genuine economic uncertainty, or does it risk leaving a firm under-resourced and unable to respond quickly once demand recovers?
Why might falling vacancies be a more useful early-warning signal than the unemployment rate itself? Because unemployment is a lagging indicator — it only rises after firms have actually made people redundant. Vacancies falling shows firms pulling back on hiring plans before any redundancies happen, so it tends to signal a cooling labour market several months before it shows up in the headline unemployment figures.
Late September 2026 Costs & Pricing

Consumer confidence slides as the BRC warns rising business rates threaten the high street

The GfK consumer confidence index fell to its weakest reading since the spring in September, as rising inflation and higher mortgage costs weighed on household sentiment. The British Retail Consortium separately warned that upcoming business rates increases — layered on top of already-elevated employment costs from successive National Living Wage and employer National Insurance rises — risked accelerating store closures on the high street, particularly among smaller independent retailers with thinner margins than larger chains.

Exam link Costs, pricing and the external environment — business rates are a fixed cost tied to property value rather than sales performance, meaning a rates rise hits struggling retailers hardest precisely when they can least afford it, unlike a variable cost that scales with revenue. Link to the consumption function: weaker consumer confidence typically precedes a fall in discretionary spending, squeezing retailer revenue from one direction while rising costs squeeze it from the other. Evaluate: should the government reform business rates to better reflect a retailer's ability to pay, or would this simply shift the tax burden elsewhere, given the revenue local authorities depend on rates to raise?
Why does a rise in business rates hit smaller independent retailers harder than large chains, even at the same percentage increase? Because business rates are a fixed cost tied to a property's rateable value, not to how much the business actually sells. A large chain can spread rates costs across many stores and higher overall revenue, while a small independent retailer with thinner margins and a single site has much less room to absorb the same fixed cost increase.

Late August–September 2026 Supply Chain & Risk

US-Canada trade war disrupts UK supply chains reliant on North American components

The tariff dispute between the US and Canada that erupted in late August showed no sign of resolution through September, with retaliatory tariffs still in place on both sides. UK manufacturers and distributors with supply chains running through North America — particularly in sectors sourcing steel, aluminium and specialist components — reported longer lead times and rising costs as firms on both sides of the dispute rerouted sourcing to avoid the tariffs, adding friction to supply chains that had previously operated with minimal trade barriers.

Exam link Supply chain resilience and lean production — firms operating just-in-time supply chains built around efficient, low-friction North American sourcing are now exposed to exactly the kind of disruption JIT is vulnerable to, echoing the earlier Middle East conflict disruption covered in your spring editions. Link to global sourcing decisions: a trade dispute thousands of miles away can still raise costs for a UK firm several steps removed from the original tariff, illustrating how interconnected modern supply chains are. Evaluate: should firms build permanent supply chain redundancy (dual sourcing, higher buffer stock) given how frequently these external shocks now recur, and how should that cost be weighed against the efficiency lost?
Why can a trade dispute between two other countries still raise costs for a UK firm that doesn't trade directly with either of them? Because modern supply chains are highly interconnected — a UK firm may buy components from a supplier that itself sources materials from the US or Canada. When tariffs disrupt that upstream link, the extra cost and delay can still work its way through to the UK firm, even though it has no direct relationship with either country in the dispute.
September 2026 Strategy & Restructuring

Harvey Nichols confirms further store closures as luxury retail struggles with the squeeze

Department store group Harvey Nichols confirmed further store closures in September, continuing a period of restructuring as high-end retail faces its own version of the consumer squeeze: wealthier shoppers pulling back on discretionary luxury spending amid a weaker economic backdrop, alongside structurally higher costs from business rates, rents and staffing. The closures follow a broader trend among department stores and premium retailers rethinking their physical footprint in favour of a smaller number of flagship locations and stronger online offerings.

Exam link Strategic retrenchment and the product life cycle — closing underperforming stores while investing in flagship locations and online is a retrenchment strategy, prioritising profitability over scale in a mature, contracting market segment. Link to Ansoff's Matrix: this is market consolidation rather than growth, a rational response when the addressable market itself is shrinking rather than a sign of poor management alone. Evaluate: is store closure the right response to a cyclical consumer squeeze that may ease once interest rates and inflation fall, or does it represent a necessary structural adjustment to a permanently changed retail landscape?
Why might closing stores actually be a sign of good strategic management rather than business failure? Because if the underlying market for a firm's product is genuinely shrinking (as with premium high-street footfall), continuing to run unprofitable stores just to maintain scale destroys value. Deliberately retrenching to a smaller number of profitable locations, while investing in channels that are still growing, can be a rational, value-preserving strategy rather than a sign of failure.
September 2026 Costs & Supply

Oil prices resurge on Middle East supply fears, reviving cost pressure for logistics-heavy firms

Brent crude climbed back above $80 a barrel in September after months of relative calm, as renewed fears over Middle East supply disruption pushed traders to price in a bigger risk premium. For businesses with fuel-intensive operations — logistics, distribution, manufacturing with energy-heavy processes — the move revived cost pressures that many had assumed were fading, complicating budgeting just as firms finalise plans for the final quarter of the year.

Exam link Costs and operational planning — a renewed commodity price shock is a good example of an external cost businesses cannot control but must plan around, typically through fuel hedging, surcharge clauses in customer contracts, or route and process efficiency improvements. Link back to this edition's cost-of-finance story: firms already facing higher borrowing costs now face higher input costs too, a double squeeze on margins from two different directions at once. Evaluate: should logistics-heavy firms prioritise passing rising fuel costs on to customers through surcharges, or absorb them to protect customer relationships and market share, accepting a temporary margin hit?
Why do rising fuel costs create a genuine strategic dilemma rather than a simple decision to just raise prices? Because passing the full cost increase on to customers through higher prices risks losing price-sensitive customers to competitors (depending on price elasticity of demand), while absorbing the cost protects market share but directly reduces profit margin. There's no automatically correct answer — it depends on how sensitive that firm's specific customers are to price changes.

20–21 August 2026 Big Story — Fiscal Policy & Cost of Finance

Banks warn Healey against a windfall tax as Budget speculation mounts

UK banking groups lobbied Chancellor Healey directly in the week of 20 August, warning that a windfall tax on lender profits — one option reportedly under consideration to help plug the surprise £1.8bn deficit revealed earlier that week — would raise the cost and reduce the availability of business lending just as firms already face a "hawkish hold" from the Bank of England. The intervention came as speculation intensified over how Healey will close the gap between current spending plans and the fiscal rules ahead of his 28 October Budget, with tax rises, spending cuts or some combination all still on the table.

Exam link Government intervention and the cost of finance — a sector-specific windfall tax is a form of government intervention aimed at raising revenue from firms seen as making "excess" profits, but banks' argument is a classic incidence-of-tax point: a tax on lenders may simply be passed through as higher borrowing costs or reduced lending to businesses and households, rather than being absorbed by shareholders. Link to stakeholder theory: government (revenue), banks (profitability) and business borrowers (cost of finance) all have competing interests in the same policy decision. Evaluate: is taxing bank profits a fair way to fund the deficit, or does it ultimately raise costs for the wider business community that relies on bank lending to invest?

Vistry warns on profits as the housing market cools — a strategic reset for the housebuilder

Housebuilder Vistry issued a fresh profit warning in mid-August, citing softer build-to-rent demand and slower private sales as elevated mortgage rates and cautious consumer confidence weighed on the housing market. The warning follows a difficult 18 months for the sector, and comes just as rising gilt yields threaten to keep mortgage rates higher for longer, further dampening demand for new-build homes. Vistry's leadership said it was reviewing its build rate and land-buying strategy in response.

Exam link Strategic response to a changing external environment — a profit warning signals that a firm's previous strategy (build rate, land bank, sales assumptions) no longer matches current market conditions, forcing a strategic reset. Link to the wider cost-of-finance thread running through this edition: higher gilt yields feed through to mortgage rates, directly reducing demand for a housebuilder's product — a clear example of how macroeconomic conditions shape strategic options in a specific industry. Evaluate: should a housebuilder slow build rates to match weaker demand (protecting margins) or continue building to maintain market share and economies of scale, betting on a market recovery?

easyJet joins the FTSE 100 as a summer travel rebound reshapes the index

Evaluate: is rising up an index a genuine sign of underlying business strength (higher revenue, improving margins), or can it partly reflect short-term sentiment and sector rotation that may not persist?

28 July–18 August 2026 Health, Safety & Productivity

England's driest July on record puts workplace heat and productivity on the agenda

England recorded its driest July since records began, with three successive heatwaves pushing temperatures well above seasonal norms through late July and into August. For employers, the extended heat raised practical workforce questions: the UK has no legal maximum workplace temperature, but the Health and Safety Executive expects "reasonable" steps to keep employees safe and comfortable, and several trade unions renewed calls for a statutory maximum. Sectors with significant outdoor or physically demanding work — construction, logistics, warehousing — reported productivity dips and higher rates of heat-related absence.

Exam link Health and safety, motivation and productivity — employers must weigh the cost of measures like flexible hours, additional breaks or cooling equipment against the productivity loss and legal/reputational risk of doing nothing, even without a specific statutory temperature limit. Link to Maslow's hierarchy of needs: physiological comfort at work is a basic need that, if unmet, undermines motivation and performance regardless of other reward strategies in place. Evaluate: should the government introduce a statutory maximum workplace temperature, giving businesses certainty but less flexibility, or is a "reasonable steps" duty better suited to very different workplaces and job types?
16 July 2026 Employment Rights Act 2025

Government pushes back the harassment-prevention duty — but the October compliance wave still looms

The government's updated implementation timeline, published 16 July, pushed back the new duty on employers to take "all reasonable steps" to prevent sexual harassment from 1 to 30 October 2026, while confirming that extended employment tribunal time limits still take effect from 1 October as planned. In a further twist, the government quietly withdrew its draft Tipping Code of Practice without explanation, leaving hospitality employers uncertain what tipping rules they will actually need to follow when the underlying tipping fairness legislation remains in force. For HR and compliance teams, this is now a genuinely staggered set of deadlines to track through the autumn rather than a single "go live" date.

Exam link Legal environment and HR compliance planning — staggered implementation dates increase administrative complexity for HR functions, who must track multiple go-live dates rather than a single compliance deadline. Link to PESTLE analysis: legal change is rarely a single event but an unfolding process, and firms that build flexible compliance roadmaps cope better than those planning around one fixed date. Evaluate: does phased implementation give businesses welcome extra preparation time, or does the repeated shifting of deadlines (as with the withdrawn Tipping Code) itself become a cost, through wasted planning and uncertainty?
20 August 2026 Workforce & Restructuring

UK business distress rises 9%, hitting consumer-facing sectors hardest

Data published on 20 August showed the number of UK businesses in "significant" financial distress rose 9% quarter-on-quarter, with retail, hospitality and consumer-facing services seeing the steepest increases as households pulled back on discretionary spending. Insolvency practitioners linked the rise to a combination of elevated borrowing costs, softer consumer confidence earlier in the summer, and rising employment costs following successive increases to the National Living Wage and employer National Insurance contributions.

Exam link Workforce planning under financial pressure — rising business distress typically precedes redundancy programmes, hiring freezes and restructuring, making this a leading indicator worth tracking alongside labour market data. Link to Herzberg and job security as a hygiene factor: employees in visibly distressed sectors may experience lower motivation and higher voluntary turnover even before any redundancies are announced, simply from perceived insecurity. Evaluate: which cost-cutting lever should distressed consumer-facing firms prioritise first — headcount, discretionary spend, or pricing — and what are the risks of each to long-term competitiveness?

Ofgem confirmed on 26 August that the household energy price cap will rise by around 4% from October, though a planned VAT cut on domestic energy is expected to soften the impact for consumers. Exam link Costs, pricing strategy and competitiveness — rising energy costs squeeze gross margins unless firms can pass costs on through higher prices, which risks reducing demand depending on price elasticity. Evaluate: should energy-intensive firms prioritise fixed-price contracts to manage cost certainty, or floating tariffs that could fall if wholesale prices ease — and how does that choice affect their ability to plan and price confidently?

20–21 July 2026 Big Story — Political Environment & Strategy

Burnham becomes PM, sacks the chancellor — and gilt markets react within hours

Keir Starmer resigned as Prime Minister on 22 June after losing the confidence of Labour MPs; Andy Burnham, the former Greater Manchester mayor, was confirmed unopposed as leader on 17 July and became Britain's seventh PM in a decade on 20 July. Within hours of taking office he replaced chancellor Rachel Reeves with John Healey and told reporters he would stick to the existing fiscal rules but use "any flexibility" within them — a remark that immediately unsettled bond markets, sending the 10-year gilt yield up 8 basis points to 5.03% and the 30-year yield to 5.75%, its highest in two months, while sterling dipped. Yields eased slightly once Healey — seen as a more cautious figure — was confirmed as chancellor. For businesses, a change of prime minister and chancellor mid-year introduces genuine strategic uncertainty just months ahead of the Autumn Budget, with borrowing costs, tax policy and spending commitments all potentially in play.

Exam link External environment (PESTLE) — a change of government and finance minister is a political risk factor that directly affects the economic environment businesses plan in: borrowing costs, business rates, employer taxes and regulation can all shift. Link to the cost of finance: rising gilt yields feed through to corporate borrowing costs, since government bond yields act as a benchmark for the wider cost of debt across the economy. Evaluate: a single ambiguous phrase from a new PM moving markets shows how much weight investors place on perceived fiscal credibility rather than any specific policy detail — useful for discussing business confidence and investment appraisal under political uncertainty. Good material for a case study on how firms build scenario planning around a change of political leadership they cannot control.

McCormick's bid for Unilever's foods business — CMA opens its review

The Competition and Markets Authority opened an initial review on 21 July into McCormick & Company's proposed acquisition of Unilever's food business, one of the largest recent deals in the global food industry, inviting comments from interested parties until 5 August before deciding whether to launch a formal Phase 1 investigation. Combining McCormick's flavourings and seasonings portfolio with Unilever's food brands would create a business with significant scale and bargaining power over retailers — but also significant market concentration in some categories, which is exactly what the CMA's review process exists to test.

Exam link Strategic growth through acquisition — a horizontal merger combining two branded-goods portfolios, pursued for economies of scale, cross-selling and stronger bargaining power against powerful retail buyers (a form of countervailing power, per Porter's Five Forces). Link to Ansoff's Matrix: this is market development and diversification through acquisition rather than organic growth. Evaluate the trade-off regulators face — permitting the deal may let both firms compete more effectively at scale, but could reduce choice and raise prices for retailers and, ultimately, consumers, if it substantially lessens competition in any single product category.

£2bn broadband merger fast-tracked straight to a Phase 2 competition probe

Rather than risk delay from a staged review process, the parties behind a £2bn tie-up between full-fibre broadband providers nexfibre and Netomnia requested — and were granted — a fast-track reference straight into an in-depth Phase 2 CMA investigation, due to conclude by 15 December 2026. A rival network operator's chief executive argued the deal would remove a successful challenger and reduce consumer choice in a market where several firms have been racing to build competing full-fibre networks street by street across the UK.

Exam link Strategic decision-making under regulatory risk — choosing to fast-track straight to the toughest level of scrutiny is itself a strategic move, trading a longer, more certain timeline for the (small) chance of avoiding an unnecessary Phase 1 stage. Link to industry life cycle and consolidation: full-fibre broadband has seen multiple firms build competing infrastructure simultaneously, a capital-intensive land grab that was always likely to end in consolidation once the market matured. People & Employment Law
1 July 2026 Employment Rights Act 2025

Unfair dismissal protection shortens to six months for staff hired from 1 July

From 1 July 2026, any employee hired from that date onward will qualify for unfair dismissal protection after just six months' continuous service, rather than the current two-year qualifying period — part of the Employment Rights Act 2025's phased rollout, though the change in practice only bites once dismissals occur on or after 1 January 2027. The cap on unfair dismissal compensation will also be removed for employees engaged from this date. Separately, the government's updated implementation timeline (published 16 July) pushed back the new duty on employers to take "all reasonable steps" to prevent sexual harassment from 1 to 30 October 2026, while confirming that extended employment tribunal time limits still take effect from 1 October as planned. In a further twist, the government has quietly withdrawn its draft Tipping Code of Practice without explanation, just months before its planned 1 October start — leaving hospitality employers uncertain what tipping rules they will actually need to follow.

Exam link Recruitment, selection and employment law — a shortened qualifying period raises the legal risk and cost of getting recruitment and probation decisions wrong, since new hires can bring an unfair dismissal claim much sooner. Link to HR planning: firms will need more robust induction, appraisal and probation review processes to manage this risk within the first six months rather than two years. Evaluate: does reducing the qualifying period genuinely protect vulnerable new employees, or does it risk making firms more cautious about hiring at all, particularly SMEs with less HR capacity to manage compliance? The withdrawn Tipping Code is a good example of policy uncertainty itself being a cost to business — firms cannot finalise tipping policies without knowing what the rules will require.
21 July 2026 Recruitment & Workforce Planning

Youth unemployment hits 16.4% — an 11-year high — as real wages keep falling

The ONS's July labour market release showed headline UK unemployment easing slightly to 4.9%, but youth (16-24) unemployment climbing further to 16.4% — its highest level in 11 years, meaning roughly one in six young people looking for work cannot find it. Job vacancies fell again, down 7,000 in the latest quarter to 712,000, below pre-pandemic levels.
