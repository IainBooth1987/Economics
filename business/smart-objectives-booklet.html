0

Tell me why

3 min

Answer each question in your head or in one sentence, then reveal the answer to check.

1. Why might a business with no clear objectives struggle to coordinate its departments?
Without a shared target, each department pursues its own priorities. Marketing might chase sales volume while finance cuts costs, so they pull in different directions. Objectives give every department the same end point, so their decisions support each other.
2. Why do banks and investors care whether a firm has clear objectives?
Objectives show where the business is heading and give outsiders a way to judge progress. A lender can check whether the firm is hitting its targets before trusting it with more money, which reduces the perceived risk.
3. Why is "to make a profit" a weak objective?
It gives no amount, no deadline and no owner, so nobody can tell whether it has been achieved or who should act. £1 of profit would technically count as success. It gives no direction for decisions.

Key idea: what makes an objective SMART?

S
Specific
States exactly what is to be achieved, with no vague words like "improve" or "better".
M
Measurable
Includes a number or clear measure, ideally a start point and a target.
A
Accountable
A named person or function is responsible for delivering it.
R
Realistic
It can be achieved with the resources and market conditions the firm faces.
T
Time specific
It has a clear deadline or time period.
Watch out: many websites and GCSE textbooks say A = "Achievable". Your specification says A = Accountable. Use Accountable in the exam. The "can it be done?" idea belongs under Realistic.

Who owns it?

5 min

Kiln & Co, a homeware retailer, has set a corporate objective: increase operating profit by 15% by March 2028. The board has broken this into the functional objectives below. Choose which director should be accountable for each one.

Tell me why: how does giving each objective a named owner make the corporate objective more likely to be met?
Each director knows exactly which part of the 15% profit target they are responsible for, so effort is not duplicated and nothing falls between departments. Performance can be reviewed person by person, so if one function falls behind the board knows who to support or challenge. Being held responsible also tends to motivate managers to prioritise the target.

Apply

10 min

Read the case study and data, then write three SMART objectives for Loaf & Ladle. Each must be owned by a different manager and tackle a problem shown in the data.

Case study: Loaf & Ladle

Loaf & Ladle is a family-owned chain of seven bakery-cafés across West Yorkshire, run by founder Priya Shah. Every site bakes on the premises each morning, and the business has built a loyal following for its soups and sourdough.

Mission: "Proper food, baked this morning, served by people who know your name."

Revenue has grown slowly, but larger rivals in the region have been growing at around 6% a year. Priya has noticed more unsold bread going in the bin at the end of the day, and store managers complain that they are always training new staff. Online orders through the firm's app remain a small share of sales.

Priya wants to open an eighth café in Harrogate within two years. It will cost about £400,000, and the bank has told her it wants to see evidence of improving profitability before it agrees a loan.

Reveal model objectives (only after writing all three of yours)
Operations manager: reduce food waste from 14% to 9% of baked goods across all seven sites by 31 August 2027, reviewed monthly by site.

HR manager: reduce staff turnover from 48% to 35% by 30 September 2027.

Marketing manager: increase app/online orders from 4% to 10% of revenue by 31 December 2027.

Finance manager: raise operating profit margin from 5.0% to 7.5% by the end of the 2027/28 financial year.

Notice that each one starts from a figure in the data, so it is Measurable against a baseline, and none tries to fix everything at once, so each stays Realistic.

Reveal the Level 3 model answer
Setting SMART objectives would help Loaf & Ladle reverse its falling profitability, because it turns a vague aim like "do better" into a precise target that a named manager must act on. Food waste has risen from 9% to 14% of baked goods in two years. If the operations manager is made accountable for cutting waste back to 9% by August 2027, bakers at all seven sites know exactly what to aim for and progress can be checked every month, which means that overproduction at a particular site is spotted early instead of at the year end. As a result, fewer ingredients are thrown away, so cost of sales falls without Priya having to raise prices in a market where rivals are growing faster. Consequently the operating margin, which has slid from 8% to 5%, should begin to recover. This matters for Loaf & Ladle in particular because the bank wants evidence of improving profitability before lending the £400,000 for Harrogate, and measurable, time-bound targets that are being hit provide exactly that evidence.

Highlighted = connectives driving the chain. Underlined = application to the case.

Teacher note. Common Level 2 answers list several benefits (focus, motivation, measurement) without developing any, or quote SMART letters back as definitions. Push students to follow one benefit through to margin and the bank loan. Other valid chains include staff turnover → accountable HR target → training costs and service quality → reviews → revenue, and coordination across seven sites.

Stretch: are SMART objectives always valuable?

If you finish early, write three or four sentences arguing the other side. Think about the mission statement.

Reveal possible limitations
Unintended consequences: a manager chasing the waste target could simply bake less, leaving empty shelves by lunchtime and losing sales.
Hard to measure what matters: "served by people who know your name" is central to the mission but hard to turn into a number, so it may get ignored.
Rigidity: if flour or energy prices jump, a fixed margin target may become unrealistic, and sticking to it could push managers into short-term cuts.
Monitoring cost: a small family firm may lack the systems and management time to track several targets across seven sites.
Motivation can backfire: targets seen as unrealistic, such as halving staff turnover in a tight labour market, can demotivate the managers held accountable.
