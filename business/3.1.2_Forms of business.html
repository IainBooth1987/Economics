Different Forms of Business Ownership

1. Sole Trader

A sole trader is a business owned and operated by one individual. It is the simplest and most common form of business ownership in the UK. The owner has complete control over all business decisions and keeps all profits after tax.

Key Characteristics:

  • Easy and inexpensive to set up
  • Owner has full control and decision-making authority
  • All profits belong to the owner
  • Unlimited liability - owner is personally responsible for all business debts
  • Owner pays income tax on business profits
  • Limited ability to raise capital
UK Examples:
  • Local plumbers, electricians, and builders - Many tradespeople operate as sole traders
  • Independent hairdressers and beauticians - Often start as sole traders before expanding
  • Freelance consultants and graphic designers - Common in the creative and professional services sectors
  • Mobile food vendors and market stall holders - Street food entrepreneurs often begin as sole traders

2. Partnerships and Limited Liability Partnerships (LLPs)

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people (usually 2–20) who share the risks, decision-making and profits. Partners normally sign a deed of partnership setting out how much capital each puts in, how profits are split and what happens if a partner leaves.

Key Characteristics:

  • More capital and skills than a sole trader, because each partner contributes
  • Workload and decision-making are shared
  • An ordinary partnership has unlimited liability: each partner can be personally liable for the whole firm's debts, including those caused by another partner
  • Disagreements between partners can slow decisions or break up the business
  • Not a separate legal entity; profits are taxed as each partner's income

A Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) keeps the partnership structure but gives partners limited liability, and must file accounts at Companies House. LLPs are common among professional firms such as accountants, solicitors and architects.

UK Examples:
  • Local GP surgeries, dental and veterinary practices - Often run as partnerships of qualified professionals
  • PwC, Deloitte, EY and KPMG - The "Big Four" accountancy firms operate in the UK as LLPs
  • Law firms - Most large UK law firms are LLPs, with senior lawyers becoming partners

3. Private Limited Company (Ltd)

A private limited company is a business structure where ownership is divided into shares. The company is a separate legal entity from its owners, providing limited liability protection. Shares cannot be sold to the general public and are often held by family members or a small group of investors.

Key Characteristics:

  • Separate legal entity from its owners
  • Limited liability protection for shareholders
  • Shares cannot be publicly traded
  • Must file accounts with Companies House
  • Pays corporation tax on profits
  • More complex and expensive to set up than sole trader
  • Easier to raise capital than sole traders
UK Examples:
  • Dyson Ltd - The technology company founded by Sir James Dyson remains privately owned, so it does not have to answer to outside shareholders
  • JCB - The construction-equipment maker is privately owned by the Bamford family
  • Specsavers Optical Group Ltd - The opticians chain is a private limited company
  • Aldi Stores Ltd - The UK subsidiary of the German supermarket chain
  • Virgin Atlantic Ltd - Part of the Virgin Group, structured as a private limited company

4. Public Limited Company (plc)

A public limited company has shares that are traded on a stock exchange and can be bought by the general public. This is the largest form of business organisation in terms of capital and typically involves significant regulatory requirements.

Key Characteristics:

  • Shares traded on stock exchanges (e.g., London Stock Exchange)
  • Can raise substantial capital from public investors
  • Must have minimum share capital of £50,000
  • Subject to strict regulatory requirements
  • Limited liability for shareholders
  • Separate legal entity
  • Must publish detailed financial information
  • Risk of hostile takeovers
UK Examples:
  • Tesco plc - One of the UK's largest supermarket chains, listed on the London Stock Exchange
  • BP plc - British multinational oil and gas company
  • Marks & Spencer Group plc - Major British retailer
  • Rolls-Royce Holdings plc - Engineering company specialising in power systems
  • Vodafone Group plc - Telecommunications company
  • Unilever plc - Consumer goods company (Dove, Hellmann's) listed on the London Stock Exchange

Watch out: not every plc is listed on the stock exchange. John Lewis Partnership plc is a public limited company, but its shares are held in trust for its employees ("Partners") and are not traded. This is why it is often described as employee-owned.

5. Co-operatives

Co-operatives are businesses owned and democratically controlled by their members, who share the profits. There are different types of co-operatives depending on who the members are.

Worker Co-operative

Owned and controlled by the employees who work in the business. Each worker has an equal say in decision-making, typically following the principle of "one member, one vote".

Key Characteristics:

  • Democratic control by employees
  • Profits shared among worker-members
  • Focus on job security and working conditions
  • Members have voting rights regardless of capital contribution
UK Examples:
  • Suma Wholefoods - Worker co-operative and the UK's largest independent wholefood distributor
  • Unicorn Grocery - Worker co-operative supermarket in Manchester
  • Equal Exchange Trading - Fair trade worker co-operative

Consumer Co-operative

Owned by customers who use the services of the business. Members benefit from discounts, dividends, and a say in how the business operates.

Key Characteristics:

  • Owned by customers/consumers
  • Profits returned to members as dividends or lower prices
  • Democratic decision-making
  • Focus on meeting member needs rather than maximising profit
UK Examples:
  • The Co-operative Group - Major consumer co-operative operating supermarkets, funeral services, and insurance
  • Midcounties Co-operative - Large regional consumer co-operative with retail and travel services
  • Central England Co-operative - Trading society operating food stores and funeral homes

Producer Co-operative

Owned by producers (often farmers or craftspeople) who join together to process and market their products collectively. This gives them greater bargaining power and access to markets.

Key Characteristics:

  • Owned by producers/suppliers
  • Collective bargaining power
  • Shared resources and facilities
  • Better access to markets and distribution
UK Examples:
  • Organic Milk Suppliers Co-operative (OMSCo) - Farmer-owned co-operative supplying organic milk
  • Arla Foods - European dairy co-operative owned by its farmer members, including thousands of UK dairy farmers
  • Anglia Farmers - Agricultural purchasing co-operative

6. Social Enterprises

Social enterprises are businesses that trade with the primary purpose of creating positive social or environmental impact rather than maximising profit for shareholders. They reinvest surpluses back into the business or community to achieve their social mission.

Key Characteristics:

  • Primary objective is social or environmental benefit
  • Profits reinvested in the mission rather than distributed to shareholders
  • Can take various legal forms (Ltd, CIC, charity, co-operative)
  • Trade goods or services like conventional businesses
  • Transparent about social impact
  • Accountable to stakeholders and communities

Common Legal Structures:

  • Community Interest Company (CIC) - Special type of limited company designed for social enterprises
  • Charitable company - Company limited by guarantee with charitable status
  • Co-operative society - Run democratically for benefit of members and community
UK Examples:
  • The Big Issue - Magazine sold by homeless vendors; profits support homeless individuals
  • Jamie Oliver's Fifteen Restaurant - Trained disadvantaged young people (now closed but was prominent example)
  • Divine Chocolate - Fairtrade chocolate company partly owned by cocoa farmers
  • The Eden Project - Environmental education and conservation attraction in Cornwall
  • Cafe Direct - Fairtrade tea and coffee company
  • Social Bite - Sandwich shop chain that employs homeless people and distributes food to those in need

Key Issues to Consider When Comparing Forms of Business

1. Control

Control refers to who makes decisions within the business and how much influence different stakeholders have.

Sole Trader: Complete control by the owner. All decisions made independently without consultation.

Private Limited Company (Ltd): Control shared among shareholders based on shareholding. Directors manage day-to-day operations. Original owners can maintain control by retaining majority shares.

Public Limited Company (plc): Control potentially diluted as shares are widely held. Shareholders elect board of directors. Risk of hostile takeover. Institutional investors may exert significant influence.

Co-operatives: Democratic control - typically "one member, one vote" regardless of capital contribution. Decisions made collectively, which can slow down processes but increases engagement.

Social Enterprises: Control structure varies depending on legal form. Often governed by mission and stakeholder interests rather than just shareholder value.

2. Objectives

Business objectives vary significantly between different forms of ownership.

Sole Trader & Private Ltd: Often focus on survival, profit maximisation, and growth. May prioritise work-life balance or lifestyle objectives.

Public Limited Company (plc): Primary objective typically profit maximisation and shareholder value. Strong focus on share price growth. May face short-term pressure from shareholders.

Co-operatives: Balance profit with member welfare. Worker co-operatives prioritise job security and fair wages. Consumer co-operatives focus on member satisfaction and fair prices.

Social Enterprises: Primary objective is social or environmental impact. Profit is a means to achieve the social mission rather than an end in itself. Must balance financial sustainability with social goals.

3. Sources of Finance

The ability to raise finance varies considerably between business forms.

Sole Trader: Limited to personal savings, bank loans, and retained profit. May struggle to secure large loans due to unlimited liability risk.

Private Limited Company (Ltd): Can raise capital by selling shares to private investors, bank loans, venture capital, and retained profit. Greater credibility when seeking loans. Share sales restricted to private investors.

Public Limited Company (plc): Access to large amounts of capital through public share issues on stock exchanges. Can issue bonds (debentures). Greater access to bank loans at favourable rates. Ability to raise funds through rights issues.

Co-operatives: Limited ability to raise capital as members typically have equal voting rights regardless of investment. Rely on member contributions, retained profit, and bank loans. Some co-operative banks exist to support co-operative businesses.

Social Enterprises: Can access grants, social investment, crowd-funding, and community shares. If charitable status, can receive donations and Gift Aid. Growing social investment market. Some traditional finance sources may be limited.

4. Distribution of Profits

How profits are distributed reflects the ownership structure and objectives of the business.

Sole Trader: Owner keeps all profits after tax. No legal requirement to share profits with anyone.

Private Limited Company (Ltd): Profits distributed to shareholders as dividends based on number of shares held. Directors decide dividend amounts. Profits may be retained for reinvestment.

Public Limited Company (plc): Regular dividend payments to shareholders expected. Dividend policy carefully managed to maintain share price. Pressure to maintain or increase dividends annually.

Co-operatives: Profits (surplus) shared among members, often based on usage or participation rather than capital invested. Some profits retained for development. Member dividends in consumer co-operatives.

Social Enterprises: Profits reinvested in the social mission rather than distributed to shareholders. Any surplus used to expand social impact or sustain operations. If charitable structure, no profit distribution permitted.

5. Unlimited and Limited Liability

Liability refers to the legal responsibility for business debts.

Unlimited Liability: The owner is personally responsible for all business debts. Personal assets (home, car, savings) can be seized to pay business debts. Applies to sole traders and partnerships.
Limited Liability: Shareholders are only responsible for business debts up to the amount they invested. Personal assets are protected. The business is a separate legal entity. Applies to limited companies (Ltd, plc) and some co-operatives.

Why it matters:

  • Limited liability reduces personal financial risk for investors
  • Makes it easier to attract investors and raise capital
  • Encourages entrepreneurship by protecting personal wealth
  • Banks may be more willing to lend to limited companies
  • However, sole traders may benefit from lower setup costs and greater privacy

6. Reasons to Buy Shares

Investors purchase shares in public and private companies for various reasons:

Capital Gain: Investors buy shares hoping the share price will increase, allowing them to sell at a profit. This is particularly attractive in growth companies.

Dividend Income: Regular dividend payments provide income for investors. Particularly attractive to pensioners and those seeking steady income streams. Some companies (e.g., utility companies) known for reliable dividends.

Voting Rights: Shareholders can vote on major company decisions, including board appointments. Large shareholders can influence company strategy and direction.

Ownership and Control: Buying sufficient shares can give an investor control over a business. Strategic investors may acquire shares to gain access to technology, markets, or supply chains.

Portfolio Diversification: Spreading investment across different companies and sectors reduces risk. Shares provide exposure to different industries and economic sectors.

Speculation: Short-term traders buy shares expecting quick price movements. Day traders and speculators aim to profit from volatility.

7. Influences on Share Price

Share prices fluctuate based on numerous factors:

Company Performance:

  • Profit announcements and financial results
  • Revenue growth and market share gains
  • Launch of successful new products or services
  • Efficiency improvements and cost reductions

Economic Factors:

  • Interest rate changes affect borrowing costs and investment returns
  • Economic growth or recession impacts consumer spending
  • Inflation affects real returns and company costs
  • Exchange rate movements impact international companies
  • Unemployment levels affect consumer confidence

Market Sentiment and Speculation:

  • Investor confidence and market optimism/pessimism
  • Media coverage and analyst recommendations
  • Market trends and sector performance
  • Fear and greed driving irrational behaviour

Political and Regulatory Factors:

  • Government policy changes (taxation, regulation)
  • Political stability or uncertainty
  • Changes in legislation affecting specific industries
  • Trade agreements and tariffs

Supply and Demand:

  • More buyers than sellers pushes prices up
  • More sellers than buyers pushes prices down
  • Share buybacks reduce supply, increasing price
  • New share issues increase supply, potentially reducing price

Industry-Specific Factors:

  • Technological changes and disruption
  • Competitor actions and market dynamics
  • Changes in consumer preferences
  • Commodity price movements (e.g., oil for airlines)

8. Significance of Share Price Changes

Changes in share price have important implications for various stakeholders:

For the Company:

  • Ability to Raise Finance: Higher share prices make it easier and cheaper to raise capital through new share issues
  • Takeover Risk: Low share prices make the company vulnerable to hostile takeovers. High prices provide protection
  • Reputation and Credibility: Rising share prices enhance company reputation, attract talent, and improve negotiating position with suppliers
  • Cost of Acquisition: Companies making acquisitions can use their shares as currency. High share prices make acquisitions more affordable
  • Employee Morale: Many employees have share options or share schemes. Rising prices boost morale and retention

For Shareholders:

  • Wealth: Rising share prices increase shareholder wealth through capital gains
  • Returns: Potential to sell shares at a profit or hold for future gains
  • Dividend Expectations: Strong share prices often associated with higher dividend payments
  • Confidence: Rising prices indicate market confidence in company prospects

For Management:

  • Performance Indicator: Share price used to judge management effectiveness
  • Remuneration: Executive pay often linked to share price performance through bonuses and options
  • Job Security: Persistent low or falling share prices can lead to management changes
  • Strategic Decisions: Pressure to maintain share price can influence strategy (sometimes leading to short-termism)

For Other Stakeholders:

  • Creditors: Rising share prices indicate financial health, improving creditworthiness
  • Suppliers: Strong share prices suggest a reliable, stable customer
  • Customers: May indicate company stability and ongoing support for products
  • Employees: Job security perception linked to company performance and share price
Short-Termism Concern: Excessive focus on share price can lead to short-term decision-making at the expense of long-term sustainability. Companies may cut investment, reduce quality, or make hasty decisions to maintain quarterly results and share price, potentially harming long-term competitiveness.

9. Reasons for Choosing a Form of Business

No form of business is "best". Owners choose the form that fits their situation, and the key factors are usually:

Factor Question the owner asks Likely choice
Risk How much could I lose if the business fails? High-risk ventures (borrowing, stock, contracts) favour limited liability: Ltd or LLP
Finance needed How much capital do I need to start and grow? Small needs: sole trader or partnership. Moderate: Ltd. Very large: plc
Control How much say do I want to keep? Full control: sole trader or Ltd with a majority stake. Less control accepted in a plc
Privacy Am I happy to publish my accounts? Sole traders and ordinary partnerships publish nothing; companies must file accounts; plcs disclose the most
Objectives Is the main aim profit, or a social or member benefit? Profit: company. Members' benefit: co-operative. Social mission: social enterprise / CIC
Cost and administration How much time and money can I spend on paperwork? Sole trader is simplest; plc is most expensive (regulation, audits, stock exchange fees)
Image and credibility Will customers, suppliers and banks take me seriously? "Ltd" or "plc" can improve credibility with suppliers and lenders

10. Reasons for Changing Form of Business

Businesses often change form as they grow, or as their owners' objectives change.

Sole trader or partnership → Private limited company (Ltd)

  • To gain limited liability as the business takes on more debt, staff and contracts
  • To raise more finance by selling shares to family, friends or business angels
  • To improve credibility with banks, suppliers and large customers
  • Possible tax advantages once profits are high (corporation tax rather than income tax)
  • Cost: loss of privacy (accounts published) and more administration

Private limited company → Public limited company (plc) and stock market flotation

  • To raise large amounts of capital for expansion through an initial public offering (IPO)
  • To let founders and early investors sell some of their shares and realise their wealth
  • To raise the business's profile and make it easier to use shares to buy other firms
  • Cost: loss of control, takeover risk, short-term pressure from shareholders, high flotation and compliance costs
UK Example: Deliveroo's flotation (2021)

Deliveroo floated on the London Stock Exchange in March 2021 to raise money for growth. To protect his control, founder Will Shu kept special shares with extra voting rights for a period after flotation, and several large investment funds refused to buy because of this structure and concerns about the treatment of riders. The share price fell by around a quarter on its first day of trading. This shows that becoming a plc brings scrutiny from investors as well as access to capital.

Public limited company → Private company ("going private" / delisting)

  • To escape short-term pressure from stock market investors and focus on long-term plans
  • To reduce the cost and disclosure of being listed
  • Often happens through a takeover by a private equity firm, which buys all the shares
  • Risk: private equity buyers often borrow heavily to fund the purchase, leaving the business with high debts
UK Example: Morrisons (2021)

After a bidding war, the supermarket chain Morrisons was bought by the US private equity firm Clayton, Dubilier & Rice in 2021 and taken off the stock market. It no longer had to report to thousands of shareholders, but the deal left the business carrying a large amount of debt, and analysts argued this made it harder for Morrisons to cut prices and invest in the years that followed.

Company → Employee ownership

  • Founders may sell to an employee ownership trust to protect jobs, culture and independence when they retire
  • Can increase staff motivation and commitment, as employees share in success
UK Example: Richer Sounds (2019)

Founder Julian Richer transferred 60% of the hi-fi retailer to an employee ownership trust in 2019, rather than selling to a competitor or private equity buyer, so that the business would stay independent and staff would benefit from its success.

11. Ownership and Control (the principal–agent problem)

In a sole trader or small Ltd, the owners usually also run the business. In a plc, ownership is spread across thousands of shareholders who appoint directors to run it for them. This is called the divorce (separation) of ownership and control.

  • Principals = shareholders, who own the business and want a good return
  • Agents = directors and managers, who control the business day to day

The problem is that agents may pursue their own objectives, such as higher pay, bonuses, status, empire-building through takeovers, or an easy life, rather than maximising shareholder value.

How shareholders try to keep control:

  • Voting at the Annual General Meeting (AGM), including electing or removing directors
  • Linking directors' pay to performance, for example through share options or bonuses tied to share price and dividends
  • Non-executive directors on the board to challenge executives
  • Large institutional shareholders and activist investors putting pressure on the board
  • Selling their shares, which lowers the share price and increases the threat of takeover
Evaluation: linking pay to the share price can make the principal–agent problem worse by encouraging short-termism. Directors might cut investment or training to boost this year's profits and their bonuses. Small private shareholders also have little real power; in practice large institutions such as pension funds decide most votes.

12. Effects of Ownership on Mission, Objectives, Decisions and Performance

Form Mission and objectives Decision-making Likely effect on performance
Sole trader / partnership Owner's personal goals: income, independence, lifestyle Quick and flexible, but limited by the owners' skills Responsive to customers, but growth limited by finance and time
Private Ltd Profit and growth, often with a long-term or family focus Owners can take long-term decisions without stock market pressure Can invest patiently (e.g. Dyson, JCB), but growth may be limited by private finance
Listed plc Shareholder value: profit, dividends, share price Board decides, under pressure from shareholders and analysts Access to capital supports growth, but short-termism and takeover threat can distort decisions

Tell Me Why: Quick-fire Questions

Answer out loud or on a mini-whiteboard, then reveal.

Tell me why a builder might switch from sole trader to Ltd.
To gain limited liability: as contracts and debts grow, a failed job could otherwise cost the owner their house. Ltd status also looks more credible to bigger clients.
Tell me why a plc might want to go private.
To escape short-term pressure from shareholders and the costs and disclosure of a listing, so managers can focus on a long-term turnaround.
Tell me why the principal–agent problem is bigger in a plc than a sole trader.
A sole trader owns and controls the business. In a plc, thousands of shareholders own it but directors control it, so their objectives can differ.
Tell me why co-operatives find it hard to raise capital.
"One member, one vote" means investing more gives no extra control, so outside investors have little reason to put in large sums.
Tell me why a founder might keep special voting shares when floating.
To raise capital from the public while keeping control of strategy, although some investors refuse to buy shares with weaker voting rights.
Tell me why an LLP suits a firm of accountants.
Partners share skills and profits, but limited liability protects each partner from losses caused by another partner's mistakes.

Exam-style Practice

6 marks

Analyse

Extract A: Northfield Glazing
Amir Khan has run Northfield Glazing as a sole trader for eight years. The business now employs 14 fitters and has just won a £400,000 contract to replace windows across a housing estate. To complete the contract Amir must borrow £120,000 to buy materials and a second van. His accountant has advised him to convert the business into a private limited company.

Analyse one benefit to Amir of converting Northfield Glazing into a private limited company.

Show guidance and indicative content

Structure: start with a definition, then build one developed chain of reasoning that uses the extract.

  • Definition: a private limited company is a separate legal entity whose owners have limited liability.
  • Application: Amir must borrow £120,000 for a £400,000 contract.
  • Chain: as a sole trader he has unlimited liability, so if the client fails to pay or the job overruns, the bank could claim his personal assets, such as his house → as a Ltd, the debt belongs to the company, so his personal loss is limited to his investment → this reduces the personal risk of taking on bigger contracts, making him more willing to grow the business.
  • Alternative benefit: improved credibility with lenders and large clients, making the £120,000 loan easier to secure.
9 marks

Assess

Assess whether a successful private limited company should become a public limited company and float on the stock exchange.

Show guidance and indicative content

For: access to large amounts of share capital without interest or repayment; lower gearing; founders can sell shares and realise wealth; higher profile; shares can be used to buy other businesses.

Against: loss of control; risk of hostile takeover; short-term pressure for dividends and share price; flotation and compliance costs; public disclosure of information.

Judgement depends on: how much finance is needed and whether alternatives (loans, venture capital) exist; how much the owners value control (could they keep special voting shares?); stock market conditions at the time (as Deliveroo's first day showed); whether the business's objectives are long-term.

15 marks

Evaluate

Evaluate whether the form of ownership is the most important influence on a business's objectives.

Show guidance and indicative content

Arguments that ownership matters most: plcs face shareholder pressure for profit and dividends; co-operatives and employee-owned firms put members and staff first; social enterprises are bound by their social mission; the principal–agent problem shapes plc objectives.

Counter-arguments: objectives are also shaped by the stage of the business (survival vs growth), the economic environment (e.g. recession), competition, stakeholder pressure, and the values of the leader. Two plcs can pursue very different objectives, and many private companies are just as profit-driven as plcs.

Conclusion (V·B·E·U): give a Verdict, explain Because (the key reason), acknowledge the strongest point against it (Even though…), and say what the answer depends on (Unless…). For example: ownership is a major influence because it decides who the business answers to, even though economic conditions can force any business to switch to survival, unless the owners deliberately choose long-term goals, as in employee-owned firms.

Financial Analysis: Market Capitalisation, Dividend Per Share, and Dividend Yield

1. Market Capitalisation

Market capitalisation (or market cap) represents the total market value of a company's outstanding shares. It is calculated by multiplying the current share price by the total number of shares issued.

Market Capitalisation = Share Price × Number of Shares Issued

Why Market Capitalisation Matters:

  • Company Size Indicator: Market cap shows the total value investors place on a company
  • Size Classification: In the UK, listed companies are usually grouped by stock market index: the FTSE 100 (the 100 largest by market cap), the FTSE 250 (the next 250) and smaller companies below that. Membership is reviewed every quarter as share prices change.
  • Takeover Implications: Indicates how much it would cost to buy the entire company
  • Index Inclusion: Determines eligibility for indices like FTSE 100 (largest 100 UK companies by market cap)
  • Risk Profile: Generally, larger market cap suggests more stability; smaller cap may offer higher growth potential but greater risk

Worked Example 1: Tesco plc (illustrative figures)

Given Information:

  • Current share price: £2.85
  • Number of shares issued: 7.5 billion

Calculation:

Market Capitalisation = £2.85 × 7,500,000,000

Market Capitalisation = £21,375,000,000

Market Cap = £21.38 billion

Interpretation: Tesco has a market capitalisation of £21.38 billion. This places it among the largest retailers in the UK and comfortably within the FTSE 100 index. The high market cap indicates significant investor confidence and substantial company value.

Worked Example 2: Marks & Spencer plc (illustrative figures)

Given Information:

  • Current share price: £1.95
  • Number of shares issued: 1.8 billion

Calculation:

Market Capitalisation = £1.95 × 1,800,000,000

Market Capitalisation = £3,510,000,000

Market Cap = £3.51 billion

Interpretation: M&S has a market capitalisation of £3.51 billion. While still substantial, this is only around a sixth of Tesco's value. The lower market cap could reflect challenges the company has faced in recent years, including competition and changing consumer preferences. However, it also suggests potential for growth if the company successfully implements its turnaround strategy.

Practice Question:

BP plc has 19.5 billion shares issued and a current share price of £4.20. Calculate the market capitalisation and classify the company by size.

Reveal answer & working

Market Capitalisation = £4.20 × 19,500,000,000 = £81,900,000,000

Market Cap = £81.9 billion (illustrative figures), which would place it among the largest companies in the FTSE 100

2. Dividend Per Share (DPS)

Dividend per share represents the total amount of dividend paid out to shareholders divided by the number of shares issued. It shows how much each share earns in dividends annually.

Dividend Per Share = Total Dividends Paid ÷ Number of Shares Issued

Why Dividend Per Share Matters:

  • Income for Investors: Shows the actual cash return per share owned
  • Company Health Indicator: Consistent or growing DPS suggests strong financial health
  • Investment Decision: Helps investors compare dividend returns across different companies
  • Sustainability Assessment: Very high DPS relative to earnings may be unsustainable
  • Share Attractiveness: Higher DPS can make shares more attractive to income-seeking investors

Worked Example 3: British American Tobacco plc (illustrative figures)

Given Information:

  • Total dividends paid in the year: £4.5 billion
  • Number of shares issued: 2.3 billion

Calculation:

Dividend Per Share = £4,500,000,000 ÷ 2,300,000,000

Dividend Per Share = £1.96 per share

DPS = £1.96

Interpretation: British American Tobacco pays £1.96 per share in annual dividends. This is relatively high, reflecting the mature, cash-generative nature of the tobacco industry. For an investor holding 1,000 shares, this would mean annual dividend income of £1,960. Tobacco companies traditionally pay high dividends to compensate investors for regulatory and health-related risks.

Worked Example 4: Comparing Two Companies

Company A (Utility Company):

  • Total dividends: £800 million
  • Shares issued: 1 billion
  • DPS = £800m ÷ 1bn = £0.80 per share

Company B (Tech Start-up):

  • Total dividends: £0 (reinvesting all profits)
  • Shares issued: 500 million
  • DPS = £0 ÷ 500m = £0 per share

Interpretation: Company A pays a steady dividend of £0.80 per share, typical of mature utility companies with stable cash flows. This makes it attractive to income investors, particularly retirees. Company B pays no dividend, instead reinvesting all profits into growth. This is common for growth companies and technology firms. While offering no income, Company B might offer greater potential for capital gains through share price appreciation. Different investors would prefer different companies based on their investment goals.

Reveal answer & working

Dividend Per Share = £3,200,000,000 ÷ 26,800,000,000

Real-world link: in 2024 Vodafone announced it would halve its dividend, using the cash to reduce debt and invest. Income investors who had relied on its high yield saw their dividend income cut sharply. This is a good example of why a high yield is only as good as the company's ability to keep paying it.

3. Dividend Yield

Dividend yield expresses the dividend as a percentage of the current share price. It shows the rate of return an investor receives from dividends alone, without considering capital gains.

Dividend Yield = (Dividend Per Share ÷ Current Share Price) × 100

Why Dividend Yield Matters:

  • Return Comparison: Allows comparison with other investments (bonds, savings accounts)
  • Income Investment Metric: Key consideration for income-focused investors
  • Value Indicator: High yield may indicate undervalued shares (or dividend risk)
  • Industry Benchmarking: Compare dividend yields within sectors
  • Investment Strategy: Some investors specifically target high-yield dividend stocks
Important Note: A very high dividend yield (e.g., over 8-10%) should be investigated carefully. It may indicate:
  • The share price has fallen significantly (increasing the yield calculation)
  • The company is in financial difficulty
  • The dividend may be unsustainable and could be cut
  • Market concerns about the company's future

Worked Example 5: National Grid plc (illustrative figures)

Given Information:

  • Dividend per share: £0.53
  • Current share price: £9.45

Calculation:

Dividend Yield = (£0.53 ÷ £9.45) × 100

Dividend Yield = 0.0561 × 100

Dividend Yield = 5.61%

Interpretation: National Grid offers a dividend yield of 5.61%, which is relatively high compared to the FTSE 100 average (typically around 3-4%). This is characteristic of utility companies, which generate stable, predictable cash flows. The high yield makes National Grid attractive to income investors. However, the company's growth potential may be limited due to its mature market position. This yield compares favourably to typical savings account interest rates and provides a steady income stream.

Worked Example 6: ASOS plc (Online Retailer) (illustrative figures)

Given Information:

  • Dividend per share: £0
  • Current share price: £3.80

Calculation:

Dividend Yield = (£0 ÷ £3.80) × 100

Dividend Yield = 0%

Interpretation: ASOS pays no dividend, resulting in a 0% dividend yield. This is common for growth-focused online retailers that prioritise reinvesting profits into expansion, technology, and market share growth. Investors in ASOS are seeking capital growth (share price appreciation) rather than income. This strategy is typical of younger, faster-growing companies in competitive markets. Income investors would avoid this stock, while growth investors might find it attractive if they believe in the company's expansion potential.

Worked Example 7: Comparing Dividend Yields

Company X (Established Retailer):

  • DPS: £0.15
  • Share price: £2.00
  • Dividend Yield = (£0.15 ÷ £2.00) × 100 = 7.5%

Company Y (Tech Giant):

  • DPS: £0.45
  • Share price: £32.00
  • Dividend Yield = (£0.45 ÷ £32.00) × 100 = 1.41%

Analysis:

Company X offers a higher dividend yield (7.5%) despite paying a lower absolute dividend per share. This is because its share price is much lower. The high yield might indicate:

  • Market concerns about the company's future (share price has fallen)
  • A mature, stable business in a low-growth industry
  • Potential dividend sustainability concerns

Company Y offers a lower dividend yield (1.41%) despite paying more in absolute terms per share. This suggests:

  • Strong share price performance (high price reduces yield percentage)
  • Market confidence in growth prospects
  • Company prioritises reinvestment over high dividend payments

Investment Decision: Income-focused investors might prefer Company X for higher yield, while growth-oriented investors might prefer Company Y for capital appreciation potential, despite lower yield.

Dividend Yield in Depth

a) Why yield matters more than DPS on its own

Dividend per share tells you how much cash you get per share. It does not tell you whether that is a good return, because it ignores what you had to pay for the share. A 50p dividend is excellent on a £5 share (10%) but poor on a £50 share (1%). Dividend yield turns the dividend into a percentage return on the money invested, so investors can compare it with:

  • other companies' shares, whatever their share price
  • the interest rate on savings accounts
  • the return on government bonds (gilts), which are seen as low-risk
The see-saw effect: share price is the denominator in the formula, so dividend yield moves in the opposite direction to the share price. If the dividend stays the same:
  • Share price rises → dividend yield falls
  • Share price falls → dividend yield rises
Because share prices change every trading day, the yield on a listed company changes every day too, even though the dividend itself is usually only set once or twice a year.
What happens Effect on dividend yield Likely meaning
DPS rises, share price unchanged Yield rises Higher profits or more confidence from directors; good news for income investors
DPS unchanged, share price rises Yield falls Market more optimistic about the company; new buyers get a lower income return
DPS unchanged, share price falls Yield rises Could be a bargain, or a warning sign that investors expect a dividend cut
DPS cut, share price falls Yield could go either way Usually bad news: lower income and a capital loss for shareholders
DPS rises and share price rises by the same % Yield unchanged Steady, healthy growth; shareholders gain income and capital

b) Yield is only half of a shareholder's return

Shareholders make money in two ways: dividends (income) and changes in the share price (capital gain or loss). Dividend yield measures only the first.

Total Shareholder Return (%) ≈ Dividend Yield + % Change in Share Price

Worked Example 8: Two years, same dividend

An investor buys shares at £5.00. The company pays a DPS of 25p.

Dividend yield on purchase = (£0.25 ÷ £5.00) × 100 = 5%

Year A: share price rises to £5.40 → capital gain = (£0.40 ÷ £5.00) × 100 = 8%. Total return ≈ 5% + 8% = 13%

Year B: share price falls to £4.50 → capital loss = (−£0.50 ÷ £5.00) × 100 = −10%. Total return ≈ 5% − 10% = −5%

Interpretation: the 5% yield was identical in both years, but in Year B the shareholder still lost money overall. A good yield does not guarantee a good investment; shareholders weigh yield against the risk of the share price falling.

Existing vs. new shareholders: the published yield uses today's share price, so it tells a new buyer what income return they would get now. Someone who bought years ago at a lower price earns a higher return on what they originally paid. For example, a shareholder who bought at £4.00 and now receives a 40p dividend earns 10% on their original money, even though the quoted yield at today's price of £8.00 is only 5%.

c) How dividend yield influences different shareholders

Not all shareholders want the same thing, so the same yield can be attractive to one group and irrelevant to another.

Type of shareholder What they want How dividend yield affects them
Retired individuals / income investors A regular, reliable cash income to live on Actively seek higher-yield shares (e.g. utilities, banks, tobacco). A dividend cut directly reduces their income, so they are likely to sell if the dividend looks at risk.
Pension funds and insurance companies Steady cash to pay pensions and claims Large institutional holders that value predictable dividends. Because they own big stakes, their buying or selling can move the share price significantly, and they vote at the AGM.
Income funds To deliver a target income to their own investors Some funds are only allowed to hold shares above a certain yield. If a company cuts its dividend, these funds may be forced to sell, pushing the price down further.
Growth investors A rising share price over time Often prefer a low or zero yield, because profits retained and reinvested should drive future growth. They may see a high payout as a sign the firm has run out of good investment ideas.
Short-term traders Quick profits from price movements Care less about yield itself, but react sharply to dividend announcements, since a surprise rise or cut moves the share price.
Founding families / employee shareholders Income plus continued control or job security May depend on dividends as personal income, but may also accept lower dividends if retained profit protects the business and jobs.

How yield changes shareholder behaviour:

  • Buying decisions: a yield above the return on savings or gilts can attract income investors, increasing demand for the shares and pushing up the price.
  • Selling decisions: if a dividend is cut, or the yield no longer beats safer alternatives, income investors sell, increasing supply and pushing the price down.
  • Voting and pressure: shareholders can vote against directors or the remuneration report at the AGM if dividends disappoint, and activist investors may publicly demand higher payouts.
  • Reinvesting dividends: many long-term shareholders reinvest dividends to buy more shares, so a higher yield compounds their holding faster over time.

Worked Example 9: The effect of interest rates

A utility company pays a DPS of 20p and its share price is £4.00, giving a yield of (£0.20 ÷ £4.00) × 100 = 5%.

Savings accounts pay 1%, so the 5% yield looks very attractive and income investors buy the shares.

The Bank of England then raises interest rates and savings accounts now pay 5%. Why take the risk of owning shares for the same 5% you could get from a safe savings account? Some investors sell, and the share price falls.

If the price falls to £3.20, the yield becomes (£0.20 ÷ £3.20) × 100 = 6.25%, which is high enough to compensate investors for the extra risk again.

Key point: this is why high-yield shares such as utilities often fall when interest rates rise. Investors are comparing dividend yield with what they could earn elsewhere.

d) Why businesses care about their dividend yield

A company does not set its yield directly. It chooses its dividend, and the market sets the share price. However, directors watch the yield closely because it shapes how investors see the business.

Reason Explanation
Supporting the share price A reliable, competitive yield attracts and keeps income investors, supporting demand for the shares. A higher share price raises market capitalisation and the company's value.
Raising new finance If the company later wants to issue more shares (e.g. a rights issue), a healthy share price means it can raise more money for each share issued. A history of dependable dividends makes investors more willing to buy new shares.
Protection against takeover Satisfied shareholders are less likely to sell to a bidder, and a strong share price makes the company more expensive to take over. Disappointed shareholders whose dividend has been cut may welcome a bid.
Signalling confidence Directors know more about future profits than outsiders. Raising the dividend signals they expect strong, lasting cash flow; cutting it signals trouble. Markets read dividend changes as information, which is why firms try hard to avoid cuts.
Attracting the "right" investors A mature, stable firm may deliberately offer a high, steady yield to build a loyal base of long-term income investors. A growth firm may pay little or nothing and attract investors who value reinvestment instead.
Accountability of directors Shareholders judge directors partly on dividends. Poor returns can lead to votes against directors at the AGM, pressure from activist investors, and directors losing their jobs.
Comparison with competitors Investors compare yields across a sector. If rivals offer a better yield with similar risk, investors may switch, so firms benchmark their dividend policy against competitors.

e) The trade-off: dividends vs. reinvestment

Every pound paid out as a dividend is a pound not retained in the business. Retained profit is usually the cheapest and most important source of internal finance, so a high dividend has an opportunity cost.

Paying a high dividend (high yield) Retaining profit (low or zero yield)
+ Keeps income-seeking shareholders happy
+ Signals confidence and financial strength
+ Supports share price in a mature business
− Less money for investment, R&D and expansion
− May need to borrow, raising interest costs and gearing
− Hard to sustain if profits fall 		+ Funds growth without borrowing or issuing shares
+ Strengthens the balance sheet for downturns
+ Can create larger capital gains later
− Income investors may sell, lowering the share price
− Shareholders may doubt managers will invest wisely
− Greater pressure from activist shareholders
Stakeholder conflict: paying large dividends can cause tension with other stakeholders. Employees may object if dividends rise while pay is frozen or jobs are cut; customers and regulators may object if a business pays shareholders rather than investing in service quality. UK water companies, for example, have faced strong public and political criticism for paying dividends to shareholders while under-investing in infrastructure. A dividend decision is therefore never just a shareholder issue.

f) The "dividend trap": when a high yield is a warning

Worked Example 10: A yield that looks too good to be true

January: DPS 30p, share price £6.00 → yield = (£0.30 ÷ £6.00) × 100 = 5%

June: the company issues a profit warning. DPS is still 30p (based on last year), but the share price falls to £3.00 → yield = (£0.30 ÷ £3.00) × 100 = 10%

A 10% yield looks very attractive, but the market is signalling that it does not believe the dividend can be paid.

Following year: the company cuts its DPS to 10p and the share price falls again to £2.50 → yield = (£0.10 ÷ £2.50) × 100 = 4%

Interpretation: an investor who bought in June for the "10% yield" received far less income than expected and lost money as the share price fell further. The published yield is backward-looking: it uses last year's dividend and today's price.

g) Limitations of dividend yield (evaluation)

  • Backward-looking: based on the most recent dividend, which may not be repeated.
  • Ignores capital gains: a low-yield share may still give a far better total return if its price rises strongly.
  • Distorted by share price swings: a falling price inflates the yield, so a high yield can signal risk rather than value.
  • Says nothing about sustainability: investors also need to check whether profits comfortably cover the dividend (the payout ratio or dividend cover).
  • One-off special dividends: a one-off payment can make the yield look temporarily high.
  • Sector differences: utilities and tobacco typically yield more than technology firms, so comparisons are only meaningful within a sector.
  • Other ways to return cash: some companies return money through share buybacks instead of dividends, which raises the share price rather than the yield.
  • Not relevant to most businesses: sole traders, most private companies and co-operatives have no quoted share price, so dividend yield mainly applies to plcs.
Exam technique – building a chain of analysis on dividend yield:
"Company X's dividend yield has risen from 3% to 7% (calculation). However, this is because its share price has halved while DPS was unchanged (cause). This suggests investors fear the dividend will be cut (consequence for shareholders). If it is cut, income investors such as pension funds are likely to sell, pushing the share price down further and making it harder and more expensive for X to raise finance through a share issue (consequence for the business)."
Then evaluate: it depends on whether profits can cover the dividend, how the yield compares with rivals and with interest rates, and whether the business needs the cash for investment.

Integrated Analysis: Bringing It All Together

When analysing companies and making investment decisions, it's important to consider all three metrics together:

Comprehensive Example: Comparing Two FTSE 100 Companies

Company Alpha (Energy Company):

  • Share price: £5.40
  • Shares issued: 18 billion
  • Total dividends paid: £7.2 billion

Calculations:

Market Cap = £5.40 × 18,000,000,000 = £97.2 billion

DPS = £7,200,000,000 ÷ 18,000,000,000 = £0.40

Dividend Yield = (£0.40 ÷ £5.40) × 100 = 7.41%

Company Beta (Software Company):

  • Share price: £28.00
  • Shares issued: 3 billion
  • Total dividends paid: £600 million

Calculations:

Market Cap = £28.00 × 3,000,000,000 = £84 billion

DPS = £600,000,000 ÷ 3,000,000,000 = £0.20

Dividend Yield = (£0.20 ÷ £28.00) × 100 = 0.71%

Comparative Analysis:

Metric Company Alpha (Energy) Company Beta (Software)
Market Capitalisation £97.2bn (Larger) £84bn
Dividend Per Share £0.40 (Higher absolute) £0.20
Dividend Yield 7.41% (Much higher) 0.71%

Investment Implications:

Company Alpha (Energy) would suit:

  • Income investors seeking high dividend returns
  • Conservative investors wanting established, large-cap stability
  • Investors comfortable with energy sector volatility and regulatory risks
  • Retirees needing regular income

Company Beta (Software) would suit:

  • Growth investors prioritising capital appreciation
  • Investors believing in technology sector growth
  • Those with longer investment horizons
  • Investors comfortable with lower immediate income and higher volatility

Risk Considerations:

Alpha's very high dividend yield (7.41%) could indicate market concerns about dividend sustainability, perhaps due to energy transition challenges or cyclical demand. The high yield might be attractive, but investors should investigate whether profits can sustain these payments long-term.

Beta's low yield reflects its growth focus and high share price. While offering less income, the high share price suggests market confidence in future growth. However, software companies can face disruption risks and intense competition.
