Part 0 · The big picture

Investment means spending money now on assets or projects that are expected to bring returns in the future — for example, new machinery, a new shop, an IT system, solar panels or a new product launch.

Investment appraisal is the use of financial (quantitative) methods to judge whether an investment is worthwhile and to compare one option with another. Managers look at:

  • the initial cost of the investment (paid in Year 0 — "today")
  • the expected net returns each year, and how many years they last
  • the timing of those returns — early or late?
  • the risk involved — how confident are we in the forecasts?

So… what's the point? Why calculate anything?

  • Big sums, hard to undo. Investments often cost a lot and can't easily be reversed. A bad decision can damage a business for years.
  • Money is limited. Choosing one project means giving up another (opportunity cost). Appraisal lets managers rank options.
  • Evidence, not gut feeling. Numbers help managers justify a decision to owners, shareholders or a bank lending the money.
  • Cash matters. Managers need to know how long cash will be tied up — especially if the business has a tight cash-flow position.
  • Is there a better use for the money? The money could simply sit in a bank earning interest, or be used to repay a loan. The project should beat that alternative.
  • Targets to review against. Forecasts give a benchmark to check the project against once it's running.

Key vocabulary

Part 1 · Payback

Definition

Payback period = the length of time it takes for an investment's net returns to repay (cover) the initial investment. It is measured in years and months. A shorter payback is usually preferred.

Why calculate it?

  • It answers the question every cautious manager asks first: "When do we get our money back?"
  • Liquidity: if cash is tight, or the money has been borrowed, getting it back quickly matters.
  • Risk: returns expected soon are more certain than returns expected in five years. A short payback means less time for things to go wrong.
  • Fast-changing markets: where technology may be out of date within a few years, a quick payback is essential.

How to calculate it

Method A — when the net return is the same every year

Payback (years) = Initial investment ÷ Annual net return

Method B — when net returns are different each year (the most common exam type)

  1. Write the initial investment as a negative number in Year 0.
  2. Add each year's net return to build a cumulative cash flow column.
  3. Find the last year the cumulative figure is still negative. That gives you the full years.
  4. Take the amount still to be repaid and divide it by the net return of the next year.
  5. Multiply that fraction by 12 to turn it into months.
Months = (Amount still to be repaid ÷ Net return in the payback year) × 12

⚠ Watch out

  • 0.75 of a year is not 7.5 months or 75 months — it is 0.75 × 12 = 9 months.
  • "2.4 years" is not "2 years 4 months". 0.4 × 12 = 4.8 months.
  • If months come out as a decimal (e.g. 4.8), write 4.8 months or round up to 5 months — the money isn't fully back until that month ends.
  • Always give units: years and months.

Part 1Payback — strengths, limitations, true/false

✔ Strengths of payback

  • Quick and simple to calculate and understand.
  • Focuses on cash — useful when liquidity is a worry or money is borrowed.
  • Favours early returns, which are more certain — lowers risk.
  • Useful where products or technology go out of date quickly.

✘ Limitations of payback

  • Ignores returns after payback, so says nothing about the overall return.
  • Ignores the time value of money — £1 in Year 3 counts the same as £1 in Year 1.
  • Based on forecasts; the further ahead, the less reliable.
  • No clear accept/reject answer unless a target is set; may encourage short-termism.

Project A (cost £10m) returns 4, 4, 5, 1 → payback 2 years 5 months, net cash gain £4m. Project B (cost £10m) returns 1, 1, 6, 8 → payback 3 years 3 months, net cash gain £6m. Payback picks A, even though B makes more overall.

Part 2 · Average Rate of Return (ARR)

Definition

Average rate of return = the average annual profit of an investment, expressed as a percentage of the initial investment. A higher ARR is preferred.

Why calculate it?

  • Payback ignores what happens after the money comes back. ARR looks at the whole life of the project — its overall profitability.
  • A percentage is easy to compare: with other projects, with a target ARR, or with the interest rate on a loan or savings account.
  • Shareholders and owners care about return on their money — ARR speaks their language.

How to calculate it

ARR (%) = (Average annual profit ÷ Initial investment) × 100
Average annual profit = (Total net returns − Initial investment) ÷ Number of years
  1. Add up all the net returns (Years 1 onwards).
  2. Subtract the initial investment → total profit.
  3. Divide by the number of years the project lasts → average annual profit.
  4. Divide by the initial investment and × 100.

Worked examples

Kettle & Crumb van

Cost £40,000; £16,000 × 5 years
① Total returns = £80,000
② Profit = 80,000 − 40,000 = £40,000
③ Average = 40,000 ÷ 5 = £8,000 per year
④ ARR = 8,000 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 20%

Inkwell Print press

Cost £120,000; 30, 45, 60, 50 (£000)
① Total returns = £185,000
② Profit = 185,000 − 120,000 = £65,000
③ Average = 65,000 ÷ 4 = £16,250 per year
④ ARR = 16,250 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = 13.54%

What does "profit" mean here?

In ARR, profit means total net returns minus the initial investment — the net cash gain from the project. It is not the same as the profit shown in the income statement, which also allows for things like depreciation.

⚠ Watch out

  • The most common mistake: forgetting to subtract the initial investment.
  • Divide by the number of years of returns. Year 0 is not a year of returns — a project with Years 0–4 lasts 4 years.
  • In step 4 divide by the initial investment, not the total returns.
  • ARR is a percentage — don't forget the % sign.

✔ Strengths of ARR

  • Uses all the returns over the project's life — measures overall profitability.
  • A percentage is easy to compare with targets, other projects and interest rates.
  • Fairly simple to calculate and understand.

✘ Limitations of ARR

  • Ignores the timing of returns — £10m in Year 1 counts the same as £10m in Year 4.
  • Ignores the time value of money.
  • Longer projects rely on forecasts far into the future, which are less reliable.
  • Doesn't tell you how quickly the money comes back (liquidity).

Part 3 · Net Present Value (NPV)

Definition

Net present value = the total present value of an investment's future net returns, minus the initial investment. It takes account of the time value of money. A positive NPV means the investment is financially attractive at the chosen discount rate — as long as the forecasts turn out to be accurate. On financial grounds, the higher the NPV, the better. Non-financial factors (Part 4) may still change the decision.

The big idea: the time value of money

Would you rather have £1,000 today or £1,000 in three years? Today — because:

  • Opportunity cost: money today could be put in a bank (or used to repay a loan) and earn interest.
  • Inflation reduces what money can buy in the future.
  • Risk: money promised in the future might not arrive.

If the interest rate is 10%, then £0.91 placed in a bank today grows to about £1 in one year. So £1 received in one year's time is worth only £0.91 today. That 0.91 is the discount factor. The further into the future, the smaller the discount factor.

In the exam, discount factors are always given. Year 0 always has a discount factor of 1.00. Notice: the higher the interest rate, the lower the discount factors.

Why calculate it?

  • It is the only one of the three methods that allows for the time value of money — early returns count for more than later ones.
  • It compares the project with the alternative (leaving the money in the bank at the discount rate).
  • It gives a clear financial decision rule: positive = financially attractive at that discount rate; negative = the money would earn more in the alternative — if the forecasts hold. It also gives a £ value for the difference.

How to calculate it

Present value = Net return × Discount factor
NPV = Total present value of returns − Initial investment
  1. Multiply each year's net return by that year's discount factor.
  2. Add up the present values.
  3. Subtract the initial investment.
  4. State the NPV — with brackets or a minus sign if negative — and its units.

✔ Strengths of NPV

  • Takes account of the time value of money.
  • Uses all returns over the project's life.
  • Clear financial decision rule, and a £ figure showing how much the project beats the alternative at the chosen discount rate.
  • Can reflect risk — a riskier project can be assessed with a higher discount rate.

✘ Limitations of NPV

  • More complex; harder for non-financial managers to understand.
  • The discount rate is a judgement — if interest rates change, so does the answer.
  • Still relies on forecasts of future returns.
  • Doesn't show how quickly the money comes back.
  • NPV is a £ figure, so it's hard to compare projects of very different sizes.

Part 4 · Beyond the numbers

The specification also asks you to weigh up investment criteria, non-financial factors, and risk and uncertainty. The calculation is never the whole answer.

1 · Investment criteria

Investment criteria are the targets a business sets that a project must meet before it is accepted, e.g. "payback within 3 years", "ARR of at least 12%", "positive NPV at 8%". They make decisions consistent, link investment to the business's objectives, and ensure a project earns more than the cost of the finance.

2 · Risk and uncertainty

Risk

The chance that the outcome differs from the forecast, where the probability can be estimated (e.g. from past data).

Uncertainty

Where the future cannot be predicted or given a probability (e.g. a new pandemic, a new technology, a sudden change in government policy).

Sources: inaccurate sales forecasts · changes in costs · recession or boom · interest-rate changes · exchange rates · competitor actions · new technology · the length of the project (the further ahead, the less reliable).

Ways to manage it: sensitivity analysis ("what if returns are 20% lower?") · a shorter payback target · a higher discount rate for riskier projects · market research or a pilot · comparing best-case and worst-case forecasts.

3 · Non-financial factors

  • Strategic fit — does it support the mission and objectives?
  • Employees — job losses, morale, retraining, skills needed.
  • Ethics, CSR and the environment — emissions, local community, reputation.
  • Customers and quality — will it improve the product or service?
  • Legal requirements — some investments must happen anyway (e.g. health and safety).
  • Availability of finance — can the business actually raise the money?
  • Competitors — what happens if we don't invest?

Harrow & Hale is a family-owned bakery in West Yorkshire with six shops. Joint managing directors Priya Hale and Tom Harrow have £300,000 available for investment this year, and must choose one of two options.

Option A — automated bread line (£240,000). Replaces older ovens in the central bakery. It would cut energy use by around 30% and improve consistency. It would also remove 6 of the 28 bakery jobs; some bakers have worked there for over 20 years, and the family has always described its staff as "part of the family".

Option B — a seventh shop in Leeds city centre (£300,000). Returns are expected to be high at first as the new shop attracts attention, then fall as two national chains open cafés on the same street. The lease is for 4 years. Forecasts are based on a small amount of market research.

Other information: the business has an overdraft of £65,000 against a limit of £80,000. It can borrow at 8%. Its investment criteria are: payback within 3 years, ARR of at least 10%, and a positive NPV at 10%.
