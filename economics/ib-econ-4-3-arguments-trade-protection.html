Arguments For Trade Protection

ArgumentExplanation
Infant (sunrise) industry protectionNew domestic industries may need temporary protection from established foreign competitors to grow large enough to achieve economies of scale and become internationally competitive.
National securityGovernments may protect industries seen as strategically essential (food, energy, defence production) so the country is not dangerously dependent on foreign supply in a crisis.
Health and safetyImport restrictions or standards can keep out goods that pose genuine health or safety risks to domestic consumers.
Environmental standardsProtection can prevent imports produced under weaker environmental regulations from undercutting domestic producers who face stricter (and costlier) environmental rules.
Anti-dumpingProtection can counter "dumping" — foreign firms selling below cost (often subsidised) to drive out domestic competitors, then raising prices once competition is eliminated.
Unfair competitionProtection can offset foreign government subsidies or other unfair advantages that let foreign firms undercut domestic producers on artificial, non-market grounds.
Balance of payments correctionRestricting imports can reduce a current account deficit by cutting import expenditure, particularly useful during a foreign exchange crisis.
Government revenueTariff revenue can be an important and relatively easy-to-collect source of government income, especially where domestic tax collection capacity is limited.
Protection of jobsProtection can preserve domestic employment in industries that would otherwise struggle to compete against lower-cost foreign producers.
Economically least developed country (ELDC) diversificationProtection can help an ELDC reliant on one or two primary exports build up new industries, reducing vulnerability to world price shocks in its traditional exports.

The infant industry argument is not a modern invention — it has a long, named intellectual history, and its most-cited real-world "success story" also shows exactly how hard it is to know when a protected industry has genuinely grown up.

1791 — Alexander Hamilton's "Report on Manufactures"→ 1841 — Friedrich List's "The National System of Political Economy"→ 1960s-80s — South Korea's chaebol-led industrialisation→ 1990s-2000s — selective liberalisation once globally competitive
Real-world: US Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton's 1791 Report on the Subject of Manufactures, submitted to Congress, is usually credited as the first systematic statement of the infant industry argument — Hamilton argued a young United States needed tariffs and subsidies to build up manufacturing capacity against a far more advanced Britain. German economist Friedrich List, writing in exile partly inspired by time spent in the US, generalised the idea in his 1841 book Das nationale System der politischen Ökonomie ("The National System of Political Economy"), arguing that Britain itself had only preached free trade after using protection to build its own industrial lead — a "kick away the ladder" critique still used in Paper 1 evaluation today.
Real-world: South Korea's military government under President Park Chung-hee launched the 1973 Heavy and Chemical Industry (HCI) Drive, channelling subsidised credit, tariff protection and directed lending toward a small number of family-controlled conglomerates — the chaebol (Samsung, Hyundai, LG, SK among the largest) — to build steel, shipbuilding, electronics and automobile capacity from almost nothing. By the 1990s, several chaebols had become globally competitive multinationals, and Korea gradually liberalised sector by sector through the 1990s and 2000s (a process accelerated, painfully, by the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and IMF bailout — see 3.1 for the macro side of that crisis). Evaluatively, this is the strongest real case FOR infant industry protection precisely because it had a visible, time-limited exit into competitiveness — unlike cases below where protection simply never ended.
Real-world: Ethiopia has used tariffs and import restrictions to nurture its developing textile and garment manufacturing sector — a classic infant industry argument, intended to give local firms time to reach competitive scale before facing full international competition.
Real-world: Japan maintains substantial protection for its domestic rice sector, partly justified on food security (national security) grounds — ensuring the country retains some capacity to feed itself even if global food supply chains were disrupted.
Real-world: Rwanda banned imports of secondhand clothing in 2018, citing both public health concerns and a wish to protect and grow its own domestic textile manufacturing industry — combining the health/safety and infant industry arguments in a single policy.
Real-world: India has imposed anti-dumping duties on various Chinese steel and solar panel products, arguing that underpriced, often state-subsidised Chinese exports were undercutting Indian producers unfairly.
Real-world: Sri Lanka imposed sweeping import restrictions during its 2022 foreign exchange and debt crisis, specifically to conserve scarce foreign currency and correct a rapidly worsening balance of payments position.
Real-world: Zambia has explored industrial policy and selective protection measures to diversify its economy beyond copper exports — directly addressing the ELDC diversification argument and the over-specialisation risk raised in the 4.1 HL extension file.
Real-world: In March 2018, US President Donald Trump used Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 — a national-security provision — to impose tariffs of 25% on imported steel and 10% on imported aluminium, arguing that reliance on foreign metal endangered America's capacity to produce ships, tanks and munitions. The measure applied even to close military allies (Canada, the EU, South Korea, Japan), which triggered a formal WTO dispute, and the EU retaliated in June 2018 with tariffs on politically symbolic US exports — including Harley-Davidson motorcycles, Kentucky bourbon and Levi's jeans — chosen specifically to pressure Republican-held states ahead of midterm elections. This case is a textbook illustration of how a "national security" justification (4.3) can blur into ordinary industrial protectionism, and of the retaliation argument against protection (Section 2 below) playing out in real time.
Real-world: The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which began its transitional reporting phase in October 2023 ahead of full charges from 2026, imposes a carbon price on imports of cement, steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen equivalent to the price EU producers already pay under the EU Emissions Trading System. The EU frames CBAM as protecting environmental standards (4.3) and preventing "carbon leakage" — domestic firms relocating production to countries with weaker climate rules — but trading partners including India, China, Brazil and South Africa have argued at the WTO that it is a disguised trade barrier that unfairly penalises developing-country exporters who did far less to cause historical emissions, a live link to the sustainable development material in 4.7.
Cross-reference: these arguments only make sense against the benefits-of-trade case built in 4.1 (comparative advantage, consumer gains from specialisation) — every argument for protection here is really a claim that, in a specific case, a cost 4.1 does not capture (an infant industry's future potential, a security risk, an externality) outweighs those standard gains from trade. The specific policy tools used to enact protection (tariffs, quotas, subsidies, embargoes) are covered in detail in 4.2.

Arguments Against Trade Protection

ArgumentExplanation
Misallocation of resourcesProtection encourages resources into industries where the country lacks a genuine comparative advantage, reducing overall efficiency.
RetaliationTrading partners may respond with their own tariffs or restrictions, escalating into a trade war that harms exporters on both sides.
Increased costsFirms that rely on imported components or raw materials face higher costs when those inputs are protected/taxed, raising their own production costs.
Higher pricesConsumers pay more for protected goods than they would under free trade, reducing their real purchasing power.
Less choiceProtection can reduce the range and variety of goods available to domestic consumers.
Domestic firms lack incentive to become more efficientShielded from foreign competition, protected firms may have less pressure to innovate, cut costs, or improve quality.
Reduced export competitivenessProtecting import-competing industries can raise costs economy-wide (e.g. more expensive protected inputs), making a country's exports less price-competitive internationally.
The same tariff diagram anchors both sides of this chapter: a tariff raising price from WP to WP+T lets domestic output rise (Q1→Q2) — the producer-surplus gain a "protection of jobs" argument (Section 1) points to, alongside government tariff revenue (also Section 1) — but it does so only by creating the two red deadweight-loss triangles, the concrete face of the "misallocation of resources" and "higher prices" arguments against protection below. Reading the same diagram from both directions is exactly the kind of evaluation Section 3 rewards.
Real-world: China and Australia engaged in a tit-for-tat cycle of tariffs and trade restrictions (on Australian barley, wine, and other exports) following diplomatic tensions in 2020 — a clear illustration of the retaliation risk, which cost Australian exporters significant lost sales in the Chinese market.
Real-world: India's historical "License Raj" era of extensive import substitution and protection (before major trade liberalisation reforms in 1991) is frequently cited as an example of how prolonged protection can leave domestic firms inefficient and globally uncompetitive when they eventually do face international competition.
Real-world: Argentina's decades of import-substitution industrialisation policy are often cited as contributing to persistently weak export competitiveness relative to countries that pursued more export-oriented, trade-open development strategies over the same period.

Two named disputes show the full crisis → response → outcome arc of anti-dumping protection, and why "legitimate anti-dumping response" and "costly, retaliation-inviting protectionism" are often the same policy viewed from two sides.

Crisis: a surge of low-priced imports threatens a domestic industry → government imposes anti-dumping or safeguard duties to stop the surge → affected exporting country disputes the measure at the WTO and/or retaliates with its own tariffs → economists later estimate the cost to domestic consumers and downstream firms per domestic job actually preserved
Real-world (EU–China solar panels, 2012–13): Following a formal complaint by European manufacturers (led by Germany's SolarWorld) that Chinese firms were exporting solar panels below cost with the help of state subsidies, the European Commission opened its largest-ever anti-dumping case in September 2012 and, in June 2013, imposed provisional anti-dumping duties on Chinese solar panels averaging 47.6%. China responded within days by opening its own anti-dumping investigation into EU wine exports — widely seen as retaliation aimed at French wine producers with political influence in Brussels. The dispute was ultimately settled in July 2013 through a negotiated "minimum price undertaking" rather than the full tariff, after EU member states split over whether to support German manufacturers or cheaper Chinese-panel-buying solar installers — a vivid illustration of how protection creates domestic winners (protected producers) and losers (downstream firms and consumers) simultaneously.
Real-world (US–China tyres, 2009): In September 2009, President Obama used Section 421 of US trade law (a China-specific safeguard provision) to impose tariffs starting at 35% on Chinese passenger and light-truck tyres, after the US steelworkers' union petitioned that a rapid rise in Chinese tyre imports was costing American jobs. China formally challenged the tariff at the WTO (and lost) but also retaliated by opening anti-dumping investigations into US exports of chicken parts and auto parts. A widely cited 2012 Peterson Institute for International Economics study estimated the tariffs saved around 1,200 US tyre-manufacturing jobs but cost American consumers roughly $1.1 billion in higher tyre prices — over $900,000 per job saved — while the same higher prices reduced consumer spending elsewhere, an estimated offsetting loss of retail jobs. This is one of the most frequently cited Paper 1 examples for quantifying the "increased costs/higher prices" argument against protection in concrete numbers rather than in the abstract.
Cross-reference: both disputes above were eventually shaped by WTO dispute settlement rules, the multilateral mechanism explored fully in 4.4 — anti-dumping and safeguard measures are among the very few forms of protection the WTO explicitly permits, provided they follow its procedural rules, which is exactly why so many real disputes end up litigated there rather than simply retaliated against unilaterally.

Free Trade versus Trade Protection: An Evaluation

When Protection May Be More Justified

  • A genuinely new industry with realistic long-run potential to reach competitive scale (infant industry)
  • Strategic goods where total import dependence carries serious risk (food, energy, key medical supplies)
  • A severe, acute balance of payments or foreign exchange crisis
  • Clear evidence of dumping or unfairly subsidised foreign competition

When Protection Is Harder to Justify

  • Long-standing protection with no sign the industry will ever become competitive ("infant" industries that never grow up)
  • Politically popular but economically costly protection of declining industries purely to save jobs
  • Protection likely to trigger costly retaliation from trading partners
  • Protection that primarily just raises domestic prices with no strong strategic or developmental justification
Exam tip: the strongest Paper 1 essays on this topic never simply list arguments for and against — they make a judgement, using specific criteria (how temporary is the protection? is there a credible exit strategy? how essential is the good strategically?) to reach a reasoned conclusion about whether protection is justified in a particular case.

How This Topic Is Assessed: Command Terms and Paper Structure

Where this shows up on the papers: "To what extent should governments protect domestic industries?" is one of the most frequently examined Paper 1 essay titles in the whole syllabus — part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain two arguments for (or against) protection using named examples, while part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) asks you to evaluate, which means weighing genuine cases (South Korea's chaebols, Japan's food security) against abuse cases (US Section 232, permanent import-substitution) rather than just listing both sides. Paper 2 is built from two linked Text extracts with numbered paragraphs plus data tables, and every sub-question cites an exact reference (e.g. "Text A, paragraph 3"): expect two 2-mark "define the term..." sub-parts on bolded terms from the text (e.g. dumping, tariff), a 2–3 mark AO4 calculation such as the dumping-margin formula above (occasionally paired with a 2-mark "sketch" of a tariff or quota diagram — a smaller in-text version, as against the larger "draw" request in Section 4.2), a run of 4-mark "using the diagram/information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each anchored to a named paragraph or table, and a single 15-mark "discuss"/"evaluate" question weighing a named protection measure — strong answers always link back to who gains and who loses (Section 3), not just whether the policy "works".

The Golden Chain: Evaluating Any Protection Case on Paper 1

Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units (% or $)→ Interpret the answer in context
Dumping Margin (%) = ((Normal Value − Export Price) ÷ Export Price) × 100
Forward — Calculating a Dumping Margin
A firm's normal domestic selling price (normal value) for a good is $50. It exports the same good at $38.
Dumping margin = ((50 − 38) ÷ 38) × 100 = 31.6% — the good is being "dumped" at a price 31.6% below its normal value, a common trigger for anti-dumping duties.
Reverse-Direction: Finding the Export Price
Normal value = $64; the dumping margin has been calculated at 28%. Find the export price.
64 = Export price × (1 + 0.28) → Export price = 64 ÷ 1.28 = $50
Given: normal value + dumping margin→ Export price = Normal value ÷ (1 + margin)→ check units are $
Exam tips:
  1. Always define "normal value" clearly in a dumping margin answer — it is the price the firm charges in its own domestic market (or its cost of production), not an arbitrary benchmark.
  2. When evaluating arguments for and against protection, always tie your judgement to specific criteria (how temporary, how strategic, how likely to trigger retaliation) rather than simply listing points on both sides.
  3. Use specific real-world examples (Ethiopia/Rwanda for infant industry; Japan for national security; India/Sri Lanka for anti-dumping/BoP crises; China-Australia for retaliation) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays.
