The Phillips Machine (MONIAC) — Historical Context

Bill Phillips (1914–1975) was a New Zealand engineer-turned-economist. In 1949, while a student at the London School of Economics, he built the Monetary National Income Analogue Computer (MONIAC): a machine about 2 metres tall that used coloured water flowing through transparent pipes and tanks to represent money circulating through the UK economy.

The machine made abstract macroeconomic relationships visible. Users opened and closed valves representing tax rates, spending and interest rates, and watched the water redistribute itself between tanks for households, firms, the Treasury and the rest of the world.

🗺️ Where are MONIACs today?

Around 12–14 machines were built. The prototype, built with help from Walter Newlyn of the University of Leeds, is on display at Leeds University Business School. Others can be seen at the Science Museum in London, the University of Cambridge (restored and working), the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Museum (working), the University of Melbourne and Istanbul University.

⚙️ How it worked

Water representing income flowed from the top of the machine. Some drained away as leakages (saving, taxation, imports) into separate tanks; injections (investment, government spending, exports) flowed back into the spending stream, which a pump lifted back to the top as next period's income.

📡 Why it mattered

Before digital computers were common, the MONIAC solved a whole system of economic equations at once, in real time. It was used for teaching at the LSE and elsewhere, and showed economists how time lags and feedback could make an economy overshoot or oscillate.

Aggregate Demand: AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

Aggregate demand is total planned spending on an economy's output at a given price level. In the machine, each component is a separate pipe flowing into the spending pool at the bottom.

🟢 Consumption (C) — about 60% of UK GDP

Determinants: disposable income, interest rates, confidence, wealth and credit. The marginal propensity to consume decides how much of each extra £1 of disposable income is spent.

🟡 Investment (I) — the most volatile component

Determinants: interest rates (the cost of borrowing), expected profits (affected by corporation tax), business confidence and technology. Investment also adds to productive capacity, which shifts LRAS.

🔵 Government spending (G)

A policy decision, and a direct injection into the circular flow. Note that welfare benefits are transfers, not part of G. They rise automatically in a downturn (an automatic stabiliser).

🟣 Net exports (X − M)

Exports are an injection and imports a leakage. Both depend on the exchange rate. Imports also rise with UK income, which is why a boom tends to widen the trade deficit.

The multiplier in an open economy

An injection of £1 becomes someone's income; part is re-spent, and part leaks out as saving, tax or imports. The process repeats until the extra leakages equal the original injection:

k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM) = 1 ÷ MPW
💡 Examiner's note: why the UK multiplier is small

The simple formula 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) ignores tax and imports. In an open economy with a high tax take, MPW is large, so the multiplier is often estimated at only about 1–1.5. In this simulator MPW is set at about 0.7, so k ≈ 1.4. That is a calibration choice, not a UK estimate. Run Task 1 to watch the rounds of spending build up quarter by quarter.

Fiscal Policy — Government Spending & Taxation

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending (G) and taxation (T) to influence aggregate demand, the distribution of income and the allocation of resources.

📈 Expansionary fiscal policy

↑ G or ↓ T → ↑ AD → ↑ real GDP and ↓ unemployment in the short run. It raises the budget deficit, and if the economy is already near capacity it mainly adds to inflation.

📉 Contractionary fiscal policy

↓ G or ↑ T → ↓ AD. It is used to reduce inflationary pressure or the deficit, but the multiplier works in reverse: lower output reduces tax receipts and raises benefit spending, so the deficit falls by less than the cut.

⚖️ Automatic stabilisers

Tax receipts and welfare spending move with the economic cycle without any new decision being made. They cushion downturns and cool booms, and they explain why the budget balance worsens in a recession.

🕐 Time lags & crowding out

There are recognition, implementation and impact lags. Heavy government borrowing may also push up interest rates and crowd out private investment, especially near full capacity.

💡 Examiner's note: deficit vs debt

The budget deficit is a flow: each year's gap between spending and receipts. National debt is a stock: accumulated past borrowing. The debt-to-GDP ratio can fall even while the government runs a deficit, if nominal GDP (real growth plus inflation) grows fast enough.

Monetary Policy — Bank Rate & the Bank of England

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) sets Bank Rate to meet the government's 2% CPI inflation target. In September 2026 the MPC held Bank Rate at 3.75% on a 6–3 vote, with three members voting to raise it, after an energy price shock pushed CPI inflation up to 3.1%.

The transmission mechanism

  • Consumption: ↑ rates → ↑ mortgage and loan costs, ↑ reward for saving → ↓ C
  • Investment: ↑ rates → ↑ cost of borrowing and ↑ required return → ↓ I
  • Exchange rate: ↑ rates → hot money inflows → ↑ £ → ↓ X and ↑ M
  • Asset prices: ↑ rates → ↓ house and share prices → negative wealth effect → ↓ C
  • Expectations: a credible central bank keeps expected inflation anchored near 2%, which stops wage-price spirals developing

⏳ Lags

Many mortgages are fixed for two or five years, and firms' investment plans take time to change. So a change in Bank Rate takes 18–24 months to have its full effect. In the simulator, only part of each change is felt by borrowers each quarter (shown under the Bank Rate slider).

🎯 Real vs nominal rates

What matters for spending is the real interest rate: the nominal rate minus expected inflation. If inflation expectations rise and Bank Rate stays put, policy effectively becomes looser. That is why rates must rise by more than inflation to bring it down.

💡 Examiner's note: conflicts and trade-offs

Higher rates reduce inflation at the cost of lower output and higher unemployment, at least in the short run. A cost-push shock makes this especially hard, because inflation and unemployment rise together.

The Phillips Curve — Inflation vs Unemployment

In 1958 Bill Phillips published evidence of an inverse relationship between wage inflation and unemployment in the UK between 1861 and 1957. This became the Phillips curve, suggesting that governments could choose lower unemployment at the cost of higher inflation.

📊 Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)

Demand-side policy moves the economy along the SRPC: expansion lowers unemployment and raises inflation. Each SRPC is drawn for a given level of expected inflation.

📐 Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)

Friedman and Phelps (1967–68): if unemployment is held below the natural rate, expected inflation rises and the SRPC shifts up. The LRPC is vertical at the NAIRU, so there is no long-run trade-off.

🌪️ Stagflation (1970s)

After the 1973 oil shock UK inflation peaked at around 24% in 1975, while unemployment passed one million. Rising inflation and unemployment together contradicted the simple Phillips curve and supported the expectations-augmented model.

🎯 NAIRU

The Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment. Push unemployment below it and inflation keeps rising; hold unemployment above it and inflation keeps falling. The simulator sets the NAIRU at 4.5%, but that is a calibration choice. The real NAIRU cannot be observed directly, and estimates change over time.

💡 Critical thinking: is the Phillips curve dead?

In the late 2010s UK unemployment fell below 4% while inflation stayed near target: the curve looked "flat". Possible explanations include anchored expectations, globalisation and weaker bargaining power. The 2021–23 inflation surge revived the debate.

How the Simulator Model Works

The model is a simplified, textbook-style model of a UK-sized economy. It is not a forecast. Every number below is a simulator calibration choice, picked so that effects are visible, not an established fact about the UK. See ⚙️ Model assumptions above the dashboard for the full list.

  • Demand: this quarter's spending is C + I + G + X − M, based on last quarter's income. This is how the multiplier rounds appear over time.
  • Leakages: saving, income tax and imports all rise with income. MPC = 0.75 of disposable income, tax = the income-tax slider, MPM = 0.3.
  • Interest rates: only about 40% of a Bank Rate change reaches borrowers each quarter. Spending responds to the real rate, and exports and imports respond to the exchange rate.
  • Inflation: expected inflation + 0.35 × output gap + cost-push shocks. Expected inflation adjusts slowly towards actual inflation, and is pulled back towards 2% by the Bank's credibility.
  • Unemployment: Okun's law. Each 1% of positive output gap lowers unemployment by 0.5 points below the 4.5% NAIRU, with a lag.
  • Supply side: investment above its normal level slowly raises potential output (LRAS). An oil shock raises costs (SRAS shifts left) and lowers potential slightly.
  • Public finances: receipts (income tax plus other taxes plus corporation tax) minus G, welfare transfers (which rise with unemployment) and debt interest. Debt is shown as a % of nominal GDP, which also grows with trend growth of 1.5% a year.

Every model is a simplification. Real economies face uncertainty, changing expectations and shocks that no one predicted. Use the model to understand mechanisms, not to make precise predictions.

📚 How to use the Phillips Machine

1. Change → predict → run

The simulator starts paused. Make one change, predict what will happen, then press +1 quarter to let three months pass and check. Real policy works with lags, so some effects take several quarters to show. ⏩ +1 year skips ahead, and ▶ Play lets the machine run continuously, like the original.

2. The machine

  • National income tank (top): how full it is shows the output gap.
  • Leakages: saving (S), income tax (T) and imports (M) drain out of the income stream.
  • Injections: investment (I), government spending (G) and exports (X) flow back into the spending pool.
  • Pipe width shows the size of each flow. If injections are bigger than withdrawals, next quarter's income will rise.
  • Tap a gold valve to jump to its slider.

3. The dashboard

Seven macroeconomic measures at a glance. Each tile shows whether the measure is within or outside a target. Only the 2% inflation target is official; the rest are simulator targets, normative choices that are worth debating. Tap any tile to see why.

4. Modes

  • Free Play: experiment however you like.
  • Task Mode: six guided investigations with prediction questions and “tell me why” discussion prompts. Good for lessons and homework.
  • Challenges: can you hit the simulator’s targets? Scenarios are inspired by real crises (2008, the 1970s, 2022, 2026). They are not re-runs of history. Each starts paused so you can set your opening policy. You have a set number of quarters, and you win if the targets are met in the final two quarters.

5. Board mode

Makes the text larger for projecting to a class.

ℹ️ About the MONIAC

Historical background

The Phillips Machine, formally the Monetary National Income Analogue Computer (MONIAC), was built in 1949 by New Zealand economist Bill Phillips while he was a student at the London School of Economics. It stood about 2 metres tall and used coloured water flowing through transparent pipes to show how money circulates through an economy.

The Phillips curve connection

Phillips is famous twice over: for the MONIAC, and for his 1958 paper on the relationship between wage inflation and unemployment, which became the Phillips curve. This simulator brings the two together.

Where to see one

Around 12–14 machines were built. You can see them at Leeds University Business School (the prototype), the Science Museum in London, the University of Cambridge and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Museum (both working), the University of Melbourne and Istanbul University.

Specification coverage

  • Macroeconomic objectives: growth, inflation, unemployment, the balance of payments and the public finances
  • Aggregate demand, aggregate supply and the multiplier
  • Fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy
  • Short-run and long-run Phillips curves, NAIRU and expectations

These numbers are choices made to build this simulator, picked so that the effects are clear and the challenges playable. They are not established facts about the UK economy. Economists disagree about many of them, and some (like the NAIRU and the neutral interest rate) cannot be observed at all.

💡 Evaluation tip

Questioning a model’s assumptions is evaluation. “The effect of the rate rise depends on how quickly it passes through to borrowers, which depends on the share of fixed-rate mortgages…” is exactly the kind of point that earns top-band marks.
