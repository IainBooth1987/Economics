Calculating the Effects of a Tariff

HL only
Worked Example — Full Tariff Calculation
World price = $5. At $5: domestic Qs = 50 units, domestic Qd = 150 units. A $2 tariff raises the domestic price to $7. At $7: domestic Qs = 70 units, domestic Qd = 130 units.
EffectFormulaCalculation
Consumer surplus loss0.5 × (Qd₁ + Qd₂) × ΔP0.5 × (150+130) × 2 = $280
Producer surplus gain0.5 × (Qs₁ + Qs₂) × ΔP0.5 × (50+70) × 2 = $120
Government revenueTariff × Quantity of imports after tariff2 × (130 − 70) = $120
Deadweight lossCS loss − PS gain − Govt revenue280 − 120 − 120 = $40
Cross-check via triangles: DWL = 0.5×(Qs₂−Qs₁)×ΔP + 0.5×(Qd₁−Qd₂)×ΔP = 0.5×20×2 + 0.5×20×2 = 20 + 20 = $40 ✓ — the two DWL triangles represent production inefficiency (extra domestic output costing more than the world price) and consumption inefficiency (consumers priced out of units they valued above the world price).
Numeric version of the tariff diagram: world price $5 → $7 after a $2 tariff; Qs 50→70; Qd 150→130.
Mar 2018: Section 301 tariffs begin (steel & aluminium)→ 2018–19: tariffs escalate to 25% on ~$250bn of Chinese goods→ China retaliates on US soybeans, pork & other exports→ Amiti, Redding & Weinstein (2019) quantify pass-through & DWL→ "Phase One" deal signed 15 Jan 2020 pauses further escalation
Real-world: Quantifying the US–China Tariff Deadweight Loss (2018–19) The 2018–19 US Section 301 tariffs on Chinese imports (full political timeline in the SL file's Section 1) gave economists an unusually large real-world data set to test exactly the theory in this section. Using detailed customs data, economists Mary Amiti (Federal Reserve Bank of New York), Stephen Redding (Princeton) and David Weinstein (Columbia) found in a widely-cited 2019 study that the tariffs were overwhelmingly passed through to US import prices — American consumers and firms, not Chinese exporters, bore almost the full cost — and estimated the associated deadweight loss at over a billion dollars a month by the second half of 2019, on top of several billion dollars a month in tariff revenue collected by the US government. This is the real-world counterpart of the DWL formula above: the same CS loss − PS gain − government revenue logic, just applied at national-economy scale using customs and firm-level price data instead of a single Qs/Qd diagram.

Calculating the Effects of a Quota

HL only
Worked Example — Quota with Identical Price/Quantity Effects
Using the same market: a quota restricting imports to 60 units (matching the tariff's post-tariff import level of 130−70=60) produces an identical price rise to $7 and identical Qs=70, Qd=130.
EffectFormulaCalculation
Consumer surplus loss0.5 × (Qd₁ + Qd₂) × ΔPSame as tariff = $280
Producer surplus gain0.5 × (Qs₁ + Qs₂) × ΔPSame as tariff = $120
Quota rent(Pquota − Pworld) × Quota quantity(7 − 5) × 60 = $120
Deadweight lossCS loss − PS gain − Quota rent280 − 120 − 120 = $40
The only difference from the tariff case: the $120 in the middle rectangle is quota rent, not government revenue. Whether this benefits the domestic government (if it auctions import licences competitively) or foreign exporters (if they are simply allocated licences for free) is a key qualitative distinction — the calculation itself is otherwise identical to the tariff case.
Worked Example (Paper 3, Reverse Direction) — Finding the Quota Size from a Given Rent
Question: In a market, imposing a quota raises the domestic price from a world price of $6 to $9. At the new higher price, Qs = 80 and Qd = 180. The quota rent is reported as $270. Find the size of the quota (the permitted import quantity).
Step 1 — rearrange the quota rent formula: Quota quantity = Quota rent ÷ (Pquota − Pworld).
Step 2 — substitute: Quota quantity = 270 ÷ (9 − 6) = 270 ÷ 3 = 90 units.
Step 3 — sense-check: the quota quantity (90) should equal Qd − Qs at the new price: 180 − 80 = 90 ✓ — this cross-check confirms the quota size is internally consistent with the stated diagram values.
Exam tip: when a question gives you a quota diagram, always check whether the question specifies who holds the import licences — this determines who captures the quota rent, which is frequently the actual mark-earning point even after you've done the arithmetic correctly.
Real-world: Quantifying US Sugar Quota Rents The United States has restricted sugar imports through a tariff-rate quota system dating back to a 1934 Sugar Act (in its modern form since 1990), allocating fixed country-by-country quotas at a low in-quota tariff and a much higher out-of-quota tariff. Because only a limited group of countries and firms hold import licences, the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) and USDA have periodically estimated the resulting "quota rent" transferred to licence-holders and foreign producers at over a billion dollars a year, while separate studies have put the combined annual cost to US food manufacturers and consumers — lost consumer surplus plus quota rent together — at several billion dollars. Because the US government does not auction these licences competitively, the rent is captured mostly by the foreign exporting countries permitted to sell into the US quota and by protected domestic sugar producers, not by the US Treasury — precisely the "who captures the middle rectangle" distinction stressed throughout this section.

Calculating the Effects of a Subsidy

HL only
Worked Example — Production Subsidy Calculation
World price = $5 (unchanged for consumers). A $2 per-unit production subsidy raises the effective price received by domestic producers to $7. At $7 (on the original supply curve): Qs rises from 50 to 70 units. Since the consumer price is unchanged, Qd stays at 150 throughout.
EffectFormulaCalculation
Consumer surplus change—No change — consumers still pay $5 and buy the same quantity
Producer surplus gain0.5 × (Qs₁ + Qs₂) × Subsidy0.5 × (50+70) × 2 = $120
Government costSubsidy per unit × Qs₂ (new quantity supplied)2 × 70 = $140
Deadweight loss0.5 × (Qs₂ − Qs₁) × Subsidy0.5 × (70−50) × 2 = $20
Note the government cost ($140) exceeds the producer surplus gain ($120) — the difference ($20) is exactly the deadweight loss: the extra 20 units of domestic output cost more in resources than importing them at the world price would have.
Worked Example (Paper 3, Reverse Direction) — Finding the Subsidy Rate from a Given Deadweight Loss
Question: A government subsidy causes domestic quantity supplied to rise from Qs = 60 to Qs = 90. The resulting deadweight loss is reported as $30. Find the per-unit subsidy rate.
Step 1 — rearrange the DWL formula: Subsidy = DWL ÷ [0.5 × (Qs₂ − Qs₁)].
Step 2 — substitute: Subsidy = 30 ÷ [0.5 × (90 − 60)] = 30 ÷ 15 = $2 per unit.
Step 3 — cross-check using government cost and producer surplus gain: Producer surplus gain = 0.5 × (60+90) × 2 = $150; Government cost = 2 × 90 = $180; DWL = Government cost − PS gain = 180 − 150 = $30 ✓ — matching the value given in the question confirms the $2 subsidy rate is correct.
Common error: students often try to calculate a "consumer surplus loss" for a production subsidy — but since the subsidy does not change the price consumers actually pay, there is no consumer surplus effect at all. This is the single biggest structural difference between a subsidy and a tariff/quota.
Real-world: Egypt's fuel and bread subsidies (introduced in the SL file) illustrate exactly this pattern at national scale — consumers continue paying a low, subsidised price, while the government cost of maintaining that price has repeatedly exceeded budget projections, forcing periodic subsidy reform.
Real-world: The Boeing–Airbus WTO Subsidy Dispute (2004–2021) In 2004, the US filed a WTO complaint arguing the EU had illegally subsidised Airbus through low-interest "launch aid" loans, while the EU counter-filed against US federal, state and NASA/Pentagon support for Boeing. After 17 years of litigation — one of the longest disputes in WTO history — the WTO authorised the US in 2019 to impose tariffs on about $7.5 billion of EU goods annually (from French wine to Scotch whisky), and authorised the EU in 2020 to impose tariffs on about $4 billion of US goods, in each case because the WTO found the subsidies had let Airbus and Boeing respectively sell aircraft more cheaply than an unsubsidised producer could — the WTO-scale equivalent of the "government cost creates a deadweight loss" logic above. The dispute was finally suspended by a five-year tariff truce announced in June 2021 by US President Joe Biden and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, explicitly framed as clearing the way for the US and EU to cooperate against Chinese state subsidies to its own aircraft manufacturer, COMAC — an example of how 4.2-level subsidy disputes connect directly to the 4.4 discussion of trade blocs and multilateral cooperation.

Where this shows up on the papers: the full quantitative welfare-effect calculations in this file — consumer surplus loss, producer surplus gain, government revenue or cost, quota rent, and deadweight loss, in both forward and reverse directions — are examinable only on Paper 3 (60 marks, HL only, ALL questions answered — two ~30-mark structured questions). Each Paper 3 question is one extended dataset with 7–8 short calculation sub-parts (mostly 2 marks, occasionally 4) climbing in difficulty — for example tariff revenue/deadweight loss calculations, quota rent, subsidy cost calculations, or exchange-rate arithmetic — and it is common for one sub-part to ask you to "sketch" or "draw" a named diagram, such as the export subsidy diagram covered earlier in this chapter. One sub-part near the end is typically a 4-mark "using a [named] diagram, explain..." question, and the final sub-part is a single 10-mark question framed as "Using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to [address/reduce] [specific problem]" — note this asks you to recommend a policy, not "evaluate" or "discuss", though a top answer still justifies the recommendation and notes at least one limitation, often by citing your own calculated DWL or government cost figure as evidence (a small DWL relative to government revenue suggests the policy is relatively efficient at achieving its stated aim; a very large one suggests the opposite). The same content can still appear in disguised form in Paper 1 and Paper 2 essays and data-response questions, using the AO2/AO3 command terms only, without the HL-only calculation.

Forward — New Tariff Scenario
World price = $10; tariff raises price to $13. Qs: 40→55. Qd: 120→100.
CS loss = 0.5 × (120+100) × 3 = $330
PS gain = 0.5 × (40+55) × 3 = $142.50

Reverse-Direction: Finding the Tariff Rate
World price = $8; Qs rises from 30 to 42 after a tariff; government revenue collected = $180; post-tariff Qd = 90. Find the tariff rate.
Quantity of imports after tariff = Qd − Qs = 90 − 42 = 48
Tariff rate = Govt revenue ÷ post-tariff imports = 180 ÷ 48 = $3.75 per unit
Given: govt revenue + post-tariff imports→ Tariff = Revenue ÷ Imports→ check units are $ per unit
Exam tips:
  1. Always define Qd₁/Qd₂ and Qs₁/Qs₂ explicitly (before vs after the policy) before substituting into any formula — mixing them up is the single most common HL error.
  2. For a subsidy question, check first whether the price consumers pay has changed at all — if not, there is no consumer surplus effect to calculate.
  3. Always give a cross-check for deadweight loss (via the two triangle areas) as well as the "CS loss − PS gain − govt revenue/rent" method — examiners sometimes ask for one specific method.
  4. For quota questions, state explicitly who captures the quota rent if the question gives you enough information to know (auctioned licences vs freely allocated licences).
