Absolute and Comparative Advantage

HL only
Absolute advantage
A country can produce more of a good than another country using the same quantity of resources.
Comparative advantage
A country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost (in terms of the other good forgone) than another country — regardless of who has the absolute advantage.
Opportunity cost (in this context)
The amount of one good that must be given up to produce one more unit of another good, given fixed resources.

Comparative advantage — not absolute advantage — is what determines whether specialisation and trade can benefit both countries. A country can gain from trade even if it is worse at producing everything, as long as it specialises in whichever good it is relatively least bad at.

Where the theory comes from: the two concepts have distinct historical origins, and IB examiners reward students who can place the theory in this context.

Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776): countries should specialise in goods where they hold an absolute advantage→ Problem: Smith's model cannot explain why a country with no absolute advantage in anything would ever trade→ David Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817): introduces comparative advantage via the England–Portugal cloth/wine example→ Ricardo shows Portugal (absolute advantage in both goods) still gains by specialising in wine and trading, since its opportunity cost of wine is lower than England's→ Comparative advantage supersedes absolute advantage as the theoretical justification for why all countries can gain from trade
Real-world context: Ricardo's original 1817 example used exactly two goods (cloth and wine) and two countries (England and Portugal) with labour as the only factor of production — a deliberate simplification. Twentieth-century economists Eli Heckscher and Bertil Ohlin later extended the idea into the Heckscher-Ohlin model, explaining comparative advantage through countries' relative factor endowments (land, labour, capital) rather than Ricardo's unexplained differences in labour productivity — the "sources of comparative advantage" studied in Section 2 below are essentially this later, richer explanation of what determines opportunity costs in the first place.
Worked Example 1 — Identifying Comparative Advantage
With one hour of labour, two countries can produce the following (maximum output of either good alone):
CountryCloth (units/hour)Wine (units/hour)
Country A105
Country B66
Opportunity cost of 1 unit of Cloth: Country A = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5 Wine; Country B = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0 Wine. Country A has the lower opportunity cost in Cloth → Country A has comparative advantage in Cloth.
Opportunity cost of 1 unit of Wine: Country A = 10 ÷ 5 = 2.0 Cloth; Country B = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0 Cloth. Country B has the lower opportunity cost in Wine → Country B has comparative advantage in Wine.
Country A also happens to have the absolute advantage in Cloth (10 > 6) while Country B has the absolute advantage in Wine (6 > 5) here — but as Worked Example 2 shows, comparative advantage can exist even without any absolute advantage.
Country A: max 50 Cars OR 100 Textiles. Opp. cost of 1 Car = 2 Textiles; opp. cost of 1 Textile = 0.5 Cars.
Country B: max 80 Cars OR 40 Textiles. Opp. cost of 1 Car = 0.5 Textiles; opp. cost of 1 Textile = 2 Cars.

The steeper the linear PPC, the higher the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis in terms of the good on the vertical axis. Here, Country A has a much flatter PPC (low opportunity cost of Textiles, its comparative advantage), while Country B's steeper PPC reflects its comparative advantage in Cars.

Real-world: Bangladesh has built a comparative advantage in garment manufacturing (a labour-intensive, low-capital process) despite not having an absolute advantage in manufacturing productivity generally — its low opportunity cost in garments relative to other possible uses of its labour and land is what drives specialisation and export success, not superior absolute output per worker.
Cross-reference: the SL file's section on free trade diagrams and the benefits of larger markets assumes exactly the specialisation this theory explains. Subtopic 4.2 (Types of trade protection) examines what happens when a government interferes with a pattern of trade that comparative advantage would otherwise generate, and 4.4 (Economic integration) looks at how trade blocs try to lock in and extend these comparative-advantage-driven gains across whole regions.

Gains from Trade and Sources of Comparative Advantage

HL only
Worked Example 2 — Gains from Trade Even Without Absolute Advantage
Country A can produce (per hour) 10 Cloth OR 8 Wine. Country B can produce 4 Cloth OR 5 Wine. Country A has the absolute advantage in both goods.
Opp. cost of Cloth: A = 8÷10 = 0.8 Wine; B = 5÷4 = 1.25 Wine → A has comparative advantage in Cloth.
Opp. cost of Wine: A = 10÷8 = 1.25 Cloth; B = 4÷5 = 0.8 Cloth → B has comparative advantage in Wine.
Even though Country A is better at producing everything, both countries still gain if A specialises fully in Cloth and B specialises fully in Wine, then trade — because A gives up less Wine to produce Cloth than B would, and B gives up less Cloth to produce Wine than A would.
Illustrating the gain: before specialisation, suppose each country split its time evenly: A produces 5 Cloth + 4 Wine; B produces 2 Cloth + 2.5 Wine. Combined: 7 Cloth + 6.5 Wine.
After full specialisation: A produces 10 Cloth (0 Wine); B produces 5 Wine (0 Cloth). Combined: 10 Cloth + 5 Wine — trading at a mutually beneficial rate lets both countries end up consuming more of both goods than they could before specialising.

Sources of comparative advantage — why does one country have a lower opportunity cost than another?

SourceExplanation
Factor endowmentsRelative abundance of land, labour, or capital shapes what a country can produce most cheaply (e.g. abundant cheap labour favours labour-intensive goods).
Climate and natural resourcesGeography and natural resource deposits give some countries a low opportunity cost in specific primary products.
Technology and human capitalSkills, education, and technological know-how can lower the opportunity cost of producing technologically sophisticated goods.
Infrastructure and institutionsReliable transport, energy, and legal institutions reduce the effective cost of producing and exporting certain goods.
Real-world: Vietnam's comparative advantage in electronics assembly and garment manufacturing reflects an abundant, relatively low-cost labour force combined with improving infrastructure and trade-linked institutions (e.g. free trade agreements), while its comparative advantage in rice and coffee production reflects favourable climate and land endowments.
Real-world — comparative advantage can shift over time: South Korea illustrates that a country's sources of comparative advantage are not fixed. In the 1960s, its comparative advantage lay in low-cost, labour-intensive goods (textiles, wigs, plywood) — abundant unskilled labour was its main factor endowment. Through the 1970s–80s, sustained investment in physical capital and infrastructure shifted its advantage into capital-intensive heavy industry (steel via POSCO, shipbuilding via Hyundai Heavy Industries). By the 1990s–2000s, decades of investment in education and R&D (Korea now spends roughly 4–5% of GDP on R&D, among the highest rates in the world) shifted its comparative advantage again, into technologically sophisticated goods — Samsung became the world's largest producer of memory chips and smartphones. This progression from labour-intensive, to capital-intensive, to technology/human-capital-intensive exports directly illustrates the "technology and human capital" and "factor endowments" rows of the table above changing over decades, not staying fixed as the basic theory implicitly assumes.

Limitations of the Theory of Comparative Advantage

HL only
LimitationExplanation
Assumes constant opportunity costsLinear PPCs assume opportunity cost never changes with output level — in reality, opportunity costs often rise as a country produces more of a good (increasing opportunity cost, a bowed-out PPC).
Ignores transport costsThe theory assumes trade is costless to conduct — in practice, shipping, tariffs, and logistics costs can erode or eliminate the theoretical gains.
Assumes perfect factor mobilitySpecialisation requires resources (especially labour) to move freely between industries — in reality, retraining costs, geography, and social ties can trap workers in declining industries.
Ignores externalitiesThe theory says nothing about environmental or social costs (e.g. specialising in polluting extractive industries) that may accompany specialisation.
Risk of over-specialisationHeavy reliance on one or two export goods (monoculture economies) leaves a country vulnerable to world price shocks and terms-of-trade deterioration.
Ignores non-price competitivenessComparative advantage focuses purely on cost/opportunity cost, but real trade patterns also depend on quality, branding, and reliability.
Static, not dynamicThe theory takes resource endowments and technology as fixed, but comparative advantage can shift over time as countries invest, industrialise, and develop new skills.
Real-world: Zambia's heavy reliance on copper exports (a classic case of specialising according to comparative advantage in mineral resources) has repeatedly exposed its economy to sharp swings in government revenue, export earnings and the exchange rate whenever world copper prices fall — illustrating the over-specialisation risk the pure theory does not account for. Copper typically accounts for over 70% of Zambia's export earnings; when the world copper price fell from around $7,000 per tonne in 2011 to under $4,500 per tonne in 2015, Zambia's kwacha depreciated sharply and government revenue and GDP growth both slowed markedly.
CountryCopper's share of exportsPolicy response to price volatilityOutcome
Zambia~70%+ of export earningsLimited fiscal buffers; heavy reliance on mining tax revenueSharp currency depreciation and revenue shortfalls when world copper prices fall (e.g. 2015)
Chile~50% of export earningsState-owned Codelco plus a sovereign wealth "Economic and Social Stabilisation Fund" (established 2007) saving copper windfalls during price boomsGreater ability to smooth government spending through copper price cycles, though still exposed to long-run terms-of-trade shifts
Exam tip: the strongest evaluative Paper 1 answers weigh the theoretical gains from comparative advantage against at least two of these real-world limitations, rather than treating specialisation-and-trade as an unambiguous win for every country in every circumstance. Contrasting Zambia and Chile — two countries with similar copper dependence but very different policy responses — is a strong way to show that the "over-specialisation" limitation is about policy management as much as about the theory itself. This links forward to 4.5 (Terms of trade), where the long-run price trends facing commodity exporters like these are analysed in full, and to 4.10 (Economic growth and/or economic development strategies), which examines diversification away from single-commodity dependence as a development strategy.

Calculating Export/Import Quantities and Values from a Diagram

HL only

The SL file introduced the free trade diagram showing imports or exports as the gap between Qs and Qd at the world price. At HL, you must be able to read actual numeric values off such a diagram and calculate the resulting import expenditure or export revenue.

At world price $10, domestic Qs = 50,000 units, domestic Qd = 150,000 units — imports fill the 100,000-unit gap.
Worked Example — Import Expenditure
From the diagram: world price = $10; Qs (domestic supply at WP) = 50,000 units; Qd (domestic demand at WP) = 150,000 units.
Quantity of imports = Qd − Qs = 150,000 − 50,000 = 100,000 units
Import expenditure = Quantity of imports × World price = 100,000 × $10 = $1,000,000
Reverse-Direction: Finding Qd from Import Expenditure
World price = $8; Qs = 40,000 units; import expenditure = $960,000. Find Qd.
Quantity of imports = 960,000 ÷ 8 = 120,000 units
Qd = Qs + imports = 40,000 + 120,000 = 160,000 units
Export Revenue = Quantity of Exports × World Price = (Qs − Qd) × World Price
Worked Example — Export Revenue
World price = $15; Qs at WP = 90,000 units; Qd at WP = 30,000 units.
Quantity of exports = Qs − Qd = 90,000 − 30,000 = 60,000 units
Export revenue = 60,000 × $15 = $900,000
Exam tip: always read Qs and Qd off the diagram at the world price line specifically — not at the domestic equilibrium price — since the whole point of the exercise is the gap that opens up once the country trades at the (different) world price.

On Paper 1, comparative advantage supplies the theoretical foundation (AO1/AO2) for essays on the benefits of trade and, combined with the limitations in Section 3, for extended AO3 evaluation of trade liberalisation versus protection.
Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula/method→ Work through step by step→ State the units→ Interpret the answer in context
Opportunity Cost of Good X = Max Output of Good Y ÷ Max Output of Good X
Forward — Identifying Comparative Advantage
Country P: 1 hour produces 12 Bikes or 6 Laptops. Country Q: 1 hour produces 9 Bikes or 9 Laptops.
Opp. cost of Bikes: P = 6÷12 = 0.5 Laptops; Q = 9÷9 = 1.0 Laptops → P has comparative advantage in Bikes.
Opp. cost of Laptops: P = 12÷6 = 2.0 Bikes; Q = 9÷9 = 1.0 Bikes → Q has comparative advantage in Laptops.
Reverse-Direction: Finding a Missing Output Level
Country R can produce 20 Bikes or an unknown number of Laptops per hour. Its opportunity cost of 1 Bike is known to be 0.75 Laptops. Find R's maximum Laptop output.
Opp. cost of Bike = Max Laptops ÷ Max Bikes → 0.75 = Max Laptops ÷ 20
Max Laptops = 0.75 × 20 = 15 Laptops
Import Expenditure = Quantity of Imports × World Price; Export Revenue = Quantity of Exports × World Price
Forward — Export Revenue from a Diagram
World price = $12; Qs at WP = 70,000 units; Qd at WP = 25,000 units.
Quantity of exports = 70,000 − 25,000 = 45,000 units
Export revenue = 45,000 × $12 = $540,000
Reverse-Direction: Finding Qs from Export Revenue
World price = $9; Qd = 20,000 units; export revenue = $450,000. Find Qs.
Quantity of exports = 450,000 ÷ 9 = 50,000 units
Qs = Qd + exports = 20,000 + 50,000 = 70,000 units

Exam tips:
  1. Never confuse absolute advantage (more output) with comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost) — a question asking which country "should" specialise in a good is always about opportunity cost, not raw output levels.
  2. When constructing a linear PPC from output data, plot the two intercepts exactly as given (max output of each good alone) and join them with a straight line — do not bow the curve.
  3. For export/import value calculations, always identify Qs and Qd at the world price line specifically, not at the domestic equilibrium.
  4. Evaluative essays on comparative advantage should always weigh the theoretical gains against at least one real-world limitation (transport costs, factor immobility, over-specialisation) for full marks.
  5. Use real-world contrasts (Bangladesh/Vietnam for comparative-advantage-without-absolute-advantage; Zambia for over-specialisation risk) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative points.
