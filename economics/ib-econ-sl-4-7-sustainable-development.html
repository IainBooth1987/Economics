The Meaning of Sustainable Development

Sustainable development
Development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
Intergenerational equity
Fairness between generations — ensuring that today's consumption of resources and environmental capacity does not leave future generations worse off.
Intragenerational equity
Fairness within the current generation — ensuring that the benefits of development and the burden of environmental costs are shared reasonably between richer and poorer people and countries alive today.

Sustainable development therefore asks two linked questions at once: can growth and development continue without running down the natural-resource base and environmental capacity that future generations will depend on (intergenerational equity), and are today's benefits and costs shared fairly between rich and poor now (intragenerational equity)? A country can raise GDP rapidly in the short run by over-exploiting fisheries, forests, soil, or fossil fuels — but if this degrades the natural capital that supports future output, it is growth that is not development in the sustainable sense.

Real-world: Chinese economist and former World Bank chief economist assessments of "green GDP" — an experimental adjustment to GDP that subtracts an estimated monetary cost of environmental damage and resource depletion — found that pollution and resource costs could subtract several percentage points from China's headline growth figures in the mid-2000s; the government shelved official green GDP publication in 2007 after provincial officials objected to how it affected their growth rankings, illustrating how politically difficult it can be to make sustainability, rather than raw output, the measure that matters.
Why this distinction matters for evaluation: examiners reward answers that separate growth (a rise in real GDP) from sustainable development (a rise in wellbeing that does not erode the resource base future generations need). A country can show strong growth statistics while depleting groundwater, forests or fish stocks — a classic Paper 1 evaluative point is that GDP growth is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sustainable development.
Fig: the Environmental Kuznets Curve — a hypothesis that environmental degradation rises with GDP per capita in the early stages of development, then falls once a country becomes wealthy enough to prioritise (and afford) cleaner technology and stronger regulation.
Real-world: economists have pointed to trends such as improving urban air quality in wealthier East Asian cities (e.g. measured reductions in particulate pollution in South Korea and Japan since the 1990s) as consistent with the falling side of an Environmental Kuznets Curve — but critics note the curve does not hold for all pollutants (notably carbon dioxide emissions, which have kept rising with income in many high-income countries) and that rich countries have partly "exported" pollution by importing manufactured goods rather than genuinely cleaning up production.

The History of Sustainable Development as a Concept

1987 — Brundtland Report / "Our Common Future"→ 1992 — Rio Earth Summit / Agenda 21→ 2000 — Millennium Development Goals (8 goals)→ 2015 — UN Sustainable Development Goals (17 goals)
Real-world: the term "sustainable development" was popularised by the 1987 report Our Common Future, produced by the UN World Commission on Environment and Development and chaired by Norwegian Prime Minister Gro Harlem Brundtland — hence its common name, the "Brundtland Report." It defined sustainable development as development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs," a definition still used in IB Economics today. Five years later, the 1992 UN Conference on Environment and Development — the Rio Earth Summit, held in Rio de Janeiro and attended by representatives of 172 governments — adopted Agenda 21, a detailed action plan for sustainable development covering everything from poverty to biodiversity, alongside the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. In 2000, the UN General Assembly adopted the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) — eight targets, including halving extreme poverty, to be achieved by 2015. When the MDG deadline arrived, the UN replaced them with a far broader successor: on 25 September 2015, all 193 UN member states adopted Resolution A/RES/70/1, "Transforming our World: the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development," setting out 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 169 associated targets, to be achieved by 2030.
Why this history matters for evaluation: the shift from the MDGs (8 goals, focused mainly on developing countries and poverty) to the SDGs (17 goals, universal — applying to all countries, including high-income ones, and covering environment, inequality, and institutions as well as poverty) reflects a broadening of what "development" is understood to mean. A strong answer can use this history to show sustainable development is a contested, evolving concept rather than a single fixed definition.

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
Seventeen universal goals, with 169 targets, adopted by all UN member states in 2015 as part of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, intended to end poverty, protect the environment, and ensure prosperity for all by 2030.
SDG (selected)Key TargetCountry Example
SDG 1 — No PovertyEnd extreme poverty for all people everywhere, currently measured as living below the international poverty line (US$2.15 per day at 2017 PPP, revised from $1.90) by 2030.Rwanda's Vision 2050 development strategy targets upper-middle-income status alongside continued poverty reduction, building on the earlier Vision 2020 programme.
SDG 6 — Clean Water and SanitationAchieve universal, equitable access to safe and affordable drinking water for all by 2030 (Target 6.1).India's Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in 2019, aims to provide piped tap water to every rural household — reporting several hundred million additional rural taps connected within its first few years.
SDG 7 — Affordable and Clean EnergyEnsure universal access to affordable, reliable, and modern energy services by 2030 (Target 7.1).India's Saubhagya rural electrification scheme (launched 2017) raised household electricity access from around 82% (2015) to near-universal levels by 2019.
SDG 13 — Climate ActionStrengthen resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards, and integrate climate measures into national policy (Targets 13.1–13.2).Kenya generates the large majority of its grid electricity from geothermal, hydro, and wind sources (notably the Olkaria geothermal complex and the Lake Turkana Wind Power project), reducing reliance on imported fossil fuels.
SDG 15 — Life on LandHalt deforestation and restore degraded forests and land by 2020/2030 (Target 15.2).Brazil's national space agency (INPE) satellite monitoring recorded Amazon deforestation falling to multi-decade lows around 2012 before rising again through the late 2010s — a widely cited example of how enforcement and political priorities drive progress on this goal.
Exam tip: you are not expected to memorise all 17 SDGs and 169 targets by number. What examiners reward is being able to explain, with a named country example, how a specific SDG connects economic activity to an environmental or social outcome — and to discuss factors that help or hinder progress towards it.
Real-world — SDG progress reporting: the UN's annual Sustainable Development Goals Report and the independent Sustainable Development Report (published by the SDSN, the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network) both track country-by-country progress using a "dashboard" of colour-coded indicators. The 2023 UN mid-point assessment of the 2030 Agenda found only a minority of SDG targets were on track globally, with progress on poverty and hunger reduction actually reversed in some regions by the combined effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, conflict, and climate-related shocks — illustrating that SDG progress is neither automatic nor irreversible.

Comparing SDG Progress Across Countries

Paper 2 data-response questions on this topic often supply an SDG indicator table for a single named country and ask candidates to discuss its progress towards specific goals. The table below illustrates the kind of comparison a strong answer should be able to draw across countries at different income levels.

CountrySDG 1 (Poverty)SDG 7 (Energy Access)SDG 13 (Climate)
IndiaExtreme poverty (World Bank international line) fell from roughly 22% (2011) to under 10% (2019) — a large absolute reduction given India's population size, though thresholds just above the poverty line remain vulnerable to shocks.Household electricity access rose to near-universal (~99%) by 2019, from around 82% in 2015, via the Saubhagya scheme.India has pledged to reach 500 GW of non-fossil-fuel electricity capacity by 2030 as part of its Nationally Determined Contribution, while remaining the world's third-largest emitter in absolute terms due to continued coal use.
BangladeshExtreme poverty fell from around 19% (2010) to roughly 5% (2022) on World Bank estimates, alongside rapid growth in the ready-made garment export sector.Grid electricity access rose from roughly 60% (2010) to over 95% by the early 2020s, partly through off-grid solar home systems reaching remote rural areas.Bangladesh is ranked among the countries most physically exposed to climate-related flooding and cyclone risk, despite contributing a small fraction of global emissions — a frequently cited case of climate vulnerability being unevenly distributed relative to responsibility.

Why this table matters for evaluation: the comparison shows that SDG progress is uneven both across goals within one country (India's strong SDG 7 progress alongside continued SDG 13 emissions challenges) and across countries at similar income levels (Bangladesh's faster poverty reduction than Kenya's). A top-band Paper 2 answer uses exactly this kind of cross-goal, cross-country nuance rather than treating "SDG progress" as a single uniform score.
Cross-references: compare these poverty and inequality figures with 3.4 (measures of inequality, e.g. the Gini coefficient); use 4.8 (measuring development) to interpret HDI and related composite indicators alongside SDG data; see 4.9 (barriers to growth and development) for underlying causes of the gaps shown above; and see 4.10 (growth and development strategies) for policies aimed at closing them.

Exam Technique

How This Topic Is Assessed: Command Terms and Paper Structure

Assessment ObjectiveWhat It TestsCommand Terms Used on This Topic
AO1 — KnowledgeStating and describing content accuratelydefine (sustainable development, intergenerational equity, SDGs), describe, outline, state
AO2 — Application/AnalysisExplaining mechanisms and applying theory to a named country/SDGexplain (how a named policy contributes towards a specific SDG), analyse (the relationship between growth and environmental degradation), distinguish (growth from sustainable development)
AO3 — Synthesis/EvaluationWeighing competing views and judging significanceevaluate, discuss, "to what extent" (can sustainable development be achieved without addressing poverty?), compare and contrast (SDG progress across two named countries)
AO4 — SkillsInterpreting datainterpret SDG indicator data/tables, describe trends shown in a data extract
Where this shows up on the papers: on Paper 1, this topic typically supports a part (a) ~10-mark "explain" sub-question (e.g. explain why sustainable development requires balancing economic, social, and environmental goals) alongside a part (b) ~15-mark "evaluate"/"discuss" essay — "to what extent can sustainable development be achieved without addressing poverty?" is a natural essay angle here, since it forces candidates to weigh the SL sustainable-development content against the HL poverty-sustainability relationship. Paper 2 (40 marks, answer one of two questions, built from two linked Text extracts with numbered paragraphs plus data tables, every sub-question citing an exact paragraph or table reference) commonly supplies an actual SDG-progress data table for a named country: expect two 2-mark "define the term..." sub-parts — (a)(i) and (a)(ii) — then a short calculation or sketch sub-part, a run of 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each anchored to a specific paragraph or table, and a single 15-mark question often asking candidates to "discuss the impact of [some named policy or shock] on the country's ability to achieve two of its Sustainable Development Goals" — precisely the skill practised in the SDG comparison table in Section 4 above. Paper 3 is lighter on this subtopic specifically: it is more likely to draw on sustainable development for context or discussion within a broader HL country-profile data set (e.g. a data set that happens to include SDG indicators alongside GDP, inflation, or trade figures) than to examine it as its own dedicated calculation type.
Exam tips:
  1. Always distinguish clearly between growth (rising real GDP) and sustainable development (development that does not compromise future generations' ability to meet their needs) — do not treat the two as synonyms.
  2. When a Paper 2 extract gives you SDG data for a named country, engage with the specific numbers given (e.g. a named poverty rate or electricity-access percentage) rather than writing generically about "helping the country develop."
  3. Use specific, named examples to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays — the Brundtland Report (1987) and Rio Earth Summit (1992) for the historical concept; India's Jal Jeevan Mission/Saubhagya scheme, Kenya's geothermal energy mix, or Brazil's Amazon deforestation data for named SDG progress or setbacks.
  4. Remember 4.10 (growth and development strategies) is where you discuss policies to achieve sustainable development in depth — in 4.7, focus on defining the concept and evidencing SDG progress, rather than writing a full policy-evaluation essay.
