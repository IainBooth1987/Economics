Credit and Debit Items; Surplus or Deficit

Balance of payments (BOP)
A record of all economic transactions between the residents of one country and the rest of the world over a given time period, usually a year or a quarter.
Credit item
A transaction that brings an inward flow of money (foreign currency) into the country — for example, exporting a good, or a foreigner investing in a domestic firm.
Debit item
A transaction that involves an outward flow of money from the country — for example, importing a good, or a domestic resident buying a foreign asset.
Credit item (+)Debit item (–)
Exporting goods or services to another countryImporting goods or services from another country
Receiving investment income (interest, dividends, profits) from abroadPaying investment income to foreign owners of domestic assets
A foreign firm investing in a domestic business (inward FDI)A domestic firm investing in a foreign business (outward FDI)
Receiving remittances or aid from abroadSending remittances or aid overseas
Surplus
Credits exceed debits on a given account over the period — more money flows in than out.
Deficit
Debits exceed credits on a given account over the period — more money flows out than in.
Real-world: the United States recorded a current account deficit of roughly $818.8 billion in 2023 (around 3.0% of GDP), according to the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) — debit items such as imported consumer goods, imported oil, and income paid to foreign holders of US assets exceeded credit items such as exported goods, services, and income received from abroad.
Real-world: by contrast, Germany recorded a current account surplus of around €248 billion in 2023 (roughly 6.1% of GDP), according to the Deutsche Bundesbank — reflecting decades of strong manufactured-goods exports (machinery, vehicles, chemicals) exceeding its import bill.
Link to 4.5: the balance of payments and the exchange rate are closely linked. Credit items (like exports) create foreign demand for the domestic currency; debit items (like imports) create domestic demand for foreign currency. HL students study this relationship — and what a persistent imbalance implies for the exchange rate — in depth in the HL extension file.

Components of the Balance of Payments

The balance of payments is divided into three main accounts: the current account, the capital account, and the financial account.

The Current Account

Sub-componentWhat it records
Balance of trade in goodsExports minus imports of physical, tangible goods (e.g. cars, oil, foodstuffs).
Balance of trade in servicesExports minus imports of intangible services (e.g. tourism, banking, insurance, education, shipping).
IncomeNet flows of investment income — wages, interest, dividends, and profits — earned by residents abroad minus income earned by foreigners domestically.
Current transfersOne-way flows with no good or service exchanged in return, e.g. worker remittances, foreign aid grants, and pension payments across borders.

The Capital Account

Sub-componentWhat it records
Capital transfersOne-off, non-recurring transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness by another government, or transfers of assets by migrants moving country.
Transactions in non-produced, non-financial assetsSales/purchases of intangible assets not produced through the production process, e.g. patents, trademarks, copyrights, and land purchased by foreign embassies.
Careful: the capital account is usually the smallest of the three by value, and it is easy to confuse with the financial account — remember that the capital account records transfers and non-produced assets, while the financial account (below) records the far larger flows of actual investment.

The Financial Account

Sub-componentWhat it records
Foreign direct investment (FDI)Cross-border investment establishing a lasting management interest in a foreign business (e.g. building a factory abroad, or acquiring a controlling stake).
Portfolio investmentCross-border purchases of financial assets — shares and bonds — that do not give a controlling interest.
Reserve assetsForeign currency, gold, and other reserve assets held and managed by the central bank/monetary authority.
Official borrowingLoans between governments, or borrowing from institutions such as the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

Illustrative example: a current account deficit (debit side, red) is offset by a financial account surplus and small capital account surplus (credit side, blue/green) — net inflows on the financial account finance the net outflow on the current account, so the three accounts sum to zero.

Why this matters for evaluation: some countries run current account deficits or surpluses for decades rather than a single year — a strong answer identifies whether an imbalance is persistent (structural) or a one-off (e.g. a single bad harvest), since the appropriate policy response and level of concern differ. China's Persistent Surplus and Reserve Accumulation

1994 — China unifies its exchange rate, pursues export-led growth→ 2001 — WTO accession accelerates export growth→ 2000s — the People's Bank of China (PBOC) accumulates foreign exchange reserves→ 2014 — reserves peak near $4 trillion
Real-world: from the mid-1990s, China ran a large and persistent current account surplus fuelled by export-oriented manufacturing. Rather than allowing this surplus to push the renminbi's value up sharply, the People's Bank of China bought large quantities of foreign currency (chiefly US dollars), building foreign exchange reserves that peaked at close to $4 trillion in 2014 — among the largest reserve stockpiles ever accumulated by a single country. Economists have labelled this pattern of export-led growth financed by reserve accumulation "Bretton Woods II," after the original post-war fixed exchange rate system. China's surplus has since narrowed from its mid-2000s peak (above 8% of GDP) toward roughly 1–2% of GDP today, as domestic consumption has grown as a share of the economy.

Interdependence Between the Accounts

Zero balance
By the accounting logic of the balance of payments, the current account, capital account, and financial account must sum to zero (in principle) — every credit entry must be matched by an equal and opposite debit entry elsewhere in the accounts.
Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account (+ Net Errors & Omissions) = 0

This follows from double-entry bookkeeping: every international transaction is recorded twice. If a UK firm exports £10 million of goods (a current account credit), the payment received must show up somewhere else in the accounts — e.g. as a financial account debit if the exporter deposits the foreign currency it receives into a foreign bank account, or the foreign buyer might finance the purchase by selling assets, which shows up as a financial account credit from the exporting country's perspective.

Credits are always matched by debits: a deficit on one account (say, the current account) must be matched by an offsetting surplus somewhere else in the overall balance of payments (typically the financial account) — this is why "financing the deficit" is such a common phrase: a current account deficit is, by definition, financed by net inflows on the capital and financial accounts.
Real-world: the UK's persistent current account deficit (around –3.0% of GDP in 2023, per the ONS) has for decades been financed largely through the financial account — substantial portfolio investment inflows (foreign purchases of UK government bonds and shares) and foreign direct investment into UK businesses and property. This is a direct real-world illustration of the two accounts moving in offsetting directions to keep the overall balance of payments at zero.
Net errors and omissions: in practice, because current account, capital account, and financial account data are collected from different sources (customs records, bank surveys, investment registries), the recorded figures rarely balance exactly. Statistical agencies such as the ONS or the US BEA insert a "net errors and omissions" balancing item to reconcile measurement gaps and ensure the accounts sum to zero — a reminder that BOP figures are estimates built from imperfect data, not an exact ledger.

Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units ($/£ or %)→ Interpret the answer in context
Current Account Balance = Balance of Trade in Goods + Balance of Trade in Services + Net Income + Net Current Transfers
Forward — Calculating the Current Account Balance
A country's data: balance of trade in goods = –$40bn; balance of trade in services = +$18bn; net income = +$6bn; net current transfers = –$4bn.
Current account balance = (–40) + 18 + 6 + (–4) = –$20 billion (a current account deficit).
Reverse-Direction: Finding a Missing Component
A country's current account balance is –$15bn. Balance of trade in goods = –$30bn, balance of trade in services = +$12bn, net current transfers = –$1bn. Find net income.
–15 = (–30) + 12 + Net Income + (–1) → –15 = –19 + Net Income → Net Income = –15 – (–19) = +$4 billion.
Given: overall balance + known components→ Missing component = overall balance − known components→ check the sign and units
Exam tips:
  1. Always double-check whether a transaction is a credit or debit by asking: does money flow into the country (credit) or out of it (debit)? This is the most common source of AO2 marking errors.
  2. Do not confuse the capital account with the financial account — the capital account is small (transfers, non-produced assets); the financial account (FDI, portfolio investment, reserves, official borrowing) is where the large offsetting flows usually appear.
  3. Use specific real-world examples (Germany/China for persistent surpluses; the UK/US for persistent deficits) to anchor Paper 1 and Paper 2 evaluative answers.
