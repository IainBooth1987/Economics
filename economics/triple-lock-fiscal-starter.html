The Triple Lock Starter: What does it cost?

On 29 September 2026 the Prime Minister announced the state pension triple lock will change from April 2030. Use the data to work out what it has cost the public finances, and what the change might save.

A-Level EconomicsFiscal policy & public finances20–25 minutesCalculation practice

1The news

What happened?

29 September 2026, Labour conference, Liverpool. In his first conference speech as Prime Minister, Andy Burnham announced that the triple lock will stay in place until April 2030, then be replaced.

From 2030 the state pension will rise each year by the higher of CPI inflation or 2.5%, with a promise that it will still hold its value relative to earnings over time.

The government says the change could save around £15 billion a year by the end of the 2030s, to help pay for a National Care Service.

The three locks, and which one goes

Every April the state pension rises by whichever is highest of:

Average earnings growth
(May–July, previous year) CPI inflation
(September, previous year) 2.5%
(the floor)
Tell me why

Why do economists call the triple lock a ratchet? Tell me why.

Each year the pension jumps to the highest of three measures and never falls back. Over time it rises faster than any single measure: in a year when inflation spikes it follows prices; the next year when wages catch up it follows earnings. Each rise then becomes the base for the next one, so the gains compound.

2. The full new state pension is paid weekly. Calculate its annual value in 2026/27 (52 weeks), and the increase in annual value if the provisional 2027 figure of £250.71 is confirmed.

Clue: Annual value = weekly amount × 52. Then subtract the 2026/27 annual figure from the 2027/28 one.

2026/27: £241.30 × 52 = £12,547.60

2027/28: £250.71 × 52 = £13,036.92

Increase = £489.32 a year per full pensioner.

3. In April 2024 the pension rose by 8.5% while CPI inflation was 6.7%. Calculate the real increase in the state pension in 2024.

Clue: Real change ≈ nominal change − inflation. For the precise answer: (1 + nominal rise) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, then × 100. Write 8.5% as 1.085.

Approximate: 8.5 − 6.7 = 1.8 percentage points

Precise: (1.085 ÷ 1.067 − 1) × 100 = 1.7%

CalculateTable 1

4. Calculate the percentage increase in the full new state pension between 2022 (£185.15) and 2026 (£241.30). Then use the CPI and earnings columns for 2023–2026 to calculate how much prices and earnings rose over the same period.

Clue: % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. For prices and earnings, turn each % into a multiplier (10.1% → 1.101), multiply the four together, then subtract 1.

Pension: (241.30 − 185.15) ÷ 185.15 × 100 = 30.3%

Prices: 1.101 × 1.067 × 1.017 × 1.038 = 1.240 → 24.0%

Earnings: 1.055 × 1.085 × 1.041 × 1.048 = 1.249 → 24.9%

The pension beat both by more than 5 percentage points: this is the ratchet in action.

5. Suppose the 2030 rule (higher of CPI or 2.5%) had applied from April 2024 instead. Starting from £203.85, calculate the full new state pension for 2026/27. How much less would a full pensioner get per week and per year?

Clue: Each year, pick the higher of CPI or 2.5% from Table 1. Turn it into a multiplier and multiply last year's pension. Compare your 2026 answer with £241.30, then × 52 for the yearly gap.

2024: CPI 6.7% → £203.85 × 1.067 = £217.51

2025: CPI 1.7%, so floor 2.5% → £217.51 × 1.025 = £222.95

2026: CPI 3.8% → £222.95 × 1.038 = £231.42

Actual: £241.30. Difference ≈ £9.88 a week ≈ £514 a year per full pensioner.

7. (a) The OBR says £15.5bn is about 0.5% of GDP. Use this to estimate the size of UK GDP.
(b) State pension spending is forecast to rise from 5% to 7.7% of GDP. Calculate the rise in percentage points and as a percentage change.
(c) What percentage of that rise is due to the triple lock (1.6 pp)?

Clue: (a) If 0.5% of GDP = £15.5bn, what is 1%? Then 100%? (b) Percentage points = new − old. Percentage change = that difference ÷ old × 100. (c) part ÷ whole × 100.

(a) £15.5bn ÷ 0.005 = £3,100bn (£3.1 trillion)

(b) 7.7 − 5 = 2.7 percentage points; 2.7 ÷ 5 × 100 = 54% increase

(c) 1.6 ÷ 2.7 × 100 = 59%: the triple lock causes more of the rise than the ageing population.

8. The government has chosen to honour its manifesto pledge and keep the triple lock until April 2030. What do you believe the impact of this will be on the government's fiscal position?

Clue: Think about spending, borrowing and debt now (to 2030) versus later (the 2030s). Use your answers to Q5, Q6 and Q7. Then judge: how big is the cost relative to GDP, what does it depend on (wages vs prices), and what would breaking the pledge have cost?

Short run (to 2030): the fiscal position worsens. Keeping the lock adds about £15.5bn a year by 2029-30 compared with an earnings link and £22.9bn compared with CPI only (Table 2). Higher current spending means a larger deficit, more borrowing and higher debt interest, which makes the fiscal rules harder to meet and leaves less room for other spending (or forces tax rises elsewhere). None of the £15bn saving arrives in this parliament.

Long run: the position improves. From 2030 the ratchet is broken. Pension spending would grow with CPI (or 2.5%) rather than the highest of three, so the 1.6 pp of GDP the OBR attributes to the triple lock (59% of the projected rise, Q7c) is largely avoided. A credible plan to slow spending growth may also reassure the OBR and gilt markets, keeping borrowing costs lower.

Evaluation:

  • Scale: 0.5% of a £3.1 trillion economy is significant but not a crisis on its own.
  • It depends on wages vs prices: the cost only arises when earnings growth beats CPI. If wage growth slows towards CPI before 2030, the extra cost of keeping the earnings leg is small. But forecasts are unreliable: the OBR's estimate overshot by 198% (Q6).
  • Some money comes back: pensioners have a high marginal propensity to consume, so spending generates VAT and multiplier effects; and with the personal allowance frozen, more of the state pension is taxed (fiscal drag; by 2027/28 the full pension of £13,036.92 exceeds £12,570).
  • Ageing still adds 1.1 pp of GDP, so the long-run pressure on the public finances does not go away.
  • Credibility: breaking a manifesto pledge immediately would have saved money sooner, but at a political cost and with the risk of reversal. A pre-announced change is more likely to stick.

Judgement: keeping the pledge worsens the fiscal position in the short run, but setting a date for reform improves long-run sustainability. The overall impact depends on whether the 2030 change survives the next election.
