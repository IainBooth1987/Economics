Part 1

Aggregate Demand (AD)

What is it?

Aggregate demand = total planned spending

Aggregate demand is the total planned spending on an economy’s goods and services at a given price level, in a given time period.

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

👆 Tap a component to see real-world examples.

Examiner tip

Benefits and pensions are transfer payments, not part of G — no output is produced in return. They only affect AD indirectly, when recipients spend them (C).
The slope

Why does AD slope downward?

A fall in the price level increases the quantity of real output demanded. Three effects explain this:

1. Real wealth effect

A lower price level raises the real value (purchasing power) of households’ savings and wealth, so they feel better off and consumption (C) rises.

2. Interest rate effect

At a lower price level, less money is needed for transactions, so demand for money falls and interest rates tend to fall — encouraging consumption (C) and investment (I).

3. International trade effect

If UK prices fall relative to other countries, UK exports become more competitive and imports relatively dearer — exports (X) rise and imports (M) fall.

The golden rule

A change in the price level causes a movement along AD. A change in anything else that affects C, I, G, X or M causes a shift of AD.

The multiplier

Why an injection has a bigger final effect

When government spending rises by £10bn, that money becomes someone’s income. They spend part of it (their marginal propensity to consume) and the rest leaks out as saving, taxes and imports (withdrawals). Each round of spending creates further income — so the final increase in real GDP is larger than the initial injection.

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − marginal propensity to consume)

Equivalently, 1 ÷ marginal propensity to withdraw. The bigger the leakages, the smaller the multiplier.

Part 2

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

What is it?

SRAS: what firms will supply when costs are fixed

Short-run aggregate supply shows the total planned real output that firms are willing to supply at each price level, in a period when the prices of factors of production — especially money wages — are fixed or slow to change.

Why does SRAS slope upward?

  • Profitability: wages and many input prices are set by contracts in the short run. If the price level rises while costs stay put, producing becomes more profitable, so firms expand output.
  • Rising costs at higher output: to produce more, firms pay overtime, use less efficient machinery and run into diminishing returns — so they need a higher price level to justify extra output.

What shifts SRAS? Costs of production

  • Money wage rates (relative to productivity)
  • Raw material and energy prices (oil, gas, commodities)
  • Import prices — via the exchange rate
  • Indirect taxes (e.g. VAT) and producer subsidies
  • Short-run changes in productivity
  • Regulation and compliance costs

Golden rule again

A change in the price level = movement along SRAS. A change in costs of production = shift of SRAS. Costs up → SRAS shifts left; costs down → SRAS shifts right.

Part 3

Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

What is it?

LRAS = the economy’s productive potential

Long-run aggregate supply shows the maximum real output an economy can produce when all its factors of production are fully and efficiently employed — its productive potential, or full-employment output (Yfe).

Link it to the PPF

An outward shift of the production possibility frontier and a rightward shift of LRAS show the same thing: the economy’s capacity to produce has grown. Both are long-run economic growth.

What shifts LRAS?

  • Quantity of factors: labour force size (migration, retirement age, participation), the capital stock, natural resources
  • Quality of factors: education, training, health
  • Productivity and technology: innovation, AI, better management
  • Institutions and incentives: property rights, competition, tax incentives
  • Supply-side policies: market-based and interventionist
Two views

Classical vs Keynesian LRAS

Classical (free-market) view — vertical

In the long run, wages and prices are fully flexible, so markets clear and the economy returns to full employment. Output is fixed by the quantity and quality of factors of production, not by the price level. AD only affects the price level in the long run.

Keynesian view — reverse L-shape

  • Horizontal section: deep recession, lots of spare capacity — output can rise without pushing up costs.
  • Upward section: bottlenecks appear as the economy nears capacity — scarce skilled labour and materials push costs up.
  • Vertical section: full capacity — more AD only raises the price level.

Part 4

The Full AD/AS Model

Equilibrium and output gaps

Putting AD, SRAS and LRAS together

Short-run equilibrium

Where AD = SRAS. This sets the actual price level and actual real GDP.

Long-run equilibrium

Where AD = SRAS = LRAS. Actual output equals potential output — no output gap.

Output gap

The difference between actual real GDP and potential real GDP, usually expressed as a percentage of potential.

Negative output gap

Actual < potential. Spare capacity, cyclical unemployment, weak wage growth and downward pressure on inflation.

Positive output gap

Actual > potential. The economy is "overheating": labour shortages, overtime, rising wages and demand-pull inflation.
