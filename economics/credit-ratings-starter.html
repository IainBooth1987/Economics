The Credit Ratings Starter: Can the UK keep its AA?

1 The news

What happened?

30 October 2024, Rachel Reeves's first Budget. The new Chancellor rewrote the UK's fiscal rules. Day-to-day spending must be paid for by tax, but the government may borrow to invest. She also changed the way debt is measured, from public sector net debt (PSND) to public sector net financial liabilities (PSNFL), which subtracts financial assets the state owns, such as student loans owed to it. The IFS estimated the switch would have created about £53 billion of extra room under the debt rule.

The market reaction. Gilt yields rose when she confirmed the change on 24 October, fell during her speech, then jumped once the OBR showed borrowing would be about £28 billion a year higher than planned. The 10-year yield hit 4.57% the next day, its highest since August 2023. Gilts sold off, but this was no repeat of the 2022 mini-budget crisis.

November 2025. Reeves tightened the framework further: the OBR now judges the rules once a year, at the Budget, and from 2026-27 the target is always the third year of the forecast.

2026: the rules are tested. On 20 July, new Prime Minister Andy Burnham said he would keep the rules but "use any flexibility within them". The 30-year yield jumped 0.1 percentage points. On 28 October, Chancellor John Healey's Budget kept Reeves's rules in full, and yields edged down.

Reeves's two fiscal rules

1. The stability ruleThe current budget (tax minus day-to-day spending) must be in balance by the third year of the forecast. From 2026-27, "balance" means within ±0.5% of GDP. Borrowing for investment is allowed: this is a golden rule.
2. The investment rulePSNFL must be falling as a share of GDP by the target year.
Old measure: PSND excluding the Bank of England. The new measure counts the government's financial assets as well as its debts.

Why did she change them?

Protect investment. Under the old debt rule, cutting capital spending was the easiest way to hit the target, even when the projects would pay off.
A truer balance sheet. Lending to students or buying a stake in a firm creates an asset. PSNFL counts that asset, not just the borrowing.
Credibility after 2022. Fixed rules, a "fiscal lock" requiring the OBR to check every major fiscal event, and one judgement a year all aim to reassure markets.
Tell me why

A government that sticks to its fiscal rules can usually borrow more cheaply. Tell me why.

Lenders buy gilts only if they are confident they will be repaid in full and that inflation will not erode the value of their money. Credible rules lower the risk premium they demand, so yields and debt interest stay lower. The rating agencies say this outright: Moody's said in November 2025 that it expected measures "consistent with the government-defined fiscal rules" when it affirmed the UK's Aa3 rating.

Tell me why

A credit rating downgrade can become self-fulfilling. Tell me why.

A downgrade makes gilts look riskier, so investors demand a higher yield. As old debt is refinanced, debt interest rises, which widens the deficit and adds to debt. That weakens the public finances, which is exactly what agencies look at, so the risk of another downgrade grows. With £109bn already going on interest, each rise in yields is costly (£16bn per percentage point).

5. In 2026-27, PSND is 96% of GDP but PSNFL is only 83%. Using GDP of £3.1 trillion, calculate the value of the financial assets that PSNFL subtracts. Why does this make the investment rule easier to meet?

12. The IFS estimates that a permanent rise in public investment of 1% of GDP raises potential output by 0.4% after 5 years and 2.4% after 50 years. Reeves's plans keep investment about 0.9% of GDP higher (Q8).
(a) Estimate the boost to output after 5 years and after 50 years, as a percentage and in £bn.  (b) Compare these with the £32.3bn a year of extra borrowing. What does this suggest?

(a) 5 years: 0.9 × 0.4 = 0.36% → 0.0036 × £3,100bn ≈ £11.2bn of extra GDP a year

(b) The payoff is small at first and well below the extra borrowing, but it grows over decades. Borrowing to invest only "pays for itself" if the investment is sustained, spent well and markets stay patient. Rising yields could wipe out the gain first.

Tell me why

Critics say "debt is debt" and that switching to PSNFL just moved the goalposts. Tell me why, then give one counter-argument.

Critics: the government must still sell the same gilts and pay the same interest, whatever measure it targets. Assets such as student loans are hard to value and may never be fully repaid, so the rule is easier to game. And the new room was used straight away: £32.3bn a year of extra borrowing.

Counter: the old rule rewarded cutting investment (heading for 1.7% of GDP), even where projects would raise growth and future tax revenue. A loan or a capital project creates an asset, so a balance-sheet measure gives a truer picture.

Multiple choice

13. A rating agency downgrades the UK from AA to AA−. What is the most likely immediate effect?

  • AGilt prices rise and yields fall
  • BGilt prices fall and yields rise
  • CThe Bank of England must raise Bank Rate
  • DThe government is legally required to cut spending

Riskier debt means investors pay less for it, so its yield rises. Neither the Bank of England nor the government is forced to act.

Multiple choice

14. Reeves's stability rule requires that:

  • ATotal government borrowing is zero by the target year
  • BDay-to-day spending is paid for by revenue; borrowing is only for investment
  • CNet debt stays below 60% of GDP
  • DDebt interest stays below 3% of GDP

This is a "golden rule": the current budget must balance, but borrowing to invest is still allowed. A is wrong because total borrowing (£67bn in 2030-31) can stay positive.

Multiple choice

15. Why did switching from PSND to PSNFL create around £50bn of extra room?

  • AIt ignores debt held by the Bank of England
  • BIt subtracts financial assets, such as student loans owed to the state
  • CIt counts schools and hospitals as assets
  • DIt leaves debt interest out of the calculation

C describes public sector net worth, an even broader measure that Reeves chose not to use.

Multiple choice

16. On 30 October 2024, the 10-year yield fell during Reeves's speech but reached 4.57% the next day. What best explains the rise?

  • AThe UK was downgraded that day
  • BThe OBR showed much higher borrowing, and markets expected fewer interest rate cuts
  • CThe Bank of England raised Bank Rate
  • DThe fiscal rules were scrapped

More gilt supply (about £28bn a year) and a more inflationary budget meant the Bank was expected to cut rates more slowly, so investors demanded higher yields.

Multiple choice

17. Since Reeves's November 2025 Budget, how often does the OBR judge whether the fiscal rules are being met?

  • AEvery month
  • BTwice a year
  • COnce a year, at the Budget
  • DOnly before a general election

The aim was to stop the cycle of speculation and policy changes around every spring forecast. The March 2026 forecast therefore made no judgement on the rules.

Multiple choice

18. Burnham promised to keep the rules but "use any flexibility", and the 30-year yield rose 0.1 percentage points. Which idea best explains this?

  • ACrowding out of private investment
  • BFiscal credibility: lenders want a higher risk premium if discipline looks weaker
  • CA liquidity trap
  • DThe J-curve effect

Rules that are met "technically but not in spirit" are less believable. Bond investors ("bond vigilantes") respond by charging more to lend.
