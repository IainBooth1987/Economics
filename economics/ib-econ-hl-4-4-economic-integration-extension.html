Trade Creation HL ONLY

When a country joins a customs union or free trade area, it removes tariffs on imports from fellow members. If this causes domestic consumers to switch from a higher-cost domestic producer to a lower-cost producer inside the bloc, this is trade creation — production shifts to the lowest-cost source, so world resources are used more efficiently.

Trade creation: a net increase in economic welfare that occurs when a country joins a trading bloc and its consumption switches from a high-cost domestic producer to a low-cost producer within the bloc, replacing inefficient domestic production with more efficient bloc production.

Joining an FTA/customs union lowers the price paid from WP+T to WP-FT. Domestic production falls (Q1→Q2), consumption rises (Q3→Q4). Area A = producer surplus lost by inefficient domestic firms; Area C = former tariff revenue, now transferred to consumers as extra surplus; Triangles B and D = the net welfare gain from trade creation (production efficiency gain + consumption gain).

RegionWhat it represents
AProducer surplus lost by domestic firms as domestic output falls from Q1 to Q2
BNet welfare gain from production efficiency — resources reallocated to a lower-cost use
CConsumer surplus gained that was previously government tariff revenue (a transfer, not a net gain)
DNet welfare gain from consumption — consumers who could not afford the good at WP+T now can
Real-world example: when Costa Rica deepened tariff-free access with fellow Central American Common Market (CACM) members, some domestic assembly-line manufacturing was replaced by lower-cost Guatemalan and Salvadoran output — consistent with trade creation, since production shifted to a more efficient regional source rather than simply diverting trade away from an efficient outside supplier.

Trade Diversion HL ONLY

Trade diversion is the opposite risk. If a customs union's common external tariff makes imports from an efficient non-member more expensive than imports from a less efficient member, consumption switches to the higher-cost bloc producer purely because of the tariff structure, not because it is genuinely cheaper to produce. This is a net loss of global efficiency.

Trade diversion: a net decrease in economic welfare that occurs when a country joins a trading bloc and its consumption switches from a low-cost producer outside the bloc to a higher-cost producer within the bloc, because of the common external tariff — production shifts away from the world's most efficient source.

Before joining, imports come from the efficient non-member at WP₁. After joining a customs union with a common external tariff, imports must come tariff-free from the (less efficient) member at the higher CU price. Consumers lose surplus, some of which becomes producer surplus for the domestic/member producer, but Triangles B and D are a genuine deadweight welfare loss — trade diversion.

EffectExplanation
PS gainDomestic/member producers gain surplus as the price rises from WP₁ to the CU price
Consumer lossConsumers pay more for the same quantity range — a transfer, not solely a welfare loss
B + D (DWL)Deadweight welfare loss — resources are pulled away from the world's most efficient producer toward a less efficient one purely because of the tariff structure
Key HL evaluation point: whether a trading bloc raises or lowers overall welfare depends on whether trade creation outweighs trade diversion. Blocs with low common external tariffs and members with genuinely similar comparative advantages tend to be trade-creating; blocs that protect an inefficient member behind a high external tariff risk being trade-diverting.
Real-world example: economists have argued that some Mercosur external tariffs redirected Paraguayan and Uruguayan car-part sourcing away from cheaper Asian suppliers toward higher-cost Brazilian and Argentine producers — a textbook illustration of trade diversion, since the shift followed the common external tariff rather than a genuine efficiency advantage.
Trade creation/diversion in reverse — Brexit: the UK's departure from the EU customs union and single market (formal exit January 2020, transition ended January 2021 — see the SL file, section 3, for the sovereignty context) can be read as running trade-creation logic backwards. Firms that had switched to the lowest-cost EU supplier while the UK was inside the bloc's tariff-free zone faced new customs paperwork, rules-of-origin checks, and non-tariff barriers after exit, pushing some UK buyers back toward domestic or non-EU alternatives that are not necessarily the world's most efficient producers — a real-world reminder that the trade-creation welfare gain shown in the diagram above is not automatically permanent once a country leaves a bloc.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Monetary Union HL ONLY

A monetary union takes economic integration to its deepest level: members share a single currency and, typically, a single central monetary authority. This removes independent control of the exchange rate and interest rate for each member — a major structural trade-off that HL students must be able to evaluate.

Advantages

  • Elimination of exchange rate uncertainty between members — firms trading within the union no longer face exchange rate risk on cross-border contracts.
  • Lower transaction costs — no currency conversion costs on intra-union trade and travel, benefiting firms and consumers alike.
  • Price transparency — a single currency makes prices directly comparable across member countries, intensifying competition and potentially lowering prices.
  • Increased trade and investment — reduced currency risk and transaction costs can attract foreign direct investment and boost intra-union trade volumes.
  • Anti-inflationary discipline — a shared central bank focused on low inflation can import monetary credibility to members with historically weaker central banks.

Disadvantages

  • Loss of independent monetary policy — an individual member cannot set its own interest rate to suit its own business cycle position.
  • Loss of independent exchange rate policy — a member cannot devalue its currency to restore competitiveness after an asymmetric shock or a loss of competitiveness.
  • Asymmetric shock vulnerability — a shock affecting one member (e.g. a collapse in a key export) cannot be cushioned by that member's own monetary policy; the "one-size-fits-all" interest rate may be wrong for it.
  • Need for fiscal transfer mechanisms — without monetary policy as a shock absorber, members typically need fiscal transfers or labour mobility between members to adjust smoothly, which can be politically difficult to establish.
  • Costs of adjustment and loss of seigniorage — governments lose the revenue from issuing their own currency and bear conversion/administrative costs on joining.
Optimum Currency Area (OCA) theory: economists assess whether a group of countries is suited to a monetary union by asking whether they meet OCA criteria — similar business cycles, labour mobility between members, fiscal transfer mechanisms, and flexible wages/prices. The closer members are to these conditions, the lower the cost of giving up independent monetary policy. OCA theory originates with Canadian economist Robert Mundell, whose 1961 paper "A Theory of Optimum Currency Areas" first set out these criteria — work that later contributed to his 1999 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded the same year the euro launched.

Why this table matters for evaluation: neither real-world monetary union is a textbook-perfect OCA — the comparison shows that monetary unions are formed and sustained for political and historical reasons as much as for satisfying every economic criterion, which is exactly the kind of nuanced point that separates a top-band Paper 3 evaluation from a list of textbook criteria.
Real-world example — WAEMU/UEMOA: the West African Economic and Monetary Union (eight countries including Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, and Burkina Faso) shares the CFA franc and a common central bank (BCEAO). Its long-standing peg to the euro has delivered low, stable inflation for members with historically weak central banks — but critics argue it has also constrained members' ability to respond independently to country-specific shocks, such as terms-of-trade swings tied to cocoa or cotton exports.
Real-world example — the Eurozone: the 2010s Eurozone sovereign debt crisis is the standard illustration of asymmetric-shock vulnerability — Greece could not devalue its currency to restore competitiveness and instead faced years of internal devaluation (wage and price cuts) as the adjustment mechanism, while Germany's very different economic position meant the European Central Bank's single interest rate suited the two economies very differently.

The Euro's Formation: From Convergence Criteria to Crisis

1992 — Maastricht convergence criteria set→ 1999 — euro launched as an accounting currency (11 states)→ 2002 — physical notes and coins enter circulation→ 2009–2015 — sovereign debt crisis exposes non-OCA gaps
Real-world: the 1992 Maastricht Treaty set explicit "convergence criteria" (limits on inflation, government deficit and debt, and exchange-rate stability) that countries had to meet before adopting the euro — an attempt to engineer OCA-like conditions by treaty rather than wait for them to emerge naturally. The euro launched as an accounting currency for 11 member states on 1 January 1999, with Greece joining in 2001 and physical notes and coins entering circulation on 1 January 2002. When the 2008 global financial crisis hit, several members — most visibly Greece, but also Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus — turned out not to have met the underlying economic conditions OCA theory demands, despite having satisfied the Maastricht criteria on paper; the ensuing 2009–2015 sovereign debt crisis is now the standard case study of what happens when a monetary union is formed ahead of genuine economic convergence.
Real-world — the UK's "five economic tests": the United Kingdom never joined the euro. In 1997, Chancellor of the Exchequer Gordon Brown set out "five economic tests" the UK would have to pass before adopting the single currency — covering business-cycle convergence, flexibility, the effect on investment, the effect on the City of London's financial services industry, and the effect on growth, stability and employment. The Treasury's 2003 assessment concluded the UK had not passed enough of the tests, and the UK stayed outside the Eurozone altogether — retaining full control of its own interest rate and exchange rate, a decision that meant the Bank of England, not the European Central Bank, set UK monetary policy through both the 2008 financial crisis and the Eurozone debt crisis that followed.

Missing Words

When a country joins a customs union and switches its purchases from an inefficient domestic producer to an efficient bloc producer, this is called click. When it instead switches from an efficient non-member producer to a less efficient bloc producer because of the click, this is called click, which represents a net loss of click. Where this shows up on the papers: HL-only trade creation/diversion and monetary union content is examinable on Paper 1 (an AO3 evaluative essay, e.g. "to what extent does joining a customs union benefit a country's economy?", requiring you to weigh trade creation against trade diversion using named evidence) and on Paper 2 (anchored to an exact paragraph/table reference in the extract, a 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." question on bloc or monetary-union membership, potentially feeding into the paper's single 15-mark discuss/evaluate question). Paper 3 is HL-only and quantitative: each of its two ~30-mark questions is one extended data set with 7–8 short calculation sub-parts (mostly 2 marks, occasionally 4) climbing in difficulty, and this is where the numerical diagrams in this file are tested directly — typically as a 4-mark "using a trade creation/trade diversion diagram, explain..." sub-part partway through, expecting a forward calculation (given prices and quantities, find the welfare effect) or, less often, a reverse-direction one (given a welfare-effect total, find a missing price or quantity), exactly like the worked examples below. The paper's final sub-part is usually a single 10-mark question of the form "using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [a named country] to address [a specific problem]" — note this asks you to recommend a policy, not evaluate or discuss, though top answers still justify the choice and note a limitation.

Paper 3 may ask you to calculate the net welfare effect of trade creation or trade diversion from a numeric diagram — always identify which quantity/price changes correspond to which triangle or rectangle first.

Net Welfare Effect of Trade Creation = Triangle B + Triangle D
(production efficiency gain + consumption gain)
Worked example — trade creation (forward):
Before joining a customs union, a country imports at WP+T = $12, with domestic Qs = 60 and Qd = 140.
After joining, the price falls to WP-FT = $9, with domestic Qs = 40 and Qd = 160.

Triangle B (production gain) = 0.5 × (60 − 40) × (12 − 9) = 0.5 × 20 × 3 = $30
Triangle D (consumption gain) = 0.5 × (160 − 140) × (12 − 9) = 0.5 × 20 × 3 = $30
Net welfare gain from trade creation = $30 + $30 = $60
Worked example — trade creation (reverse):
A country's net welfare gain from joining a customs union is calculated as $50, made up of two equal triangles (production gain = consumption gain). The price fell from $15 to $11 (a $4 fall).

Each triangle = $25, so: 0.5 × ΔQ × 4 = 25 → ΔQ = 25 ÷ 2 = 12.5 units (the change in domestic production, and separately the change in consumption, each equal 12.5 units).
Net Welfare Effect of Trade Diversion = − (Triangle B + Triangle D)
(deadweight loss from switching to a less efficient bloc producer)
Worked example — trade diversion (forward):
Before joining a customs union, a country imports from an efficient non-member at WP₁ = $8, with Qs = 30 and Qd = 170.
After joining, it must import tariff-free from a less efficient member at the CU price = $11, with Qs = 45 and Qd = 155.

Triangle B (production-side loss) = 0.5 × (45 − 30) × (11 − 8) = 0.5 × 15 × 3 = $22.50
Triangle D (consumption-side loss) = 0.5 × (170 − 155) × (11 − 8) = 0.5 × 15 × 3 = $22.50
Deadweight welfare loss from trade diversion = $22.50 + $22.50 = $45
Worked example — trade diversion (reverse):
A customs union's trade diversion creates a total deadweight loss of $36, split equally between the two triangles ($18 each). The CU price is $2 above the efficient non-member price WP₁.

0.5 × ΔQ × 2 = 18 → ΔQ = 18 ÷ 1 = 18 units (the combined change in quantity supplied/demanded that each triangle spans).
Exam tips:
  • Always state clearly whether a scenario describes trade creation (switch to a more efficient bloc producer) or trade diversion (switch to a less efficient bloc producer) before calculating — the direction of the price change relative to the world price outside the bloc is the giveaway.
  • In a monetary union evaluation essay, always link your advantage/disadvantage points back to the specific member countries named in the question — a generic "efficiency vs sovereignty" answer scores lower than one that engages with whether the named countries plausibly form an optimum currency area.
  • Remember that trade creation and diversion can occur simultaneously within the same customs union for different product markets — the overall welfare verdict requires weighing both effects together, not picking just one.
