Sustainability and Poverty: The Vicious Cycle HL ONLY

Poverty and environmental unsustainability are not separate problems — they frequently reinforce each other in a vicious cycle. People living in poverty often have no affordable alternative but to exploit natural resources unsustainably simply to survive day to day, even when they know this will reduce the resource's future value. That environmental degradation then lowers the very productivity (of land, water, and fisheries) that the poor depend on, deepening the poverty that caused the degradation in the first place.

The poverty–environment nexus: the two-way relationship in which poverty drives short-term, unsustainable exploitation of natural resources (e.g. deforestation for fuel, overfishing, soil exhaustion from unsustainable farming), while the resulting environmental degradation reduces the future income and resilience of the poor, reinforcing poverty.

Fig: the poverty–environment vicious cycle. Poverty (top) forces unsustainable resource use (right) to survive in the short run; this causes environmental degradation (bottom); degradation lowers the productivity of the land, water, and fisheries the poor depend on (left); lower productivity deepens poverty (back to top) — and the cycle repeats unless broken by policy intervention.

MechanismHow Poverty Drives Unsustainable PracticesHow Degradation Then Worsens Poverty
Fuelwood/charcoal dependenceHouseholds too poor to afford electricity, gas, or kerosene rely on cutting wood or making charcoal for cooking and heating, often the cheapest available fuel.Deforestation increases soil erosion and flood risk, reducing agricultural yields and raising the cost/time of future fuel collection as nearby forest is exhausted.
Subsistence/slash-and-burn farmingFarmers with no capital for fertiliser or irrigation clear new forest land (slash-and-burn) to keep soil fertile enough to survive, rather than investing in sustainable techniques.Cleared land loses fertility again within a few years, forcing farmers to clear further land — a cycle that degrades ever more land while farm incomes stay low.
Overfishing/mangrove clearanceCoastal households with no other income source fish beyond sustainable stock-replacement rates, or clear protective mangroves for short-term aquaculture (e.g. shrimp farming) income.Depleted fish stocks and lost mangrove buffers reduce future catches and remove a natural barrier against storm surges and cyclones, increasing vulnerability to disasters.
Key HL evaluation point: this cycle explains why purely environmental policies (e.g. banning deforestation) can fail if they ignore the poverty that drives the behaviour — and why purely anti-poverty policies can fail if they ignore the environmental degradation that traps the poor. The strongest HL essays argue that breaking the cycle requires addressing both sides simultaneously (e.g. providing an affordable alternative fuel source alongside forest protection), which is exactly why SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 15 (Life on Land) are treated as interlinked rather than separate goals.

Case Study: Haiti — Crisis, Response, and a Fragile Outcome

Poverty + charcoal dependence→ Forest cover collapses (est. ~60% in 1923 to low single digits by the 2000s)→ 2004 Hurricane Jeanne flooding, Gonaïves→ Reforestation/cookstove aid programmes
Real-world — Haiti's deforestation–poverty cycle: Haiti's forest cover is widely estimated to have fallen from around 60% in the early 20th century to under 4% by the 2000s, driven substantially by charcoal production — for decades, charcoal has remained the primary cooking fuel for most Haitian households, among the poorest in the Western Hemisphere by income per capita. The resulting deforestation removed vegetation that would otherwise have slowed rainwater run-off on Haiti's steep hillsides. When Hurricane Jeanne struck in September 2004, flash flooding and mudslides around the city of Gonaïves killed roughly 3,000 people — a death toll international agencies and researchers linked in part to the loss of forest cover on the surrounding hills compared with similar storms hitting more forested parts of the neighbouring Dominican Republic. In response, the Haitian government and international partners (including USAID and various NGOs) have run repeated reforestation and improved-cookstove programmes since the 2000s, aiming to reduce charcoal dependence — but funding has been intermittent, and Haiti's continued poverty means charcoal demand, and the deforestation pressure behind it, has proven difficult to fully reverse.
Real-world — a policy response, Ethiopia's Green Legacy Initiative: facing similar highland deforestation and soil-erosion pressures linked to poverty and population growth, Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed launched the Green Legacy Initiative in 2019, with the government reporting several billion tree seedlings planted in mass single-day planting campaigns in subsequent years. Independent researchers have raised questions about seedling survival rates and long-term monitoring, illustrating a common HL evaluation point: large-scale reforestation announcements are easier to make than to verify, and breaking the poverty–environment cycle durably requires sustained investment (in cheaper fuel alternatives, land rights, and farming techniques), not headline tree-planting numbers alone.
Cross-references: see the SL file (Sections 1–3) for the meaning of sustainable development and the SDG framework this cycle sits within; see 3.4 for measures of inequality that often show the poverty this cycle is rooted in; see 4.9 (barriers to growth and development) for further structural causes trapping countries in this cycle; and see 4.10 (growth and development strategies) for the policy responses (e.g. clean cookstove subsidies, land-tenure reform, microfinance) that aim to break it.

Where this shows up on the papers: this HL-only content most naturally extends the SL essay angle on Paper 1: "to what extent can sustainable development be achieved without addressing poverty?" is a strong part (b) ~15-mark essay title precisely because it requires HL students to bring in the two-way poverty–environment mechanism covered here, not just the SL definition of sustainable development. On Paper 2, this content can support a 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." sub-part if an extract's data table includes a poverty indicator alongside an environmental one (e.g. fuelwood/biomass energy use share, or a deforestation rate), feeding into the paper's single 15-mark discuss/evaluate question about a named country's development challenges. Paper 3, which is HL-only and quantitative, is unlikely to build a dedicated calculation type around this specific relationship — it is more likely to appear as context or discussion within a broader HL data set (e.g. a country profile that includes both a poverty rate and an environmental indicator) rather than its own calculation sub-part.
Exam tips:
  • Always state the relationship as genuinely two-way — poverty → unsustainable practices, and degradation → deepened poverty — rather than describing only one direction of causation.
  • Use a specific, named example (Haiti's charcoal-driven deforestation and the 2004 Hurricane Jeanne flooding, or Ethiopia's Green Legacy Initiative as a policy response) rather than writing generically about "poor countries damaging the environment."
  • In an essay evaluating whether sustainable development requires addressing poverty first, weigh the case for tackling poverty directly (e.g. cash transfers, microfinance, land-tenure reform) against the case for environmental protection alongside it (e.g. subsidised clean cookstoves, reforestation) — top answers argue both are usually needed together, not in strict sequence.
