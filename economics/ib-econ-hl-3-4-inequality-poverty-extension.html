Constructing a Lorenz Curve from Income Quintile Data

HL only

The SL file taught you to interpret a given Lorenz curve. At HL, you need to construct one yourself from raw income quintile data — dividing the population into five equal-sized groups (quintiles) ordered from poorest to richest, and plotting the cumulative share of income each group (and those below it) receives.

Worked Example — Constructing a Lorenz Curve
A country's national statistics office reports the following income shares by population quintile:
QuintileIncome Share (%)Cumulative Population (%)Cumulative Income (%)
Q1 (poorest 20%)4204
Q29404 + 9 = 13
Q3146013 + 14 = 27
Q4238027 + 23 = 50
Q5 (richest 20%)5010050 + 50 = 100
Construction steps: (1) order quintiles poorest to richest; (2) run a cumulative total down the "Cumulative Population" column in steps of 20%; (3) run a cumulative total down the "Cumulative Income" column, adding each quintile's income share to the previous cumulative total; (4) plot each (cumulative population %, cumulative income %) pair as a point, starting at (0,0); (5) join the points — this piecewise line is the constructed Lorenz curve.
The Lorenz curve constructed directly from the quintile table above: each dot is one cumulative (population %, income %) pair, joined point-to-point.
Order quintiles poorest→richest→ Cumulate population %→ Cumulate income %→ Plot & join points from (0,0)
Exam tip: examiners frequently award marks specifically for the cumulative columns of the table (showing your method), not just for the final sketch — always show your running totals even if the diagram itself is imprecise.
Real-world: a Lorenz curve constructed from South Africa's quintile income-share data would bow much further from the diagonal than one constructed from Iceland's, visually confirming the Gini coefficient gap referenced in the SL file (South Africa's Gini is among the highest in the world, close to 0.63).
Beyond the syllabus minimum — turning the constructed curve into a Gini number: examiners will not require you to compute an exact Gini value from quintile data, but seeing how the constructed curve produces a number strengthens your grasp of what the coefficient measures. A simple approximation (the "trapezoidal rule") sums the areas of the trapezoids formed under the Lorenz curve between each pair of plotted points, and compares that total to the area under the line of perfect equality — a triangle covering exactly half of the 100×100 square, i.e. an area of 5,000.
Extension — Approximating the Gini Coefficient from the Quintile Table
Using the cumulative pairs from the table above — (0,0), (20,4), (40,13), (60,27), (80,50), (100,100) — approximate the area under the curve as five trapezoids, each with area = width × (height₁ + height₂) ÷ 2.
Trapezoid 1: 20 × (0+4)÷2 = 40. Trapezoid 2: 20 × (4+13)÷2 = 170. Trapezoid 3: 20 × (13+27)÷2 = 400. Trapezoid 4: 20 × (27+50)÷2 = 770. Trapezoid 5: 20 × (50+100)÷2 = 1,500.
Total area under the Lorenz curve ≈ 40 + 170 + 400 + 770 + 1,500 = 2,880. Area under the line of perfect equality = 0.5 × 100 × 100 = 5,000.
Gini ≈ (5,000 − 2,880) ÷ 5,000 = ≈ 0.42 — a moderately unequal distribution, consistent with the shape of the constructed curve above.
List cumulative (population%, income%) points→ sum trapezoid areas under the curve→ compare to the 5,000-unit triangle under perfect equality→ Gini = (triangle − curve area) ÷ triangle

Calculating Indirect Tax Paid

HL only

The SL file explained that indirect taxes (VAT, sales tax, excise duties) tend to be regressive. At HL, you need to be able to calculate the actual amount of indirect tax paid, given the tax rate and a level of expenditure — in two possible forms of the question.

Case A — Tax Added on Top of a Tax-Exclusive Price
A consumer spends $250 (before tax) on goods subject to a 15% indirect tax.
Tax paid = 250 × 0.15 = $37.50
Total price paid (tax-inclusive) = 250 + 37.50 = $287.50
Case B — Extracting Tax from a Tax-Inclusive Price
A consumer pays $299 for a good, and this price already includes a 15% indirect tax. Find the tax paid.
Let the pre-tax price = x. Then x(1 + 0.15) = 299
x = 299 ÷ 1.15 = $260 (pre-tax price)
Tax paid = 299 − 260 = $39
Common error: students often multiply the tax-inclusive price directly by the tax rate (299 × 0.15 = $44.85) — this overstates the tax, because the rate applies to the pre-tax price, not the tax-inclusive price. Always divide by (1 + rate) first when the price given already includes tax.
Real-world: Nigeria's VAT rate of 7.5% applies to most goods and services (with some exemptions for basic food items), and understanding whether a quoted price is VAT-inclusive or VAT-exclusive is a genuinely practical everyday distinction for consumers and firms alike, not just an exam technicality.
Maurice Lauré, a French tax official at the Direction Générale des Impôts, designs a broad-based tax on the value added at each stage of production→ France introduces the world's first Value-Added Tax in 1954, replacing a patchwork of turnover taxes→ VAT spreads across the European Economic Community from the 1960s–70s, eventually becoming a condition of EEC/EU membership→ Today, VAT or an equivalent goods-and-services tax is used by well over 170 countries worldwide — though notably not by the United States, which relies instead on state-level sales taxes
Real-world: VAT rates vary widely and are a recurring source of regressivity debate — Hungary applies one of the world's highest standard VAT rates (27%), while many countries zero-rate or exempt necessities such as food, children's clothing, or medicines specifically to soften the regressive impact on low-income households, exactly as Nigeria (above) and Mexico (SL file) do.

Total Tax and Average Tax Rates from a Data Set

HL only

The SL file calculated an average tax rate directly from a single tax bill. At HL, you must calculate total tax paid — and hence the average tax rate — for an income that spans multiple tax brackets, each taxed at a different marginal rate (a progressive tax schedule).

Worked Example — Total Tax from a Progressive Bracket Table
A country's income tax schedule is:
Income BandMarginal Rate
$0 – $15,0000%
$15,001 – $45,00020%
Above $45,00035%
A worker earns $60,000. Calculate the total tax paid and their average tax rate.
Band 1 ($0–$15,000): 0% × 15,000 = $0
Band 2 ($15,001–$45,000, i.e. $30,000 of income): 20% × 30,000 = $6,000
Band 3 (income above $45,000, i.e. $15,000 of income): 35% × 15,000 = $5,250
Total tax paid = 0 + 6,000 + 5,250 = $11,250
Average tax rate = (11,250 ÷ 60,000) × 100 = 18.75% — well below the top marginal rate of 35%, because only the final $15,000 of income was taxed at that rate.
Split income into bands→ Tax each band at its own marginal rate→ Sum the band amounts = total tax→ Average rate = total tax ÷ total income
Reverse-Direction: Finding Income from Total Tax
Using the same tax schedule, a worker pays $6,500 in total tax. Which income band must their top income fall in, and what is it?
Tax fully accounted for by Bands 1+2 alone = $0 + $6,000 = $6,000 (at $45,000 income). Since $6,500 > $6,000, some income must fall in Band 3.
Extra tax beyond Band 2 = 6,500 − 6,000 = $500, taxed at 35% → extra income = 500 ÷ 0.35 = $1,428.57
Total income = 45,000 + 1,428.57 = ≈ $46,429
Exam tip: the single most common HL error is applying the top marginal rate to the entire income rather than only the portion that falls within the top band — always work through each band in turn and sum the results.
Real-world: countries vary widely in how many income tax bands they use and how steep the top marginal rate is — from flat-tax systems (e.g. several Gulf states levy no personal income tax at all, relying on other revenue sources) to highly progressive multi-bracket systems common in much of Latin America and parts of Asia, where top marginal rates can exceed 35–40% on the highest earners.
Real-world — a top tax bracket that backfired: in 2012, French President François Hollande proposed a temporary 75% top marginal tax rate on annual income above €1 million, explicitly framed as a response to rising inequality concerns after the 2008 financial crisis. The measure (revised so that firms, not individuals, were technically liable) applied for the 2013 and 2014 tax years before being allowed to lapse in 2015, having raised far less revenue than projected and been blamed for accelerating the departure of high earners from France — most visibly actor Gérard Depardieu, who registered residency in Belgium and was granted Russian citizenship by President Vladimir Putin in January 2013 in protest at the policy. The episode is a frequently cited exam example of the trade-off between a high top marginal rate's redistributive intent and its potential to shrink the tax base through avoidance, relocation, or reduced work effort.

Where this shows up on the papers: all three HL-only skills in this file — constructing a Lorenz curve from quintile data, calculating indirect tax paid, and calculating total tax/average tax rates across brackets — are examinable only on Paper 3: two ~30-mark structured questions (both compulsory, 60 marks total), each built around one extended data set of several tables, working through 7–8 short 2-mark (occasionally 4-mark) calculation/short-answer sub-parts that climb in difficulty — balance of payments components, PED, real GDP per capita, inflation from CPI/deflator, exchange-rate arithmetic, reverse-calculating a tax or subsidy rate, or (for this topic) a Gini/inequality-index calculation — with a Lorenz curve typically the subject of a 4-mark "using a [named] diagram, explain…" sub-part near the end. Use "draw" language for this standalone Lorenz-curve construction (as opposed to the smaller in-text "sketch" asked for on Paper 2 — see the SL file). The final sub-part on each question is always a single 10-mark question framed as "Using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to address/reduce [specific problem]" — note this asks you to recommend, not "evaluate" or "discuss", though top answers still justify the recommendation and acknowledge a counterargument or limitation. The underlying concepts may still appear in evaluative form on Paper 1 (e.g. weighing France's 2012 supertax episode above) and in data-response form on Paper 2, where the calculation itself would use the SL single-bracket methods rather than the full HL multi-bracket method.
Cross-reference: these calculation skills sit alongside the fiscal policy content in 3.6 (how governments decide how much to tax and spend) and the growth-vs-equity tension in 3.3 — a strong Paper 3 answer that finishes a calculation with one sentence linking the result to fiscal policy trade-offs elsewhere in the syllabus stands out from one that stops at the number.

Indirect Tax Paid = Pre-Tax Expenditure × Tax Rate
Forward — Tax-Exclusive Expenditure
Expenditure (pre-tax) = $180; tax rate = 12.5%.
Tax paid = 180 × 0.125 = $22.50
Reverse — Extracting Tax from a Tax-Inclusive Price
Tax-inclusive price = $450; tax rate = 12.5%. Find the tax paid.
Pre-tax price = 450 ÷ 1.125 = $400
Tax paid = 450 − 400 = $50
Total Tax (Multi-Bracket) = Σ (Band Width × Band's Marginal Rate)
Forward — Total Tax and Average Rate
Bands: $0–$12,000 at 0%; $12,001–$50,000 at 22%; above $50,000 at 38%. Income = $70,000.
Band 1: 0% × 12,000 = $0
Band 2: 22% × 38,000 = $8,360
Band 3: 38% × 20,000 = $7,600
Total tax = 0 + 8,360 + 7,600 = $15,960
Average tax rate = (15,960 ÷ 70,000) × 100 = 22.8%
Reverse — Finding Income from Total Tax
Using the same schedule, a taxpayer pays $12,000 in total tax. Find their income (assume it falls in Band 2).
Tax from Band 1 = $0. Remaining tax to account for = $12,000, all at 22% (Band 2 rate).
Income within Band 2 = 12,000 ÷ 0.22 = $54,545.45 — but Band 2 only extends to $38,000 of taxable width ($8,360 max tax), so $12,000 cannot be reached within Band 2 alone; some income must fall in Band 3.
Remaining tax after Band 2 = 12,000 − 8,360 = $3,640, taxed at 38% → extra income = 3,640 ÷ 0.38 = $9,578.95
Total income = 50,000 + 9,578.95 = ≈ $59,579
Given: total tax→ work backward band by band→ find remaining tax at the marginal band's rate→ add back the band threshold
Exam tips:
  1. For any Lorenz curve construction question, always present a full cumulative table before attempting the sketch — this is where most of the method marks are awarded.
  2. For indirect tax questions, the first thing to check is whether the given price is described as "before tax"/"excluding tax" or "including tax" — this single word changes the entire method.
  3. For multi-bracket tax questions, work through the bands in order from the bottom, and always check whether income "fills" a band completely before moving to the next rate.
  4. Show every intermediate band calculation explicitly — a correct final answer with no working shown risks losing method marks even if it's numerically right.
  5. Use real-world contrasts (South Africa vs Iceland for Gini/Lorenz; Nigeria for VAT; flat-tax Gulf states vs steeply progressive Latin American/Asian systems for bracket structures) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative points about tax and inequality policy.
