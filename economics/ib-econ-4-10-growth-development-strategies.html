Trade Strategies: Import Substitution, Export Promotion, Economic Integration

A country's trade strategy — how open it makes itself to the world economy, and in which direction — is one of the most consequential development choices a government makes. The 20th century supplies two clean, opposing historical experiments that IB examiners return to again and again.

Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI)
A strategy of protecting and subsidising domestic industries so they can replace previously imported manufactured goods, usually behind tariffs and quotas (see 4.2 for the tools themselves).
Export Promotion
A strategy of actively supporting industries to compete in export markets — through a competitive exchange rate, export subsidies, targeted credit, and infrastructure for exporters — rather than shielding the home market.
Economic Integration
Countries deliberately linking their economies through trade agreements, common markets or monetary unions, to gain a larger market, more foreign investment and greater bargaining power (the specific stages — free trade area, customs union, common market, monetary union — are built out fully in 4.4).

Import substitution and export promotion are best understood side by side as a single comparative historical arc — Latin America chose one path, East Asia chose the other, and the growth gap between the two regions from the 1960s onward is one of the most cited pieces of evidence in all of development economics.

1
1950s: the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC), under Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, formalises the case for ISI

Prebisch's "Prebisch–Singer thesis" (developed independently with Hans Singer) argued that the terms of trade for primary-commodity exporters tend to deteriorate over time relative to manufactured-goods importers, meaning countries relying on raw-material exports would fall permanently behind — the intellectual justification for building domestic manufacturing behind tariff walls instead.

2
1950s–1970s: Brazil, Argentina and Mexico pursue large-scale ISI

Governments used high tariffs, import licensing and state-owned enterprises (e.g. Brazil's Petrobras, founded 1953) to build steel, automobile and consumer-goods industries entirely for the domestic market, financed partly through foreign borrowing.

3
1980s: the Latin American debt crisis exposes ISI's weaknesses

Protected industries never had to become internationally competitive, external debt taken on to fund state-led industrialisation became unpayable once US interest rates rose sharply (1979–82), and Mexico's 1982 default triggered a region-wide crisis often called Latin America's "lost decade".

4
Late 1980s–1990s: a pivot toward trade liberalization and export promotion

Chile (from the mid-1970s, earliest mover), then Mexico (NAFTA, 1994) and much of the rest of the region, dismantled tariff walls and reoriented toward export-led growth — the same market-based direction covered for supply-side policy in 3.7.

★ REAL WORLD: South Korea's Export Promotion Drive under Park Chung-hee
While Latin America built inward-looking ISI economies, South Korea's military government under President Park Chung-hee (in power 1961–79) chose the opposite path: the 1960s export promotion drive used a deliberately competitive (undervalued) exchange rate, subsidised credit tied explicitly to export performance, and targeted infrastructure (the Gyeongbu Expressway, completed 1970, linking Seoul to the export port of Busan) to push a small number of family conglomerates — the chaebol — into world markets. Export revenue grew from under US$100 million in 1964 to over US$10 billion by 1977. Unlike ISI firms, Korean exporters faced constant discipline from foreign competition, which the government used as an explicit performance metric: subsidies to a firm were cut if its export targets were missed. (See 4.1 HL extension for further detail on Korea's post-1997 liberalisation and the Asian Financial Crisis.)
★ REAL WORLD: Argentina's Long ISI Hangover
Argentina pursued import substitution more persistently and for longer than most Latin American peers, protecting domestic manufacturing from the 1930s through repeated waves of policy into the late 20th century. Economic historians widely cite Argentina's case as a cautionary tale precisely because the country was among the world's ten richest by GDP per capita in 1913 — its relative decline over the following century is frequently used as evidence that prolonged protection without a credible exit strategy can convert an early advantage into chronic underperformance, echoing the "infant industry that never grows up" critique raised in 4.3.

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN, founded 1967, now ten members) established the ASEAN Free Trade Area in 1992 and the ASEAN Economic Community in 2015, giving smaller economies such as Laos and Cambodia access to a combined market of over 650 million people and making the region more attractive to inward FDI than any member could achieve alone (Section 5). The European Union's deepest form of integration — full monetary union with the euro (launched 1999/2002) — shows the other end of the spectrum: enormous gains in trade and investment for member states, but at the cost of surrendering independent monetary policy, a trade-off explored in full in 4.4.
Exam tip: Paper 1 essays on trade strategy reward candidates who can name the same country under different strategies across time (South Korea: export promotion in the 1960s–80s, gradual trade liberalisation and integration into global value chains since) rather than treating "trade strategy" as a single fixed policy a country adopts forever.

Diversification and Social Enterprise

Two strategies address different failure modes of over-specialisation and market gaps: diversification spreads a country's productive base across several sectors, while social enterprise mobilises private, market-based methods to deliver development outcomes the state or ordinary firms are not reaching.

Diversification
Broadening an economy's productive and export base across multiple industries, reducing dependence on one or two primary commodities whose prices are volatile on world markets.
Social Enterprise
A business that pursues a social or development mission (poverty reduction, financial inclusion, environmental benefit) using commercial, self-sustaining methods rather than relying solely on continuous donor funding or government subsidy.
★ REAL WORLD: Botswana's Diamond-Led Diversification Push
Botswana has been one of the world's fastest-growing economies since independence in 1966, built almost entirely on diamond exports (through Debswana, a 50/50 joint venture with De Beers established in 1969). Recognising the danger of relying on a single, geologically finite resource — the same over-specialisation risk raised for economically least developed countries in 4.3 — the government has used diamond revenue to fund a sovereign wealth fund (the Pula Fund, established 1994) and to actively promote tourism, financial services, and beef and agricultural exports. Diamonds still dominate exports, illustrating how difficult diversification is even with decades of deliberate policy and a relatively well-governed state.
★ REAL WORLD: The UAE and Dubai's Post-Oil Diversification
Dubai, one of the seven emirates of the United Arab Emirates, had comparatively modest oil reserves even at independence (1971) and invested oil-era revenue deliberately into aviation (Emirates airline, founded 1985, and Dubai International Airport), tourism, logistics (Jebel Ali Port, the largest man-made harbour in the world) and financial services (the Dubai International Financial Centre, established 2004). Oil now accounts for a small fraction of Dubai's GDP, a far more advanced diversification than Abu Dhabi, the oil-richer emirate that diversified later and more slowly — a useful within-country comparison for a Paper 1 or Paper 2 answer on diversification timing and incentives.
★ REAL WORLD: Grameen Bank — Social Enterprise as a Development Model
Economist Muhammad Yunus began lending small, collateral-free sums to poor villagers in Jobra, Bangladesh in 1976 as an experiment while teaching at Chittagong University; the project became the formally chartered Grameen Bank in 1983. Grameen combined a social mission (reaching the poorest, especially women, who commercial banks refused to lend to) with a commercial, self-sustaining lending model — borrowers organised into small peer groups that provided informal collective accountability in place of collateral, and the bank charged interest sufficient to cover its costs rather than depending indefinitely on donor grants. Yunus and Grameen Bank jointly won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 "for efforts to create economic and social development from below". Grameen is now the textbook example of a social enterprise: it behaves like a business (loans must be repaid, interest is charged, the institution must remain solvent) while pursuing an explicitly developmental goal.
Cross-reference: social enterprise through microfinance reappears in Section 6 as part of institutional change (access to banking) — the two syllabus dot points describe the same real-world institutions (Grameen Bank, and its many replications) from two angles: Section 2 as a business model, Section 6 as financial inclusion.

Market-Based Policies

Just as with the general supply-side toolkit in 3.7, market-based development strategies aim to raise growth and development by removing government intervention and letting price signals allocate resources. The syllabus names three specific tools in a development context: trade liberalization, privatization, and deregulation.

Trade Liberalization
Reducing tariffs, quotas and other barriers to trade so domestic firms face full international competition and consumers gain access to cheaper imports (the flip side of the ISI story in Section 1).
Privatization
Transferring ownership of state-owned enterprises to the private sector, on the theory that profit-driven private owners run firms more efficiently than state bureaucracies with weaker cost discipline.
Deregulation
Removing or loosening government rules that restrict who may enter a market or how a firm may operate, intended to lower the cost of doing business and encourage new firms and investment.
★ REAL WORLD: China's Deng Xiaoping Reforms from 1978
Following Mao Zedong's death (1976), Deng Xiaoping consolidated power and launched "Reform and Opening-up" (gaige kaifang) from December 1978: agricultural decollectivisation (the "household responsibility system") let farming families sell surplus output at market prices; four Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen first, from 1980) opened to foreign investment and trade under lighter regulation than the rest of the country; and, over subsequent decades, tens of thousands of state-owned enterprises were restructured or privatized, particularly from the 1990s. Average annual GDP growth exceeded 9% for the following three decades and roughly 800 million people were lifted out of extreme poverty by World Bank estimates — the single largest poverty reduction in human history, and a case that combines market-based reform (Section 3) with heavy continued state direction of strategic sectors, a genuinely contested mix for Paper 1 evaluation.
★ REAL WORLD: Chile's "Chicago Boys" Reforms under Pinochet
After the 1973 military coup that brought General Augusto Pinochet to power, a group of Chilean economists trained at the University of Chicago under Milton Friedman and Arnold Harberger — nicknamed the "Chicago Boys" — implemented sweeping market-based reform from the mid-1970s: rapid trade liberalization (average tariffs cut from over 100% to a flat 10% by 1979), privatization of hundreds of state enterprises, and deregulation of financial markets and the labour market. Chile's economy grew strongly from the mid-1980s onward and the country now has among the highest GDP per capita in Latin America, but the reforms were imposed under an authoritarian government that suppressed political opposition, and a severe banking crisis in 1982 required a costly state bailout — a reminder that "market-based" and "well-governed" are not the same thing, and that the political conditions under which a reform happens matter for evaluation.
Exam tip: the strongest Paper 1 answers on market-based development policy distinguish the economic case (efficiency, resource allocation, budget relief — as in 3.7) from the political and social context in which it was implemented (authoritarian imposition in Chile vs continued state-party control in China) — examiners reward this kind of layered evaluation over a simple "it worked/it didn't" verdict.

Interventionist Policies: Redistribution and Merit Goods

Interventionist development policy uses active government spending and regulation directly, on the theory that some development gains — reducing extreme inequality, providing merit goods that markets under-supply — will not happen fast enough, or at all, if left purely to markets (a market failure argument, exactly as for interventionist supply-side policy in 3.7).

Redistribution Policies

Progressive Tax Policies
Taxing higher incomes at a higher rate, raising revenue for spending on development priorities while directly narrowing post-tax income inequality (built out fully in 3.4).
Transfer Payments
Direct government payments to individuals or households (pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefits, conditional cash transfers) that redistribute income without any exchange of goods or services in return.
Minimum Wage Legislation
A legally mandated wage floor intended to raise the income of the lowest-paid workers, at the risk of causing classical unemployment if set above the market-clearing wage (the same trade-off analysed in 3.7's labour market diagram).
★ REAL WORLD: Brazil's Bolsa Família Conditional Cash Transfer
Brazil's Bolsa Família programme (launched 2003, consolidating several smaller schemes, later replaced by the similar Bolsa Família 2.0 in 2023) pays a monthly cash transfer to low-income families conditional on children attending school and receiving vaccinations. At its peak the programme reached around 14 million families — roughly a quarter of Brazil's population — and is widely credited by the World Bank and independent researchers with meaningfully reducing extreme poverty and improving school attendance, at a fiscal cost of well under 1% of GDP, making it one of the most frequently cited "cheap and effective" redistribution case studies in development economics.

Provision of Merit Goods

Merit Good
A good (education, health care) that is under-consumed if left to the free market because private individuals undervalue its full long-run benefit relative to its true social value — the classic justification for direct government provision or subsidy.
Infrastructure
Government investment in energy, transport, telecommunications, clean water and sanitation networks — physical foundations that lower costs for every firm and household using them and that private markets often under-supply, given high upfront cost and wide, hard-to-charge-for benefits.

★ REAL WORLD: Rwanda's Mutuelles de Santé Health Insurance
In the aftermath of the 1994 genocide, Rwanda's health system had been devastated alongside the rest of its institutions. From 1999, the government built a community-based health insurance scheme, Mutuelles de Santé, charging small, income-scaled annual premiums to give the large majority of the population access to basic healthcare — a case built in more depth as a supply-side strategy in 3.7. By the 2010s over 90% of Rwandans were covered, one of the highest health-insurance coverage rates in sub-Saharan Africa, and health outcomes (under-five mortality, life expectancy) improved sharply over the same period — though Rwanda's health gains sit alongside continued, separately documented concerns about restrictions on civil and political freedoms under President Paul Kagame, a reminder that development gains and political governance can be evaluated independently of one another.
★ REAL WORLD: Ethiopia's Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP)
Launched in 2005 in response to recurring famine and chronic food insecurity, Ethiopia's Productive Safety Net Programme is one of the largest social protection schemes in Africa, providing predictable cash or food transfers to around 8 million chronically food-insecure people, with able-bodied recipients working on public infrastructure projects (soil and water conservation, rural roads) in exchange for payment — combining a redistribution transfer payment (Section 4) with infrastructure provision (Section 4) and a deliberate break from the emergency, unpredictable famine-relief aid that preceded it (a useful contrast with the humanitarian aid discussed in Section 5).

Inward FDI, Foreign Aid and Multilateral Assistance

Where Sections 3–4 covered strategies a government runs on its own, this section covers strategies that bring in resources — capital, expertise, concessional finance — from outside the country.

Inward Foreign Direct Investment

Inward Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)
Investment by a foreign firm establishing lasting ownership or control of a productive asset in the host country (a factory, a majority equity stake), rather than short-term portfolio investment — recorded on the financial account of the balance of payments (see 4.6).
★ REAL WORLD: Vietnam's FDI-Led Growth
Following its 1986 Đổi Mới ("Renovation") reforms opening the economy to market forces and foreign investment, Vietnam pursued an explicit strategy of attracting export-oriented manufacturing FDI through low corporate tax incentives, competitive labour costs, political stability and, since 2007, WTO membership. Major multinationals including Samsung (whose Vietnamese factories now produce roughly half of all Samsung smartphones sold worldwide) and, more recently, Apple suppliers relocated significant manufacturing capacity to Vietnam, accelerated further by firms diversifying supply chains away from China during the US–China trade tensions from 2018. FDI inflows helped Vietnam's economy grow at among the fastest rates in Asia over the following two decades and shift decisively from a low-income, agriculture-based economy to a middle-income manufacturing exporter.
★ REAL WORLD: Ireland's FDI Strategy
Ireland's use of a low headline corporate tax rate (12.5%, discussed as an incentive-related supply-side policy in 3.7) combined with EU single-market membership, an English-speaking, well-educated workforce, and a stable legal system to attract enormous inward FDI from US technology and pharmaceutical multinationals (Google, Meta, Apple, Pfizer). FDI-driven activity has been a central driver of Ireland transforming from one of Western Europe's poorer economies in the 1980s to one of its richest by GDP per capita — though critics note that a large share of Ireland's headline GDP reflects the accounting presence of multinational intellectual property and profit-shifting rather than domestic economic activity, which is why economists increasingly cite Ireland's modified gross national income (GNI*) as a more reliable measure of genuine domestic living standards.

Foreign Aid

Humanitarian Aid
Emergency assistance (food, medicine, shelter) provided in response to an immediate crisis such as a natural disaster, famine or conflict, intended to save lives rather than build long-run productive capacity.
Development Aid
Longer-term assistance aimed at raising a recipient country's productive capacity and living standards (funding schools, health systems, infrastructure) rather than responding to an immediate emergency.
Debt Relief
Cancelling or restructuring a developing country's external debt, freeing up government revenue that would otherwise go toward debt service for spending on development priorities instead.
Official Development Assistance (ODA)
Government-to-government aid flows meeting OECD criteria (concessional terms, developmental or welfare intent), tracked and published by the OECD Development Assistance Committee.
Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs)
Non-profit organisations, independent of government, that deliver aid, run development projects, or advocate for policy change (e.g. Oxfam, Médecins Sans Frontières, BRAC).

Debt relief has its own crisis → response → outcome arc worth knowing in named detail.

Crisis: many low-income countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, accumulate unpayable external debt through the 1980s–90s (partly incurred financing the ISI-era industrialisation of Section 1, and worsened by 1980s interest rate rises) → debt service consumes a large share of government revenue, crowding out health and education spending → the IMF and World Bank launch the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996, later enhanced in 1999 → qualifying countries (over 35, mostly in Africa) receive debt cancellation conditional on poverty-reduction spending plans → freed-up revenue is redirected to merit-good provision (Section 4), though critics note the conditionality attached to debt relief has often mirrored the same structural adjustment conditions criticised below.
★ REAL WORLD: Jeffrey Sachs's Millennium Villages Project
Economist Jeffrey Sachs, an influential advocate for large-scale development aid, launched the Millennium Villages Project in 2005 across a network of villages in ten African countries, providing a coordinated package of aid (fertiliser and seed subsidies, malaria bed nets, school and clinic construction, safe water access) intended to demonstrate that a sufficiently large, well-targeted aid "big push" could lift entire communities out of extreme poverty within a decade. Independent evaluations found real but more modest and less durable gains than the project's own initial claims, and the project became a central reference point in the wider aid-effectiveness debate below.
★ REAL WORLD: Dambisa Moyo's "Dead Aid" Critique (2009)
Zambian-born economist Dambisa Moyo's 2009 book Dead Aid: Why Aid Is Not Working and How There Is a Better Way for Africa argued that decades of government-to-government aid to Africa had, on balance, entrenched corrupt and unaccountable governments, crowded out local entrepreneurship, and created a self-perpetuating "aid dependency" that discouraged the institutional reforms genuinely needed for growth — proposing instead that African economies should rely more on trade, inward FDI (Section 5 above) and access to capital markets. Moyo's critique, alongside similar arguments from economist William Easterly, represents the strongest counter-position to the Sachs "big push" view, and Paper 1 essays that cite both named positions on the aid-effectiveness debate typically score more highly on evaluation than essays asserting aid simply "works" or "doesn't work".

Multilateral Development Assistance

The World Bank
A multilateral institution that provides loans (often at below-market, concessional rates through its International Development Association arm) and technical assistance for development projects in low- and middle-income countries.
The International Monetary Fund (IMF)
A multilateral institution that provides short-to-medium-term financial support to countries facing balance of payments crises, usually attached to policy conditions (a "structural adjustment programme").
★ REAL WORLD: Bretton Woods, 1944, and the Structural Adjustment Backlash
Both the World Bank and the IMF were founded at the Bretton Woods Conference in July 1944, as 44 Allied nations designed a post-war international economic architecture intended to prevent a repeat of the competitive currency devaluations and trade collapse of the 1930s. From the 1980s, both institutions attached "structural adjustment programmes" to their lending — packages typically requiring trade liberalization, privatization, deregulation and reduced government spending (precisely the Section 3 market-based toolkit) as conditions for loans. Critics, especially in the 1990s and 2000s, argued these one-size-fits-all conditions were imposed without enough regard for individual country circumstances, sometimes cut health and education spending exactly when it was most needed, and contributed to the debt burdens the HIPC Initiative later had to address — a direct, named link back to the debt relief arc above.

Institutional Change

"Institutional change" strategies target the underlying rules, access and social structures within which all the other strategies in this chapter operate — a strong Paper 1 essay can argue that without institutional change, other strategies (FDI, aid, trade) deliver far less than their theoretical potential.

Improved Access to Banking
Extending formal financial services (savings accounts, credit, insurance, payments) to previously excluded populations, enabling saving, investment and risk management that informal or non-existent financial access cannot provide.
Microfinance
Small loans, savings products and insurance targeted at low-income individuals and small businesses who lack the collateral or credit history required by conventional banks.
Mobile Banking
Financial services (transfers, payments, savings) delivered via basic mobile phones rather than physical bank branches, dramatically lowering the cost of reaching rural or low-income populations.
Women's Empowerment
Policies and social change that expand women's economic opportunities, education, and decision-making power, raising female labour-force participation and, evidence suggests, household and national development outcomes.

Microfinance's own history — from a single village experiment to a global movement to a documented later crisis — is worth knowing as a complete, balanced arc in its own right, extending the Grameen story introduced in Section 2.

1
1976: Muhammad Yunus's pilot lending experiment in Jobra, Bangladesh

Small, collateral-free loans to landless villagers, organised through peer-group accountability, funded initially from Yunus's own resources and a local bank guarantee.

2
1983: Grameen Bank formally chartered by Bangladeshi legislation

The model scales nationally, reaching millions of borrowers — overwhelmingly women, who Yunus found were more likely than men to invest loans in household welfare and repay reliably.

3
1990s–2000s: global replication

Microfinance institutions modelled on Grameen spread across South Asia, Latin America and Africa; commercial banks and even Wall Street investors begin funding microfinance institutions, drawn by high historical repayment rates.

4
2006: Nobel Peace Prize awarded jointly to Yunus and Grameen Bank

International recognition cements microfinance as a mainstream development strategy, and donor and government funding accelerates further.

5
2010: the Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis, India

Rapid, poorly regulated growth of commercial microfinance in the Indian state of Andhra Pradesh led to aggressive over-lending, multiple loans to the same borrowers, and coercive collection practices; a wave of borrower suicides linked to debt distress led the state government to intervene sharply, and loan repayment collapsed across the sector. The episode remains the central case examiners expect for a balanced evaluation of microfinance — genuine financial inclusion gains (Sections 2 and 6) alongside a documented case of over-indebtedness when growth outpaces regulation and social safeguards.

★ REAL WORLD: M-Pesa, Kenya (2007)
Launched by mobile network operator Safaricom (majority-owned by Vodafone) in March 2007, M-Pesa let Kenyans send, receive and store money using basic mobile phones via a network of small local agents, without needing a traditional bank account or branch. Within a decade, M-Pesa reached the large majority of Kenyan adults and is credited by a widely cited 2016 Science study (Suri and Jack) with helping lift roughly 2% of Kenyan households out of extreme poverty, disproportionately benefiting women-headed households, by enabling safer saving, easier remittances from urban workers to rural families, and access to small emergency loans. M-Pesa is the landmark global case for "mobile banking" as a distinct institutional-change strategy from conventional microfinance, and has since been extended into savings, credit (M-Shwari) and even solar-power payment products.
★ REAL WORLD: Women's Empowerment in Bangladesh's Garment Sector
Bangladesh's ready-made garment export industry, which grew from almost nothing in the late 1970s to the country's dominant export sector (and the world's second-largest garment exporter after China), employs a workforce that is roughly 80% female. Access to formal factory wages gave many women in Bangladesh their first independent income, associated in multiple studies with delayed marriage and childbirth, higher rates of daughters' school enrolment, and greater household bargaining power — even as the same sector has faced serious and well-documented criticism over workplace safety (most notably the Rana Plaza factory collapse in 2013, which killed over 1,100 workers) and low wages, again illustrating that a genuine development gain and a genuine cost can coexist in the same named case.
World Bank evidence: cross-country World Bank data consistently show that closing the gap between male and female labour-force participation rates is associated with substantially higher GDP — frequently cited estimates put the potential gain in the range of several percentage points of global output — making women's empowerment one of the few development strategies with a broad evidence base spanning very different income levels and regions, not just a single named case.

Why this matters for exam evaluation: the single most important idea in this whole chapter is that no strategy is universally best — a strong evaluative answer weighs each strategy against a country's specific starting conditions (its institutions, its factor endowments, its stage of development, its political constraints) rather than asserting that market-based policy always outperforms interventionist policy, or vice versa. South Korea's state-directed export promotion (arguably "interventionist" in method even while market-oriented in goal) succeeded partly because of strong state capacity and discipline that many other developing countries lacked; the same policy transplanted into a weakly governed state could easily become directionless subsidy and corruption instead. Examiners consistently reward candidates who make this "it depends on starting conditions" argument explicitly, with named evidence, rather than treating it as a throwaway final sentence.

Where this shows up on the papers — read this carefully, this is the single most important framing point in the chapter:

Paper 1: part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain two named strategies and how each could raise growth or development for a hypothetical or named country; part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) then asks you to evaluate — "evaluate the view that market-based policies are more effective than interventionist policies in promoting economic development" is a natural, frequently-set essay title. This topic is a particular favourite for essays requiring you to select and justify two or three named strategies for a specific country context, rather than simply listing every strategy in the syllabus.

Paper 2 (40 marks, you answer ONE of two questions, each built from two linked Text extracts with numbered paragraphs plus data tables, every sub-question citing an exact reference): expect the standard ladder — (a)(i) and (a)(ii) are two 2-mark "define the term..." questions on bolded terms from the text; (b) is a 2–3 mark calculation or a 2-mark sketch of a diagram redeployed from elsewhere in the course; (c)–(f) run through 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each citing an exact paragraph or table; and the question closes with a single 15-mark part (g) such as "using information from the text/data and your knowledge of economics, evaluate [named country]'s [specific] development strategy."

Paper 3: honestly, this topic rarely has its own dedicated arithmetic on Paper 3. Its real role is supplying the policy options that candidates draw on for the final structured question on a Paper 3 paper — the genuinely important framing point is that after working through a Paper 3 question built around, say, unemployment, inequality or a balance of payments problem in a named country, the paper's very last sub-question is routinely a 10-mark "recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to address [the problem identified earlier in the question]" — and the strategies in this chapter (trade strategy, market-based reform, interventionist spending, FDI, aid, institutional change) are exactly the menu of options that answer supplies. Treat this chapter, and this chapter alone, as your answer bank for that final Paper 3 question across every other Unit 3 and Unit 4 subtopic.

The Golden Chain: Recommending a Development Strategy for a Named Country

1
Identify the specific problem the country faces

Is it chronic under-investment (interventionist merit-good spending needed)? A narrow export base (diversification)? Weak financial inclusion (institutional change)? Read the Paper 2/3 text or data table carefully — do not assume.

2
Select 2–3 strategies from this chapter that plausibly address that specific problem

Do not attempt to cover every strategy in the syllabus — a focused, well-justified selection scores far higher than an unfocused list.

3
Justify each selection with a named real-world parallel

"Similar to Rwanda's Mutuelles de Santé..." or "as with M-Pesa in Kenya..." — anchoring your recommendation in real precedent, not abstract assertion.

4
Weigh feasibility against the country's specific starting conditions

Government revenue capacity, existing institutions, political stability — the same "it depends on starting conditions" evaluation from Section 7.

5
State a clear final recommendation

A strong final paragraph commits to a judgement rather than leaving the question open — examiners reward a reasoned recommendation over "it could go either way".

Key Exam Tips

1

Do not invent new diagrams for this topic. Redeploy a diagram you already know — a merit-good subsidy diagram, an LRAS shift, a tariff/quota diagram (4.2) — and relabel it for the development context in front of you.

2

Always classify a strategy correctly as market-based or interventionist before evaluating it — the mechanism (removing intervention vs. adding it) determines what its likely strengths and weaknesses will be.

3

Cross-reference relentlessly: this chapter is designed to be read alongside 4.1 (trade), 4.2/4.3 (protection vs liberalization), 3.4 (inequality/redistribution), 3.6 (fiscal policy), 3.7 (supply-side policy), 4.5 (exchange rates for export promotion), 4.6 (balance of payments for FDI/aid flows), 4.8 (measuring development) and 4.9 (barriers to development, which these strategies exist to respond to).

4

Use named, dated evidence on both sides of a debate — Sachs vs Moyo on aid effectiveness, the Andhra Pradesh crisis alongside Grameen Bank's Nobel Prize, China's growth alongside its authoritarian governance — rather than a one-sided case.

5

For the Paper 3 "recommend a policy" question specifically, always tie your recommended strategy directly back to the problem identified earlier in that same question — a recommendation that does not visibly follow from the data/text you were just given loses marks even if the strategy itself is well explained.
