Preferential Trade Agreements

Preferential trade agreement (PTA)
An agreement between countries to reduce (not necessarily eliminate) trade barriers between them, giving each other more favourable treatment than non-members receive.
TypeDescription
BilateralAn agreement between exactly two countries.
RegionalAn agreement among a group of neighbouring or geographically linked countries.
MultilateralAn agreement involving many countries simultaneously, typically negotiated through the World Trade Organization (WTO).
Real-world: the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is a large multilateral agreement bringing together most African Union member states to progressively reduce tariffs on trade between them — one of the largest free trade areas by number of participating countries in the world.
Link to 4.1: trading blocs and PTAs are a policy response to the case for free trade covered in 4.1 (comparative advantage and the gains from specialisation). Rather than pursuing multilateral free trade with every country simultaneously, groups of countries agree to remove barriers between themselves first — trading blocs are, in effect, "free trade in instalments."

Trading Blocs

Type of BlocKey Feature
Free trade area/agreementMembers remove tariffs and other barriers between themselves, but each member keeps its own independent tariffs against non-members.
Customs unionA free trade area that also adopts a common external tariff — all members charge the same tariff rate on imports from non-members.
Common marketA customs union that additionally allows free movement of factors of production (labour and capital) between member countries.
Joining a customs union removes the tariff on trade with fellow members: the domestic price paid for a partner-country good falls from the tariff-inclusive price toward the tariff-free partner price. Domestic supply contracts a little (Qs₁→Qs₂), domestic demand expands (Qd₁→Qd₂), and imports from the partner grow substantially — the basic free-trade gain (see 4.1) that underpins a trading bloc's "trade creation". The fuller trade-creation/trade-diversion construction, which also asks whether those imports were previously sourced more cheaply from outside the bloc, is HL-only content covered with its own diagram in the HL extension file.
Real-world: the Southern Common Market (Mercosur), comprising Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay, and associate members, operates as a customs union with a common external tariff on goods entering from outside the bloc.
Real-world: the East African Community (EAC) — including Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, and South Sudan — has progressed from a free trade area toward a customs union and common market, with efforts to allow freer movement of labour and capital across member states.

How Deep Does Integration Go? Comparing Real Trading Blocs

BlocFoundedMembersDepth of Integration
ASEAN1967 (Bangkok Declaration)10 Southeast Asian states, e.g. Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, SingaporeMostly a free trade area (AFTA, from 1992) — deliberately shallow; no common external tariff or shared currency, prioritising national sovereignty and consensus decision-making over pooled institutions.
Mercosur1991 (Treaty of Asunción)Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay (plus associate members)Customs union with a common external tariff, but free movement of labour and capital between members remains limited compared with a true common market.
USMCA (formerly NAFTA)2020, replacing the 1994 NAFTAUnited States, Mexico, CanadaFree trade area — tariffs removed between the three members, but each keeps its own independent external tariff; the 2020 update tightened rules-of-origin requirements (e.g. on car parts) rather than deepening political integration.
European Union1993 Maastricht Treaty (roots in 1951/1957)27 member statesA common/single market, with a monetary union (the euro) among 20 of its members — the deepest real-world example of economic integration.
Why this matters for evaluation: not every trading bloc sits at the same point on the FTA → customs union → common market → monetary union ladder. ASEAN has deliberately stayed shallow to protect members' sovereignty and avoid the political friction of a common external tariff, while the EU has gone all the way to a shared currency. A strong Paper 1 answer identifies where a named bloc actually sits on this ladder, rather than treating "trading bloc" as one uniform category.

The Historical Formation of the European Union

1951 — European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC)→ 1957 — Treaty of Rome creates the EEC→ 1993 — Maastricht Treaty creates the Single Market→ 1999–2002 — the euro is launched
Real-world: the EU's economic integration did not happen overnight. In 1950, French foreign minister Robert Schuman — building on a plan largely drafted by civil servant Jean Monnet — proposed pooling French and West German coal and steel production under a common authority, explicitly to make another European war "not merely unthinkable but materially impossible." This became the 1951 Treaty of Paris establishing the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) among six founding members. The 1957 Treaty of Rome broadened this into the European Economic Community (EEC), a customs union covering all goods. The 1993 Maastricht Treaty then created the Single Market (a common market) and set the legal path to a single currency, which finally launched as an accounting currency in 1999 and as physical notes and coins in 2002. The whole process took over 50 years to move through every stage of the integration ladder.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Trading Blocs

AdvantageExplanation
Greater access to markets / economies of scaleFirms can sell into a much larger combined market, supporting higher output and lower average costs.
Greater employment opportunities (with freedom of labour)Workers can move to where jobs are available across the bloc, potentially reducing unemployment in some members.
Stronger bargaining powerA bloc negotiating as one voice can secure better terms in trade negotiations than any single small member could alone.
Greater political stability and cooperationCloser economic ties can strengthen diplomatic relationships and reduce the risk of conflict between members.
DisadvantageExplanation
Loss of sovereigntyMembers may need to align regulations, standards, or policies with the bloc, limiting independent national decision-making.
Challenge to multilateral trading negotiationsBlocs can complicate broader global trade negotiations (e.g. through the WTO) by creating competing regional rules and preferences.
Note: two further important effects of trading blocs — trade creation and trade diversion — are HL-only concepts, covered with their own diagrams in the HL extension file.
Real-world: ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) member states have gained stronger collective bargaining power in trade negotiations with larger economies like China and Japan than any single member (such as Laos or Cambodia) could achieve alone.
Real-world — Brexit as dis-integration: the United Kingdom's June 2016 referendum (52% to leave) led to a formal exit from the EU in January 2020 and the end of the transition period on 1 January 2021. Leaving the EU's customs union and single market brought back customs checks, rules-of-origin paperwork, and new non-tariff barriers on UK–EU trade that had not existed for decades — UK exporters reported significant new administrative costs, and independent studies have estimated a meaningful reduction in UK–EU trade volumes compared with continued membership. Brexit is trading-bloc theory running in reverse: leaving a bloc re-imposes exactly the sovereignty-versus-market-access trade-off that joining one is meant to resolve — regaining control over immigration and regulation (sovereignty) at the cost of smoother market access. (See 4.2/4.3: many of the barriers that reappeared after Brexit — tariffs on some goods, customs procedures, quotas by another name — are exactly the protectionist tools that economic integration is designed to remove.)

Monetary Union

Monetary union
A group of countries that adopt a single shared currency and a single central monetary authority, going a step further than a common market.

A monetary union removes exchange rate uncertainty and conversion costs between members entirely, since they share one currency — but it also means members give up their own independent monetary policy (interest rates, money supply) in favour of a single central bank serving the whole union.

Note: a full evaluation of the advantages and disadvantages of monetary union is HL-only content, covered in the HL extension file.
Real-world: the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU/UEMOA) — including countries such as Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, and Burkina Faso — shares a single currency (the CFA franc) and a common central bank, illustrating monetary union outside the more commonly cited Eurozone example.

The World Trade Organization (WTO)

World Trade Organization (WTO)
The main international body overseeing global trade rules, settling trade disputes between members, and negotiating multilateral trade agreements.
Objectives and FunctionsExplanation
Setting global trade rulesEstablishes agreed rules that member countries commit to follow in their trade policy.
Dispute settlementProvides a formal process for resolving trade disagreements between member countries.
Trade negotiation forumHosts multilateral negotiations aimed at further reducing global trade barriers.
Factors Limiting WTO InfluenceExplanation
Difficulty reaching agreement on services/primary productsNegotiations on agriculture and services have proven especially difficult, as they touch politically sensitive domestic sectors in many member countries.
Unequal bargaining power of membersLarger, wealthier economies often have more negotiating leverage than smaller or lower-income members, shaping outcomes in their favour.
Real-world: WTO negotiations on agricultural subsidies and market access (the long-running "Doha Round") have repeatedly stalled for over two decades, partly because major economies have been unwilling to significantly cut agricultural subsidies that many smaller and developing-country members argue distort world prices against them.

From GATT to the WTO: A Brief History

1947 — GATT signed by 23 countries→ 1986–94 — the Uruguay Round→ 1995 — WTO founded (Marrakesh Agreement)
Real-world: the WTO did not exist until 1995. Before that, global trade rules were governed by the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), signed in 1947 by 23 countries in the aftermath of the Second World War, specifically to avoid a repeat of the tariff wars of the 1930s. GATT ran a series of negotiating "rounds"; the eighth and final one, the Uruguay Round (1986–1994), was also the most ambitious — it extended trade rules beyond goods to services and intellectual property, and its concluding 1994 Marrakesh Agreement formally created the WTO as a permanent institution with a binding dispute-settlement system, launched on 1 January 1995. The stalled Doha Round (above) shows how much harder consensus has become since: with 160-plus members today versus GATT's original 23, unanimous agreement on new rules is far more difficult to reach.

Where this shows up on the papers: on Paper 1, a part (b) essay (≈15 marks, AO3) commonly asks you to evaluate whether joining, or belonging to, a trading bloc benefits a named country or group of countries — strong answers weigh named advantages (market access, bargaining power) against named disadvantages (sovereignty, WTO friction) rather than just listing both sides. Paper 2 is built from two linked Text extracts with numbered paragraphs plus data tables, and every sub-question cites an exact reference (e.g. "Text B, paragraph 2" or "Table 3"): expect two 2-mark "define the term..." sub-parts (e.g. "define common market"), a 2–3 mark AO4 intra-bloc trade share calculation from the data, a run of 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each anchored to a named paragraph or table, and a single 15-mark "discuss"/"evaluate" question weighing bloc membership for the named country in the extract. Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units (% or $)→ Interpret the answer in context
Share of Intra-Bloc Trade (%) = (Trade with Bloc Members ÷ Total Trade) × 100
Forward — Intra-Bloc Trade Share
A country's total exports = $80 billion; exports to fellow trading-bloc members = $52 billion.
Intra-bloc trade share = (52 ÷ 80) × 100 = 65% — a high share, suggesting the bloc has become central to this country's trade pattern.
Reverse-Direction: Finding Total Trade
Exports to bloc members = $36 billion, representing 45% of total exports. Find total exports.
Total exports = 36 ÷ 0.45 = $80 billion
Given: intra-bloc trade + %→ Total trade = Intra-bloc trade ÷ %→ check units are $
Exam tips:
  1. Always distinguish clearly between the four levels of integration (PTA/FTA, customs union, common market, monetary union) by their defining feature — do not just list vague "closer cooperation" descriptions.
  2. Note that trade creation and trade diversion — the two most commonly examined effects of joining a trading bloc — are HL-only content, covered with diagrams in the HL extension file.
  3. Use specific real-world examples (AfCFTA for multilateral agreements; Mercosur/EAC for customs unions and common markets; WAEMU for monetary union; the WTO's Doha Round for negotiation difficulty) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays.
