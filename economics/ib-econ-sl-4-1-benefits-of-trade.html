Why Countries Trade: The Benefits of International Trade

International trade allows countries to consume beyond what they could produce on their own, by buying and selling goods and services across borders. The benefits are wide-ranging and go well beyond simply "more stuff to buy":

BenefitExplanation
Increased competitionDomestic firms face pressure from foreign competitors, which can push down prices, improve quality, and drive innovation.
Lower pricesConsumers can buy from the cheapest global producer rather than being limited to (often less efficient) domestic suppliers.
Greater choiceTrade widens the range of goods and services available, including products that cannot be produced domestically at all (e.g. due to climate or resources).
Acquisition of resourcesCountries lacking certain natural resources, raw materials, or technology can import them rather than doing without.
More foreign exchange earningsExporting goods and services earns foreign currency, which can be used to pay for imports or to build reserves.
Access to larger marketsFirms can sell to the whole world rather than being limited to domestic demand, supporting higher output and investment.
Economies of scaleProducing for a larger (global) market allows firms to spread fixed costs over greater output, lowering average costs.
More efficient resource allocationCountries can specialise in producing what they are relatively good at, and trade for the rest — resources are used where they are most productive.
More efficient productionCompetitive pressure and specialisation encourage firms to cut waste and adopt best-practice production methods.
Real-world: Ghana earns the majority of its foreign exchange from cocoa exports, allowing it to import machinery, fuel, and manufactured goods it cannot efficiently produce domestically — a clear example of both "more foreign exchange earnings" and "acquisition of resources" working together.
Real-world: Cambodia's garment and footwear industry grew rapidly by gaining access to large export markets in the EU and North America, allowing factories to operate at a much larger scale — and at lower average cost — than Cambodia's small domestic market alone could ever support.
Real-world — trade and structural upgrading: South Korea's trade-driven development shows several of these benefits compounding over time. Under President Park Chung-hee's export-led industrialisation drive from the early 1960s, Korea first traded on the back of cheap, abundant labour — exporting wigs, textiles, and plywood. Foreign exchange earnings and access to larger (US and Japanese) markets then financed a push into heavier industry: steel (POSCO, founded 1968, first plant 1973) and shipbuilding through the 1970s. By the 1980s–90s, Samsung and LG were exporting semiconductors and electronics, and Korea's real GDP per capita rose from roughly $158 in 1960 to over $35,000 today — one of the fastest sustained rises from low-income to high-income status on record, built on successive rounds of trade-financed capital accumulation and technological upgrading.
Cross-reference: everything in this section describes the case for free trade. Subtopic 4.2 (Types of trade protection) and 4.3 (Arguments for and against trade protection) examine what happens when governments restrict these gains with tariffs, quotas, and subsidies — treat 4.1 and 4.2/4.3 as two halves of the same evaluative argument on Paper 1. Subtopic 4.4 (Economic integration) shows how regional and global agreements (e.g. the EU single market, ASEAN Free Trade Area) deliberately engineer larger markets to capture the "access to larger markets" and "economies of scale" benefits described here on a bloc-wide scale.

Free Trade Diagrams: Exports and Imports

Whether a country ends up exporting or importing a good under free trade depends on how its domestic equilibrium price compares to the world price.

World price
The price at which a good is bought and sold on the world market — assumed fixed for a small country that cannot influence it.
Free trade with world price (WP) below the domestic equilibrium price (Pd): at WP, domestic quantity demanded (Qd) exceeds domestic quantity supplied (Qs) — the shortfall is met by imports.
Free trade with world price (WP) above the domestic equilibrium price (Pd): at WP, domestic quantity supplied (Qs) exceeds domestic quantity demanded (Qd) — the surplus is sold abroad as exports.
Compare WP to Pd→ WP below Pd → imports→ WP above Pd → exports
Real-world: Peru's domestic price for refined copper products sits well below the world price for many manufactured electronics, so Peru imports these; but for raw copper ore itself, Peru's low production costs put its domestic price below the world price, making Peru a major copper exporter — the same country can simultaneously be an importer of some goods and an exporter of others, depending on where its domestic price sits relative to the world price for each.

Why free trade diagrams look the way they do today is itself a story of institutional history — tariff levels low enough for the world price line to matter this way did not exist by accident.

GATT signed 1947 (23 countries, average tariffs ~22%)→ 8 rounds of multilateral tariff-cutting (Kennedy Round 1960s; Tokyo Round 1970s; Uruguay Round 1986–94)→ World Trade Organization founded 1995 (123 founding members)→ average global tariffs fall to under 5%; world trade roughly triples as a share of world GDP since 1950
Real-world: Vietnam acceded to the WTO in January 2007, locking in low, predictable tariffs with its trading partners. Export-led growth followed rapidly: annual merchandise exports rose from around $48bn in 2007 to over $370bn by the early 2020s, driven especially by electronics assembly (Samsung alone accounts for a large share of Vietnam's total export value from its Bắc Ninh and Thái Nguyên plants) and garments — a direct illustration of how falling world tariffs (the WP line shifting closer to, or below, many countries' domestic prices) turns the theoretical exporter/importer diagram above into observed trade flows.

Economies of Scale and Access to Larger Markets

Two of the benefits above deserve closer attention because they explain why trade can raise a country's productive potential, not just its consumption choices.

Economies of scale
Falling average costs of production as output increases, often because fixed costs (machinery, R&D, infrastructure) are spread over a larger volume of output.

A firm selling only to a small domestic market may never produce enough output to fully exploit economies of scale. Trade removes this constraint: by selling into world markets, a firm can expand output far beyond what domestic demand alone would support, pushing average costs down and often improving the range and quality of what it can offer at a given price.

MechanismEffect
Larger production runsFixed costs (factory, machinery, R&D) spread over more units, lowering average cost per unit.
Specialised inputs become viableA larger scale of operation can justify investment in specialised machinery or supply chains that a small domestic-only firm could not justify.
Learning-by-doingHigher cumulative output over time can improve worker and managerial efficiency, further lowering costs.
Real-world: Malaysia's electronics and semiconductor manufacturing sector became internationally competitive largely by producing for global supply chains rather than Malaysia's domestic market alone — the scale made possible by export access allowed firms to invest in specialised equipment and drive down unit costs to globally competitive levels.
CountryMain export specialisationWhich benefit(s) this illustrates
VietnamElectronics assembly, garments and footwearAccess to larger markets; economies of scale; foreign exchange earnings
ChileCopper ore and concentratesAcquisition of foreign exchange from resource endowment; but exposed to world price swings
GhanaCocoa beansForeign exchange earnings funding acquisition of resources (machinery, fuel)
South KoreaSemiconductors, electronics, shipbuildingEconomies of scale from global demand; efficient/dynamic resource allocation over time
Exam tip: when asked to explain "economies of scale" as a benefit of trade, always link it explicitly to market size — it is access to a larger (global) market that allows the higher output needed to achieve those falling average costs, not trade in the abstract.
Cross-reference: regional trade blocs studied under 4.4 Economic integration (e.g. the EU single market, ASEAN Free Trade Area) are, in effect, a deliberate policy tool for engineering the "larger market" that this section shows is necessary for firms to capture economies of scale — integration turns many small national markets into one large one without needing every country to trade with the entire world.

Where this shows up on the papers: in a Paper 1 essay, part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain, with a correctly drawn and labelled free trade diagram, why access to world markets benefits a named country; part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) asks you to evaluate the benefits of trade, ideally weighing them against the trade protection arguments of 4.2/4.3. On Paper 2, candidates answer ONE question built from two linked Text extracts and several data tables: expect an (a)(i)/(ii) pair of 2-mark "define the term..." questions on bolded terms such as "world price" or "economies of scale" (each cited to an exact paragraph, e.g. "Text A, paragraph 2"), a (b) calculation such as the price gap (2-3 marks, sometimes paired with a 2-mark "sketch a free trade diagram" request), then a run of 4-mark "using the free trade diagram/information in [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each anchored to a specific paragraph or table, before a single 15-mark "discuss"/"evaluate" question weighing something specific. The HL-only skill of calculating exact export revenue or import expenditure from a diagram is tested only on Paper 3, where it appears as one of several short calculation sub-parts within an extended data set.
Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units (% or $)→ Interpret the answer in context
Price Gap (%) = ((World Price − Domestic Price) ÷ Domestic Price) × 100
Forward — Price Gap (World Price Above Domestic)
Domestic price = $40; world price = $46.
Price gap = ((46 − 40) ÷ 40) × 100 = +15% — the world price is 15% above the domestic price, so this country would export the good under free trade.
Reverse-Direction: Finding the World Price
Domestic price = $60; the world price is 20% below the domestic price. Find the world price.
World price = 60 × (1 − 0.20) = $48 — since the world price is below the domestic price, this country would import the good under free trade.
Given: domestic price + % gap→ World price = Domestic × (1 ± % gap)→ Sign of gap tells you import or export
Exam tips:
  1. Always state the direction of trade (import or export) as part of your answer, not just the percentage figure — the sign of the price gap is the economically meaningful part.
  2. When drawing a free trade diagram, always mark Qs and Qd separately at the world price line — do not simply label the domestic equilibrium quantity.
  3. Note that calculating the exact quantity of exports/imports and the resulting export revenue/import expenditure from a diagram is an HL-only skill — reserved for the HL extension file.
  4. Use specific, contrasting real-world examples (Ghana/Cambodia for benefits; Peru for simultaneous import/export; Malaysia for economies of scale) to anchor Paper 1 essays.
