Austerity: Cure, Cause — or Just Rebranded?

Why governments tighten their belts, what was cut, who pays for it, why cutting spending can make the debt problem worse, how it collides with central banks — and why no politician wants to say the word any more.

1

Theory: what is austerity?

Austerity (also called fiscal consolidation or fiscal tightening) is a deliberate policy of reducing the budget deficit through cuts to government spending and/or increases in taxation, usually sustained over several years.

Budget deficit

The gap in a single year when government spending is greater than tax revenue. It is a flow.

National debt

The total stock of past borrowing still owed. Every deficit adds to the debt.

Structural vs cyclical deficit

A cyclical deficit disappears as the economy recovers (tax receipts rise, benefit spending falls). A structural deficit remains even when the economy is at full capacity — this is what austerity targets.

Key point: austerity is contractionary fiscal policy. On an AD/AS diagram, falling government spending (G) and rising taxes (lower C) both shift AD to the left. That is exactly why it is so controversial when an economy is already weak.

The UK austerity story at a glance

2008–09 · Financial crisis. Bank bailouts, collapsing tax receipts and automatic stabilisers push the UK budget deficit to around 10% of GDP — a peacetime record.
March 2009 · Bank Rate cut to 0.5%, the lowest in the Bank of England's history, and quantitative easing (QE) begins.
2010 · The Coalition's Emergency Budget. Chancellor George Osborne sets out a plan to eliminate the structural deficit within a parliament, roughly 80% through spending cuts and 20% through tax rises. VAT rises from 17.5% to 20% (January 2011). The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) is created.
2010–2019 · The austerity decade. Deep real-terms cuts to unprotected departments and local government; public sector pay caps; a four-year freeze on most working-age benefits from 2016. Deadlines to balance the budget are repeatedly pushed back.
October 2018 · Theresa May announces that "austerity is over" at the Conservative conference.
2024–2026 · "No return to austerity". Labour pledges no return to austerity, while the 2025 Spending Review asks departments to deliver around £14bn of "efficiency gains" by 2028–29, and the Chancellor asks Cabinet ministers to find further savings.
2

The reasons for austerity

Why would a government choose to cut spending or raise taxes, knowing it will be unpopular?

① Reduce the deficit and debt

After 2008, UK debt rose from under 40% of GDP to over 80% within a few years. Supporters argued that borrowing on this scale could not continue indefinitely.

② Maintain market confidence

Governments borrow by selling bonds (gilts). If investors lose faith, they demand higher yields, raising the cost of borrowing. The 2010 Greek debt crisis was used as a warning of what could happen.

③ Reduce debt interest payments

Money spent servicing debt is an opportunity cost — it cannot be spent on schools, hospitals or defence. A higher debt means more of each year's budget goes to bondholders.

④ Create "fiscal space" for the next crisis

Lower debt gives a government room to borrow heavily when the next shock hits (as it later did in 2020 with Covid-19).

⑤ Avoid crowding out

The argument that heavy government borrowing competes with firms for funds and pushes up interest rates, reducing private investment. Shrinking the state should "crowd in" the private sector.

⑥ Ideology and the size of the state

Some politicians believe a smaller state is more efficient and that the private sector allocates resources better. Austerity can be a means of permanently reducing the state's share of GDP.

⑦ Intergenerational fairness

The claim that today's borrowing is a burden passed on to future taxpayers, who will have to repay it.

⑧ Meeting fiscal rules

Governments set themselves rules (e.g. debt falling as a share of GDP within five years). If forecasts worsen, spending restraint may be needed to hit them and maintain credibility.

The intellectual case in 2010

  • Reinhart and Rogoff (2010) argued that economic growth slows sharply once government debt exceeds about 90% of GDP. This was widely cited by supporters of austerity. In 2013, a graduate student, Thomas Herndon, found a spreadsheet error and other data choices in the study; once corrected, the "cliff edge" at 90% largely disappeared.
  • "Expansionary fiscal contraction" (associated with Alberto Alesina): the idea that cutting spending could actually boost growth by raising business and consumer confidence, lowering interest rates and signalling lower future taxes. Critics, including Paul Krugman, mocked this as relying on the "confidence fairy".

Tell me why…

Why?Why did the UK deficit rise so dramatically in 2009 even before any new spending programmes were announced?

Automatic stabilisers. In a recession tax receipts fall (lower incomes, profits and spending) and welfare spending rises (more unemployment). The cyclical deficit balloons without any government decision. Bank bailouts added further pressure.

Why?Why might a government care about bond yields if it can currently borrow cheaply?

Debt must be continually refinanced. If yields rise, every new gilt issued is more expensive, so debt interest grows and squeezes other spending. Rising yields can also push up mortgage and business borrowing rates across the economy, as the UK saw after the September 2022 mini-budget.

Why?Why was the comparison with Greece in 2010 arguably misleading?

Greece uses the euro, so it had no central bank of its own to buy its bonds or let its currency depreciate. The UK borrows in its own currency, has its own central bank (which was buying gilts through QE), and its debt had a long average maturity. The risk of a Greek-style buyers' strike was therefore much smaller.

Why?Why did the Coalition choose roughly 80% spending cuts and only 20% tax rises?

Partly ideological (a preference for a smaller state) and partly based on research suggesting spending-based consolidations were more durable and less damaging to growth than tax-based ones. Tax rises are also highly visible to voters, while spending cuts can be spread across services.

Reading the charts: children's and adult social care were barely cut, or even grew, because councils have a legal duty to provide them and demand was rising. That meant the discretionary services — youth clubs, Sure Start centres, libraries, buses, planning — took cuts of 45–67%. "Protecting" some budgets concentrates the pain on everything else.

Tell me why…

Why?Why did total council spending fall less (−19%) than most individual services?

Social care is the biggest part of council budgets and was close to protected. Because the largest item barely fell, the overall average stays relatively modest, even though smaller services were cut by half or more.

Why?Why did governments choose to protect the NHS rather than spread cuts evenly?

Health is the public's top priority, demand rises every year with an ageing population, and cuts would be highly visible (waiting lists, A&E). Protecting it was politically essential — but it meant deeper cuts elsewhere to hit the same total.

Why?Why are youth services and Sure Start among the easiest services to cut?

They are discretionary (no legal duty), their users have little political voice, and the costs of cutting them — worse outcomes for children, higher crime, more pressure on social care — appear years later, often in other budgets.

4

The consequences of austerity

Austerity has winners and losers, short-run and long-run effects — and the counterfactual (what would have happened otherwise) is always contested.

Positive consequences (the case for)

Deficit fell substantially

The UK deficit fell from around 10% of GDP in 2009–10 to roughly 1–2% by 2018–19 — though years later than originally planned.

Low borrowing costs

Gilt yields fell to historic lows in the 2010s. Supporters credit fiscal credibility; critics point out QE and the UK's safe-haven status mattered too.

Strong job creation

Unemployment fell from around 8% in 2011 to about 3.8% in 2019. Supporters argue the private sector more than replaced public sector job losses.

Fiscal space for later crises

Lower deficits meant the UK could borrow heavily for furlough and Covid support in 2020 without a market panic.

Pressure for genuine efficiency

Some services reformed, digitised or shared back-office functions they might never have changed with growing budgets.

Lower inflationary pressure

Contractionary fiscal policy reduces demand-pull inflation — relevant when inflation is high, as in 2022–23.

Negative consequences (the case against)

Slow recovery and lost output

The UK recovery after 2010 was one of the slowest on record. The OBR estimated early consolidation reduced GDP growth in 2010–12.

Falling real wages

Public sector pay caps and weak growth meant average real pay did not regain its 2008 peak for well over a decade.

Cuts to public investment

Capital spending (roads, school buildings, hospitals) is the easiest to cut, damaging long-run productivity and the supply side — contributing to the UK's "productivity puzzle".

Local government under strain

The National Audit Office estimated central government funding to English councils fell by around 49% in real terms between 2010–11 and 2017–18. Northamptonshire issued a Section 114 (effective bankruptcy) notice in 2018.

Social impacts and inequality

Benefit freezes hit lower-income households hardest. Food bank use rose sharply over the decade, and youth services, libraries and social care were cut.

Political consequences

Research by economist Thiemo Fetzer (2019) linked welfare cuts to rising support for UKIP and the Leave vote in 2016, suggesting austerity fuelled discontent and populism.

Evaluation — it depends on…
  • The size of the fiscal multiplier — small multipliers mean small output losses (see the Self-defeating tab).
  • The state of the economy — austerity during a boom (positive output gap) is far less damaging than during a recession.
  • Whether monetary policy can offset it — not possible when interest rates are already near zero.
  • The composition — cutting investment hurts long-run growth more than cutting current spending; cutting benefits for the poor (high MPC) hits demand more than taxing the rich (low MPC).
  • The counterfactual — we cannot know what would have happened to bond yields without austerity.

Tell me why…

Why?Why is capital investment usually the first casualty of austerity — and why is that a problem?

It is politically easy: delaying a road or a school rebuild causes no immediate job losses or visible service cuts. But public investment raises the economy's productive capacity, so cutting it shifts LRAS less far to the right in future — damaging long-run growth and the future tax base.

Why?Why could unemployment fall to record lows while real wages stagnated?

Many new jobs were low-paid, part-time, self-employed or zero-hours, and productivity growth was very weak. Firms took on cheap labour rather than investing in capital. Without productivity growth, sustained real wage growth is not possible.

Why?Why do cuts to local government often show up years later as higher costs elsewhere?

Cutting preventative services (social care, youth services, public health) can shift costs elsewhere: elderly patients stay longer in hospital because care packages are unavailable, which raises NHS spending. This is a "false economy" — a saving in one budget creating a larger cost in another.

Why?Why is freezing benefits in cash terms a spending cut, even though nobody's payment goes down?

If prices rise and the benefit stays the same in cash (nominal) terms, its real value falls. Inflation does the cutting quietly — a classic way to reduce spending without announcing a headline cut.

5

Intergenerational equity: who really pays?

Austerity was often justified as being fair to the next generation. But it is just as possible to argue that young people bore the heaviest burden of all.

Intergenerational equity is fairness in how the costs and benefits of economic policy are shared between generations: today's young, today's working-age adults, today's pensioners, and people not yet born.

✅ The case that austerity is fair to the young

  • Debt is a deferred tax. Borrowing today must be serviced by future taxpayers, who had no vote on how it was spent.
  • Debt interest crowds out the future. Every pound of interest paid by tomorrow's taxpayers is a pound not spent on their schools or hospitals.
  • An ageing population. Pensions, health and social care costs will rise sharply; reducing debt now leaves room to meet them.
  • Fiscal space for the next shock. Future generations will face their own crises (as with Covid in 2020) and need room to borrow.

❌ The case that austerity hit the young hardest

  • Education costs shifted to students. Tuition fees in England tripled to £9,000 a year in 2012, and the Education Maintenance Allowance was scrapped in England in 2011.
  • Youth services were cut most. Council spending on services for young people and Sure Start fell by around two-thirds per person.
  • Youth unemployment passed one million 16–24-year-olds in 2011, with scarring effects on lifetime earnings.
  • Pensioners were protected. The triple lock (from 2011) raised the state pension by the highest of earnings, inflation or 2.5%, while most working-age benefits were frozen from 2016.
  • Under-investment is a hidden debt. Cutting capital spending passed on crumbling buildings — in 2023 some schools had to close classrooms because of unsafe RAAC concrete.
Evaluation — it depends what the borrowing pays for:
  • Borrowing to invest (schools, rail, green energy) benefits future generations, so it is arguably fair that they help repay it. This is the logic of the "golden rule": borrow only to invest, not to fund day-to-day spending.
  • Borrowing for current spending (e.g. public sector pay, benefits) mainly benefits people today, shifting the bill forward.
  • Who owns the debt matters: if gilts are held by UK pension funds, future generations inherit the bonds as well as the debt — the issue becomes inequality within a generation.
  • Interest rates vs growth: when the interest rate on debt is below the growth rate of the economy, the debt-to-GDP ratio can fall on its own, making debt far less of a burden on future generations than headline figures suggest.

Tell me why…

Why?Why might cutting public investment be less fair to future generations than borrowing?

Future generations inherit the infrastructure as well as the debt. If a school or railway is not built, they lose the benefit and may face higher repair or rebuilding costs later, while the debt saving may be small, especially if borrowing costs were low.

Why?Why were pensioners protected from austerity while working-age benefits were frozen?

Older voters turn out to vote in much higher numbers. Governments also argued that pensioners cannot easily increase their income through work. The political economy of austerity often reflects who votes, not just economic need.

Why?Why does youth unemployment during a downturn create a long-term cost for the whole economy?

Young people who start their careers unemployed lose skills, experience and confidence — a "scarring" effect that lowers lifetime earnings and productivity. This is a form of hysteresis: it reduces future potential output and future tax revenue.

6

Why austerity can be self-defeating

The goal is to reduce debt as a share of GDP. But cutting spending also cuts GDP — the denominator. If GDP falls enough, the ratio can get worse.

The multiplier effect

A £1 cut in government spending reduces someone's income, who then cuts their spending, and so on. If the multiplier is 1.5, a £10bn cut reduces GDP by £15bn.

Lost tax revenue

Lower GDP means lower income tax, VAT and corporation tax receipts, and higher welfare spending. Part of every cut is "clawed back" by a weaker economy.

The paradox of thrift

If households, firms and the government all try to save at the same time, aggregate demand collapses and total saving may not rise at all. Keynes' paradox applies to governments too.

Hysteresis

Long spells of unemployment erode skills; cancelled investment never happens. A temporary demand shock can permanently lower potential output — and therefore future tax revenues.

The IMF's admission (2013): IMF chief economist Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh found that forecasters had assumed fiscal multipliers of around 0.5 during the crisis, when actual multipliers had been roughly 0.9 to 1.7. In other words, austerity had been far more damaging to growth than forecasts expected — exactly the condition for it to become self-defeating.

The negative multiplier

The multiplier works in reverse. A fall in an injection — such as a cut in government spending — causes a larger fall in national income. This is the negative (or reverse) multiplier.

Multiplier = 1 ÷ marginal propensity to withdraw, where the marginal propensity to withdraw is the marginal propensity to save + the marginal propensity to tax + the marginal propensity to import (equivalent to 1 ÷ (1 − marginal propensity to consume)).

Why the UK's multiplier may be smaller

The UK is an open economy with a high marginal propensity to import, so part of each lost pound of spending would have gone abroad anyway. Higher withdrawals mean a smaller multiplier.

Why it can be larger in a crisis

When households are worried about jobs they spend less of any income they receive, and when the central bank cannot cut rates further, nothing offsets the fall. The IMF's 2013 finding of multipliers up to 1.7 reflected these conditions.

Don't confuse: a negative multiplier (a spending cut reduces GDP by more than the cut) with a negative fiscal multiplier — the claim of "expansionary fiscal contraction" that a cut could actually raise GDP through higher confidence. Most post-2010 evidence did not support the latter.

Tell me why…

Why?Why can the debt-to-GDP ratio rise even if the government cuts spending and borrows less?

The ratio has two parts. Austerity reduces the numerator (debt) by a little but, via the multiplier, may reduce the denominator (GDP) by more. If GDP falls proportionally faster than debt, the ratio rises.

Why?Why is the multiplier likely to be larger in a recession than in a boom?

In a recession there is spare capacity, so lost demand translates straight into lost output rather than lower prices; more households are credit-constrained, so they spend any income change (high MPC); and the central bank may be unable to cut rates to offset it.

Why?Why does austerity matter less when interest rates are 5% rather than 0.5%?

At 5%, the Bank of England can cut rates to stimulate consumption and investment, replacing some of the lost government demand. At 0.5% that tool is largely exhausted, so fiscal tightening passes through to output far more fully.

Why?Why might austerity in many countries at once be worse than in one country alone?

One country can offset weaker domestic demand by exporting more. If all its trading partners (e.g. the Eurozone after 2010) are tightening at the same time, export demand falls too — there is no external escape route.

Debt neutralisation, debt interest and investment

The real cost of debt is not the headline figure but the interest paid on it every year. Reduce or "neutralise" that cost, and money is freed up for the things that actually raise living standards.

Debt neutralisation means stopping the debt burden from growing, or cancelling out part of its cost, so that less tax revenue is swallowed by debt servicing (interest payments). It can happen by:
  • running budget surpluses to pay debt down;
  • growing the economy faster than the interest rate on debt, so the debt-to-GDP ratio shrinks;
  • refinancing or restructuring debt at lower interest rates and longer maturities;
  • a central bank holding government bonds and returning the interest to the Treasury (as with QE);
  • inflation eroding the real value of debt fixed in cash terms.

Real examples of debt neutralisation

🇬🇧 Quantitative easing, 2009–2022

The Bank of England bought £895bn of bonds, much of it government debt. Interest on those gilts flowed back to the Treasury — nearly £124bn up to July 2022. This neutralised part of the debt interest bill in the 2010s, helping keep debt servicing low even as debt doubled.

The catch: once Bank Rate rose, the Bank paid more interest on reserves than it earned on gilts. The Treasury began covering losses (£15bn between October 2022 and April 2023), and the OBR estimated a lifetime net loss of around £63bn.

🇬🇷 Greece after 2012

Private bondholders accepted losses of more than half their bonds' value, and remaining debt was refinanced through European loans at low rates with very long maturities. Greece later repaid its IMF loans early. Debt has fallen to about 143.5% of GDP (early 2026), and regaining investment grade has lowered its borrowing costs further.

🇬🇧 Post-war Britain, 1945–1970s

UK debt was around 250% of GDP after the Second World War. It fell dramatically over the following decades — not through austerity, but through steady growth and inflation — while the country built the NHS, council housing and the motorway network.

⚠️ The reverse: when neutralisation unwinds

As interest rates rose from 2022, a debt that cost little to service became expensive. Around a quarter of UK gilts are index-linked to inflation, so the inflation spike of 2022–23 pushed interest costs up sharply. Debt interest is now one of the largest items of spending.

Public–private partnerships (PPP) and the Private Finance Initiative (PFI)

PFI is a type of public–private partnership in which a private consortium designs, builds, finances and often maintains a public asset (a hospital, school or prison). The government then pays an annual charge for 25–30 years. It began in 1992 and expanded rapidly from 1997.

Why it appealed in times of austerity

Most PFI debt did not appear in the government's headline borrowing figures. Governments could deliver new buildings now while appearing to keep debt down, and in theory transferred construction and maintenance risk to the private sector.

The hidden cost

The National Audit Office (2018) found over 700 deals with a capital value of around £60bn, annual charges of £10.3bn, and £199bn of future charges continuing into the 2040s. Private finance usually costs more than government borrowing, and fixed payments squeeze other spending when budgets are cut.

In 2012 George Osborne relaunched the model as PF2. In the October 2018 Budget, Chancellor Philip Hammond abolished PFI and PF2 for all new projects.

When projects are cancelled: the impact on employment

Building Schools for the Future (2010)

One of the first austerity decisions: in July 2010 the government scrapped the school rebuilding programme, cancelling projects covering around 715 schools. Construction firms lost contracts they had spent millions bidding for, and many pupils stayed in ageing buildings.

Carillion (2018)

A major government contractor and PFI partner collapsed in January 2018, with around 43,000 staff worldwide (around 19,000 in the UK) and about 450 public contracts. Hospital projects in Liverpool and the West Midlands were delayed by years, and thousands of subcontractor jobs were put at risk.

HS2's northern leg (2023)

The Birmingham to Manchester section was cancelled on cost grounds, removing planned construction work and supply-chain jobs in the North.

Why cancellations hit jobs so hard: construction is labour-intensive and buys mostly from domestic suppliers (concrete, steel, local trades), so it has a relatively high multiplier. A cancelled project means redundancies, lost apprenticeships and orders lost along the supply chain — the negative multiplier in action, often concentrated in particular regions.

Tell me why…

Why?Why is debt interest a pure opportunity cost for a government?

Unlike spending on teachers or roads, interest payments buy no new goods or services today. Every pound paid to bondholders is a pound that could have been spent on public services, investment or tax cuts.

Why?Why did QE make UK debt look cheaper in the 2010s than it really was in the long run?

While Bank Rate was near zero, the Bank earned more on its gilts than it paid on reserves, so profits flowed to the Treasury. When rates rose, that reversed: the government effectively swapped long-term fixed borrowing for borrowing at Bank Rate, which became expensive.

Why?Why could PFI be described as "austerity-friendly" but bad value for money?

It kept borrowing off the headline figures, allowing investment without appearing to increase debt. But the private sector borrows at higher interest rates than the government, and inflexible 30-year contracts locked in payments, so the total cost to taxpayers was usually higher.

Why?Why can cutting public investment be self-defeating even if it reduces the deficit today?

Investment raises the economy's productive capacity and future tax revenue. Cutting it saves money now but lowers future growth, so the debt-to-GDP ratio may end up higher than if the investment had gone ahead.

The tug of war: central bank vs government

Since 1997 the Bank of England has set interest rates independently to hit a 2% inflation target, while the Treasury decides on tax and spending. The two can pull together — or pull in opposite directions.

Austerity vs record-low interest rates: one foot on the brake, one on the accelerator

From 2009 to 2021 the Bank of England kept Bank Rate at or below 0.75%, cutting to 0.25% after the 2016 referendum and to 0.1% in March 2020, and bought £895bn of bonds through QE by 2021. Monetary policy was trying to boost aggregate demand. At the same time, fiscal policy was deliberately reducing it.

Austerity shifts AD left (AD₁ → AD₂). Ultra-low rates and QE try to shift it back, but with interest rates already near zero they only partly succeed (AD₃).

The zero lower bound

Normally a central bank offsets fiscal tightening by cutting interest rates. When Bank Rate is already 0.5% there is almost no room left — the economy is close to a liquidity trap, so the full multiplier hits output.

Weaker transmission

Cheap credit only boosts spending if households and firms feel confident enough to borrow. Austerity — job cuts, pay freezes, benefit cuts — damages exactly that confidence.

Asset prices, not output

With fiscal policy tight, QE did much of the work, boosting house and share prices. This favoured wealthier asset-owners and widened wealth inequality.

Ineffective policy mix

The two arms of macro policy pulled in opposite directions. Many economists argued that with borrowing so cheap, it was the ideal time for the government to borrow to invest, not cut.

Counter-argument: supporters argued the policy mix was deliberate. Tight fiscal policy kept gilt yields low and gave the Bank of England the confidence to keep rates low for longer. Mervyn King, Bank of England Governor in 2010, publicly backed the need for a deficit reduction plan. In this view, fiscal and monetary policy were complements, not rivals.
Fiscal dominance — when debt ties the central bank's hands
When government debt is high, every rise in interest rates directly increases the government's interest bill. Through QE, a large share of UK debt was effectively refinanced at Bank Rate, and index-linked gilts rise with inflation. This creates pressure on the central bank to keep rates lower than inflation alone would justify — the risk economists call fiscal dominance. Central bank independence exists precisely to resist it.
Evaluation — who should win the tug of war?
  • In a deep recession with interest rates near zero, many economists argue fiscal policy must lead, because monetary policy has little room left.
  • When inflation is high, the two need to pull together: loose fiscal policy forces the central bank to raise rates even higher (as markets showed in 2022).
  • Credibility matters: markets will punish a government seen to undermine its central bank's fight against inflation.
  • Coordination does not require the Bank to lose independence — but each side needs to understand how the other's choices affect it.

Tell me why…

Why?Why did gilt yields spike after the September 2022 mini-budget?

Large unfunded tax cuts meant more borrowing and more demand at a time when inflation was already above 10% and the Bank was raising rates. Markets expected higher inflation, higher interest rates and more debt, so they demanded higher yields to hold gilts.

Why?Why does a rise in Bank Rate now make austerity more likely?

Higher rates raise the government's debt interest bill and the cost of new borrowing. To stay within its fiscal rules, the government may then need spending cuts or tax rises — so tight monetary policy can push fiscal policy tighter too.

Why?Why might a central bank welcome fiscal discipline during a period of high inflation?

Tighter fiscal policy reduces aggregate demand, so the Bank does not need to raise interest rates as far to bring inflation back to target. That means lower mortgage costs and less damage to investment than relying on monetary policy alone.

9

Why politicians avoid the word "austerity"

In 2010 Osborne used the word openly. By 2024 every major party was promising "no return to austerity" — while still asking departments to find billions in savings.

It became electorally toxic

After a decade of cuts, voters associate the word with closed libraries, potholes, NHS waiting lists and food banks. Using it is now politically damaging.

Its record is contested

With the IMF revising its multiplier assumptions, Reinhart–Rogoff discredited and a weak 2010s recovery, defending "austerity" as economics is harder than in 2010.

Links to populism

Evidence that cuts fed the Brexit vote and anti-establishment parties made politicians wary of repeating the experience — or the label.

But fiscal pressure hasn't gone away

Debt is now close to 100% of GDP, debt interest costs are much higher than in the 2010s, and demands on health, defence and pensions keep growing. Restraint is still needed — it just needs a new name.

Efficiency (government definition): spending less to achieve the same or greater outputs, or achieving higher outputs while spending the same amount. A genuine efficiency saving keeps the service the same; a cut reduces the quantity or quality of the service.

The modern framing: "efficiency savings"

  • The 2025 Spending Review required departments to find at least 5% efficiency savings, including a 15% reduction in administrative costs, adding up to around £14bn of efficiency gains by 2028–29, monitored by a new Office for Value for Money.
  • Several departments still faced real-terms budget cuts over 2026–29, and much of the saving was back-loaded towards the end of the period.
  • Economists have warned that pencilled-in savings leave little room for further cuts, raising the chance of tax rises instead, and that savings relying on AI and digital transformation are speculative.
  • This isn't new: the 2004 Gershon Review promised £21.5bn of efficiency savings under Gordon Brown.

Evaluation: efficiency savings are not automatically "austerity in disguise". Technology, procurement reform and better data can deliver more for less. The test is whether outputs are maintained. Warning signs that a "saving" is really a cut: back-loaded targets with no detailed plan, budgets that fall in real terms while demand rises, rising waiting lists or falling service quality, and "unidentified" savings simply pencilled into forecasts.

Tell me why…

Why?Why might a government prefer "efficiency savings" to tax rises when it needs to cut borrowing?

Tax rises are visible and immediately felt by voters, often breaking manifesto promises. Efficiency savings sound costless — the implication is that money was being wasted — so they are much easier to sell politically.

Why?Why are efficiency targets often back-loaded to the end of a spending period?

Some savings genuinely take time (e.g. new IT systems must be built first). But back-loading also delays the pain beyond the next election and makes targets easier to write into forecasts than to deliver — so they can quietly become real cuts later.

Why?Why can a department be "efficiency saving" and still deliver a worse service?

If demand is rising (e.g. an ageing population needing care) while the budget is fixed, even a genuinely more efficient service may not keep up. Output per pound rises, but output per person in need falls.

The international context: Greece and the Eurozone crisis

The UK chose austerity. Greece had it imposed — in return for bailout loans, when no one else would lend to it. It is the most extreme modern example of austerity, and of the social conflict it can cause.

Junk bonds: credit rating agencies (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) grade how likely a borrower is to repay. Bonds rated BBB− (Baa3) or above are investment grade. Anything below is sub-investment grade — known as "junk". Many pension funds and banks are not allowed to hold junk bonds, so a downgrade causes a wave of forced selling, pushing bond prices down and yields up.

Why Greece? The reasons for austerity

Hidden deficits

In October 2009 the new Papandreou government revealed the deficit was far bigger than previously reported. The final figure for 2009 was over 15% of GDP, against a Eurozone limit of 3%.

A debt mountain

Debt was already around 127% of GDP in 2009. Years of cheap borrowing after joining the euro had funded public sector wages and pensions, while tax evasion kept revenues low.

No escape route

As a euro member, Greece could not devalue its currency to boost exports, or have its own central bank buy its bonds. Its only options were default, leaving the euro, or bailouts with conditions.

The Troika's conditions

The Troika — the European Commission, the European Central Bank and the IMF — lent Greece around €290bn across three bailouts (2010, 2012, 2015). Each came with strict austerity and reform conditions.

Timeline of the Greek crisis

Oct 2009 · The deficit is revealed to be far higher than reported. Markets panic.
27 April 2010 · S&P downgrades Greek government bonds to junk (BB+). Yields soar.
May 2010 · First bailout of €110bn agreed. A general strike follows; during the 5 May protests a bank in Athens is firebombed and three employees are killed.
2011 · The "Indignant Citizens" movement occupies Syntagma Square outside parliament for weeks. Police use tear gas to clear protesters.
Feb–Mar 2012 · Second bailout. Dozens of buildings are set alight in central Athens as parliament votes. Private bondholders accept losses of over half the value of their bonds — the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history. The minimum wage is cut by 22% (32% for under-25s).
May–June 2012 · Anti-austerity parties surge; the far-right Golden Dawn enters parliament.
Jan 2015 · Syriza, led by Alexis Tsipras, wins the election promising to end austerity. Finance minister Yanis Varoufakis clashes with the Eurogroup.
June–July 2015 · Banks close and capital controls limit cash withdrawals to €60 a day. In a referendum around 61% vote "Oxi" (No) to the bailout terms — but days later, facing exit from the euro ("Grexit"), Tsipras accepts a third bailout with even tougher conditions.
Aug 2018 · Greece formally exits its bailout programme.
2023 onwards · Greek bonds return to investment grade after more than a decade as junk. Debt has fallen to about 143.5% of GDP (early 2026) and agencies have continued to upgrade the rating.

The consequences

Economic collapse

Greek GDP fell by roughly a quarter between 2008 and 2013 — a depression on the scale of the USA in the 1930s.

Mass unemployment

Unemployment peaked at around 27% in 2013, with youth unemployment close to 60%.

Self-defeating debt

Despite deep cuts, debt rose from about 127% of GDP in 2009 to around 180% by the mid-2010s — because GDP shrank so fast.

Brain drain

Hundreds of thousands of mostly young, educated Greeks emigrated, reducing the economy's long-run productive capacity (LRAS).

Pensions and public services

Pensions were cut repeatedly, public sector jobs and wages slashed, and health spending sharply reduced, with shortages of medicines in hospitals.

Eventual recovery

Greece now runs primary budget surpluses, has repaid IMF loans early and has regained investment grade. Supporters argue the reforms (tax collection, pensions, privatisation) were painful but necessary.

Austerity and civil unrest
  • General strikes repeatedly shut down transport, schools and public services from 2010 to 2015.
  • Violent clashes outside parliament in Syntagma Square, with tear gas, petrol bombs and arson — including the deaths of three bank workers in May 2010.
  • Collapse of the political centre: the two main parties that had governed Greece for decades (PASOK and New Democracy) lost huge support; anti-austerity Syriza rose from the left and the neo-Nazi Golden Dawn from the far right (later declared a criminal organisation by a Greek court in 2020).
  • Conflict between countries: anger at Germany and the Troika, and resentment in creditor countries at "bailing out" Greece, damaged trust inside the Eurozone.
  • Democracy vs creditors: the 2015 referendum rejected austerity, yet the government had to accept tougher terms anyway — raising questions about who really controls economic policy inside a currency union.
Link to the Self-defeating tab: Greece is the textbook self-defeating case. It had all the conditions for a large multiplier — a deep recession, no independent monetary policy, no ability to devalue, and its main trading partners also cutting at the same time. The IMF later published its own evaluation admitting it had underestimated the damage austerity would do to Greek growth, and that debt relief should have come sooner.

Evaluation — why did outcomes differ?
  • Own currency or not: Iceland and the UK could devalue and use their central banks; Greece could not.
  • Openness and export strength: Ireland's export base let it grow its way out; Greece's small export sector meant falling domestic demand was not replaced.
  • Starting debt and its causes: Ireland's problem was a banking crisis; Greece had a long-run fiscal and tax-collection problem.
  • Political consent: austerity imposed from outside, with little public support, is more likely to cause unrest and be resisted.

Tell me why…

Why?Why did the junk downgrade in April 2010 make Greece's situation even worse?

Many investors were not allowed to hold junk bonds, so they sold. Bond prices fell and yields rose, making new borrowing unaffordable. Greece was shut out of markets and had to turn to the Troika — on its terms. Higher yields also made the debt even less sustainable, a self-reinforcing spiral.

Why?Why could the UK borrow cheaply in 2010 with a deficit of around 10% of GDP, while Greece could not?

The UK borrows in its own currency and has its own central bank, which was buying gilts through QE — so there was almost no risk of it being unable to pay in pounds. Greece borrowed in euros it could not create, so investors faced a genuine risk of default.

Why?Why is "internal devaluation" so painful compared with devaluing a currency?

Without a currency to devalue, the only way to become more competitive is to push down wages and prices directly. That means years of unemployment and falling incomes — and because debts are fixed in euros, falling incomes make them harder to repay.

Why?Why might austerity cause more civil unrest in Greece than in the UK?

The cuts were far deeper and faster, unemployment was more than three times higher, and the measures were seen as imposed by foreign creditors rather than chosen by Greek voters. The 2015 referendum result being overridden reinforced the sense that democracy had been ignored.
