Constructing a Weighted Price Index

HL only

The SL file introduced the CPI as a measure of the average price level based on a fixed basket of goods. At HL, you need to understand how a weighted price index like the CPI is actually built — because not every item in the basket matters equally to the average household.

Weight
The proportion of total household expenditure spent on a particular category of good or service, usually expressed as a percentage or index-point share.
Weighted price index
A price index in which each item's price change is multiplied by its expenditure weight before being summed, so categories that take up more of household spending have proportionally more influence on the overall index.

Constructing the index — the steps:

1. Survey households→ 2. Identify basket & weights→ 3. Collect prices each period→ 4. Multiply each price index by its weight→ 5. Sum to get the weighted average
Worked Example — Building a Weighted Price Index
A statistics agency in Vietnam surveys households and finds the following expenditure weights and price indices (base year = 100) for a simplified three-category basket:
CategoryWeight (%)Price Index (this year)Weighted Contribution
Food4011540 × 115 ÷ 100 = 46.0
Housing3510835 × 108 ÷ 100 = 37.8
Transport2512025 × 120 ÷ 100 = 30.0
Weighted price index = 46.0 + 37.8 + 30.0 = 113.8
Inflation rate = 113.8 − 100 = 13.8% since the base year — noticeably higher than a simple unweighted average of the three price indices (114.3 vs 113.8), because the largest weight (Food, 40%) rose by less than Transport, which carries less weight.
Reverse-Direction: Finding a Missing Weight
A basket has two categories: Food (weight w%, price index 110) and Everything Else (weight (100−w)%, price index 104). The overall weighted price index is known to be 106.4. Find w.
106.4 = w(1.10) + (100−w)(1.04)
106.4 = 1.10w + 104 − 1.04w = 104 + 0.06w
2.4 = 0.06w → w = 40% — Food carries a 40% weight in this basket.
Real-world: the Philippines' statistics authority (PSA) rebases its CPI basket and expenditure weights periodically using household income and expenditure surveys, shifting weight toward categories like mobile data and ride-hailing transport as spending patterns change — a reminder that a weighted index is only as accurate as its most recent weights.
Why this matters for policy: because low-income households typically spend a much higher share of their income on food and energy than high-income households, a single national weighted average CPI can understate the inflation experienced by the poorest — a criticism that resurfaces in the "GDP/CPI as a measure of well-being" debate from the SL file.

The Sustainability of Government (National) Debt

HL only
Budget deficit
A flow — government spending exceeds tax revenue within a single year, financed by borrowing.
Government (national) debt
A stock — the accumulated total of all past budget deficits (minus any surpluses), still owed by government at a point in time.
Debt-to-GDP ratio
Government debt expressed as a percentage of GDP, used to judge whether debt is sustainable relative to the size of the economy that must eventually service it.
Annual budget deficit→ adds to→ stock of national debt→ debt-to-GDP ratio rises if debt grows faster than GDP

Why sustainability depends on the growth-rate comparison: a rising debt-to-GDP ratio is not automatically a crisis — what matters is whether the economy's nominal GDP growth rate is above or below the government's average interest rate on its debt. If GDP grows faster than the debt (interest) burden, the ratio can stabilise or fall even while the government continues to run modest deficits.

Costs of a High Debt-to-GDP RatioExplanation
Rising debt-servicing costsInterest payments consume a growing share of the budget, crowding out spending on health, education or infrastructure.
Credit rating downgradesRating agencies may downgrade sovereign debt, raising the interest rate the government must pay on new borrowing — a self-reinforcing spiral.
Intergenerational burdenFuture taxpayers bear the cost of repaying debt used to fund current consumption rather than productive investment.
Crowding outHeavy government borrowing can raise interest rates economy-wide, discouraging private-sector investment.
Reduced policy spaceA government already heavily indebted has less room to run an expansionary fiscal policy in a future downturn.
Worked Example — Debt-to-GDP Ratio
A country has government debt of $840 billion and nominal GDP of $1,200 billion.
Debt-to-GDP ratio = (840 ÷ 1,200) × 100 = 70%
Reverse-Direction: Finding the Debt Level
A country's debt-to-GDP ratio is 90% and its nominal GDP is $650 billion. Find the level of government debt.
Debt = 0.90 × 650bn = $585 billion
Debt-to-GDP Ratio (%) = (Government Debt ÷ Nominal GDP) × 100
Real-world: Japan has run a debt-to-GDP ratio above 250% — the highest of any major economy — for over a decade, yet has not faced a debt crisis, because most of its debt is held domestically by Japanese households and institutions at very low interest rates. Contrast this with Sri Lanka, which defaulted on its sovereign debt in 2022 after a much lower debt-to-GDP ratio became unsustainable once it was combined with foreign-currency-denominated debt, a collapsing currency, and a sudden loss of investor confidence — illustrating that the composition and currency of debt matter as much as the headline ratio.
Exam tip: never assume a high debt-to-GDP ratio is automatically unsustainable, or a low one automatically safe. Examiners reward answers that discuss who holds the debt (domestic vs foreign), in what currency, at what interest rate, and how it compares to the economy's growth rate.

The Phillips Curve: The Unemployment–Inflation Trade-off

HL only

The SL file treated low unemployment and low/stable inflation as broadly separate objectives. At HL, the Phillips curve makes explicit that — at least in the short run — governments often face a trade-off between the two.

Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)
Shows an inverse relationship between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate: as demand-side policy pushes unemployment down, inflation tends to rise, and vice versa.

Where the curve came from: the Phillips curve was not built from theory first — it began as an empirical pattern found in historical data, decades before it became a standard policy tool.

1958: A. W. Phillips (LSE) plots UK wage inflation against unemployment, 1861–1957→ finds a strikingly stable inverse relationship across nearly a century of data→ 1960: Samuelson & Solow relabel the vertical axis "price inflation" and present it to US policymakers as a menu of choices→ 1960s: governments treat the trade-off as stable and exploitable, targeting a chosen point on the curve→ late 1960s–70s: Friedman and Phelps predict the trade-off will break down once workers build inflation into their wage expectations→ 1970s stagflation (rising unemployment and inflation together) appears to confirm them, motivating the SRPC/LRPC distinction below
Real-world: A. W. Phillips's original 1958 paper used over a century of UK data (1861–1957) and only ever plotted wage inflation, not general price inflation — the version taught today, linking unemployment to price inflation, was popularised two years later by Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow, whose article gave 1960s US policymakers the idea that they could simply pick a point on the curve. The subsequent breakdown of that "stable menu" during 1970s stagflation is precisely what the SRPC/LRPC distinction below was developed to explain.
Short-run Phillips curve: a movement along the curve from high unemployment/low inflation (6% U, 2% π) toward low unemployment/high inflation (2% U, 6% π) as expansionary demand-side policy is used.

An AD-side account of the same trade-off: expansionary policy that shifts AD to the right lowers unemployment (higher output, more jobs) but pushes the price level up — this is simply the demand-pull inflation mechanic from the SL file, viewed through the unemployment lens.

AD shifting right along a fixed SRAS: output rises from Y₁ to Y₂ (unemployment falls) while the price level rises from P₁ to P₂ (inflation) — the AD/AS mirror of the SRPC.
Real-world: Turkey's expansionary, low-interest-rate policy stance through the early 2020s ("Erdoganomics", also referenced in the SL 3.2 file) pushed unemployment down while inflation surged past 60% — a vivid real-world illustration of moving along the SRPC toward the low-unemployment, high-inflation end.
Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)
Vertical at the natural rate of unemployment (NRU) — in the long run, monetarist/new classical economists argue there is no trade-off: any demand-stimulus gain in employment is temporary, and inflation ends up permanently higher with unemployment back at the NRU.
A → B: expansionary policy moves unemployment below the NRU along SRPC₁ as workers are fooled by rising prices ("money illusion"). B → C: once wage expectations catch up, unemployment rises back to the NRU, but now on a higher SRPC₂ — inflation stays permanently higher with no lasting fall in unemployment.
A: at NRU, inflation π₁→ B: stimulus cuts U below NRU, along SRPC₁→ C: expectations adjust, U returns to NRU on SRPC₂, inflation now π₂ > π₁
The monetarist/new classical conclusion: because the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the NRU, demand-management policy cannot permanently lower unemployment below its natural rate — it can only trade a temporary employment gain for a permanently higher inflation rate. This directly parallels the monetarist/new classical view of self-correcting LRAS from the SL 3.2 file: any output gain beyond the economy's productive potential is, in their view, temporary.
The Keynesian counterpoint: Keynesian economists are more sceptical that the economy always returns neatly to the NRU — persistent involuntary unemployment (hysteresis) can occur if demand stays weak for long enough that unemployed workers' skills atrophy or they exit the labour force altogether, permanently raising the NRU itself rather than the economy self-correcting back to an unchanged natural rate.
Real-world: Denmark's "flexicurity" labour market (referenced in the SL file) is often cited as evidence that active labour-market policy — retraining and job-search support alongside generous benefits — can help keep actual unemployment close to the NRU even after demand shocks, reducing the risk of hysteresis compared with economies offering less retraining support.

Where this shows up on the papers: this HL-only content (weighted price index, debt sustainability, the Phillips curve) is examinable only on Paper 3 (60 marks, two compulsory ~30-mark structured questions built around an extended data set of several tables). Each question runs 7–8 short 2-mark calculation/short-answer sub-parts that climb in difficulty (e.g. a forward calculation such as building a weighted index from given weights and price indices or finding a debt-to-GDP ratio, then a reverse-direction version — find a missing weight, price index, or GDP level), with one sub-part near the end typically a 4-mark "using a [named] diagram, explain..." question — often referencing named real-world cases such as Japan, Sri Lanka or Turkey. The final sub-part is always a single 10-mark question framed as "Using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to address/reduce [specific problem]" — note this asks you to recommend a policy, not "evaluate" or "discuss", though a top-mark answer still justifies the recommendation and acknowledges a counterargument or limitation. The same content can also appear as AO2/AO3 material within a Paper 1 essay on demand management (3.5, 3.6), where the Phillips curve or debt sustainability is used to evaluate a policy's side effects.
Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units (% or $)→ Interpret the answer in context
Weighted Price Index = Σ (Weight × Price Index) ÷ 100
Forward — Weighted Price Index
Two categories: Category A (weight 60%, price index 112) and Category B (weight 40%, price index 105).
Weighted index = (60 × 112 ÷ 100) + (40 × 105 ÷ 100) = 67.2 + 42.0 = 109.2 → inflation of 9.2% since the base year.
Reverse — Finding a Missing Price Index
Category A (weight 70%, price index unknown) and Category B (weight 30%, price index 106). The overall weighted index is 110.8. Find Category A's price index.
110.8 = 0.70(x) + 0.30(106) = 0.70x + 31.8
79.0 = 0.70x → x = 112.9
Debt-to-GDP Ratio (%) = (Government Debt ÷ Nominal GDP) × 100
Forward — Debt-to-GDP
Government debt = $960bn; nominal GDP = $1,600bn.
Debt-to-GDP = (960 ÷ 1,600) × 100 = 60%
Reverse — Finding GDP
Debt-to-GDP ratio = 75%; government debt = $450bn. Find nominal GDP.
GDP = 450 ÷ 0.75 = $600 billion
Given: ratio + debt→ GDP = Debt ÷ Ratio→ check units are $ billions
Exam tips:
  1. Always show the weight-multiplication step explicitly for a weighted-index question — examiners award marks for method, not just the final number.
  2. Never confuse the deficit (flow, this year) with the debt (stock, accumulated) — a common error even at HL.
  3. When discussing debt sustainability, refer to at least one qualitative factor (holder, currency, interest rate vs growth rate) alongside any calculation — this is what separates a Level 2 from a Level 4/5 evaluative response.
  4. On Phillips curve diagrams, always label whether you are showing a movement along the SRPC (short-run trade-off) or a shift of the SRPC itself (changed inflation expectations) — examiners frequently ask you to distinguish the two.
  5. Use real-world examples like Turkey (SRPC trade-off in action) or Japan/Sri Lanka (contrasting debt sustainability) to anchor evaluative points in Paper 1 essays as well as Paper 3 calculations.
