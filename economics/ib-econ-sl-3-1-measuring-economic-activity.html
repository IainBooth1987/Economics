National Income Accounting and the Circular Flow

Before governments can manage an economy, they first need to measure it. National income accounting is the framework economists use to record the total value of economic activity in a country over a given period, usually a year.

There are three ways of measuring the same total flow of economic activity — they are, in theory, always equal to one another:

Income Approach
Adds up all income earned by households and firms in the economy — wages, rent, interest and profit.
Output Approach
Adds up the value of all final goods and services produced in the economy, avoiding double-counting by using only value added at each stage of production.
Expenditure Approach
Adds up all spending on final goods and services: consumption (C) + investment (I) + government spending (G) + net exports (X − M).
Equivalence: Income = Output = Expenditure. Every pound spent by a household on a final good (expenditure) is received by a firm as revenue, which becomes value added (output), which is ultimately paid out to someone as income (wages, rent, interest, profit). The three approaches are three different ways of counting exactly the same flow of money around the economy.

This equivalence is best understood through the circular flow of income model, which shows how income flows between the key decision-makers in the economy, and where money leaks out of, or is injected back into, that flow.

Fig: the circular flow of income. Households supply factors of production to firms and receive income; firms produce goods and services households buy with consumption spending (C). Three leakages withdraw money from the flow — tax (T), savings (S), imports (M) — and three injections add spending back in — government spending (G), investment (I), exports (X). Equilibrium requires total injections (J) = total leakages (L).

The Five Sectors of the Circular Flow

The diagram above is built from five distinct decision-making sectors, each playing a different role in the flow of income and spending. A strong answer names the specific sector responsible for a leakage or injection, rather than describing the economy as an undifferentiated whole.

Households
Own all factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and supply them to firms in exchange for income (wages, rent, interest, profit); in turn, spend that income on firms' output as consumption (C), save part of it (S), and pay tax on it (T).
Firms
Combine factors of production supplied by households to produce goods and services, paying households income in return; undertake investment spending (I) — spending on capital goods such as machinery, buildings and technology, rather than on final consumer goods.
Government
Withdraws money from the flow through taxation (T) — a leakage — and injects money back in through government spending (G) on public services, benefits and infrastructure; the balance between the two determines the government's budget position (see 3.6).
Financial Sector
Banks and other financial institutions that hold households' savings (S) — a leakage from the flow — and channel funds to firms as loans for investment (I) — an injection back into the flow; a well-functioning financial sector is what allows one household's saving to become another firm's investment funding (see the credit-crunch discussion in 3.5, where this channel breaks down).
International (Foreign) Sector
Domestic spending on imports (M) is a leakage, since that money flows abroad to pay foreign producers rather than circulating domestically; foreign spending on the country's exports (X) is an injection, since it brings money in from abroad (see the companion Unit 4 material on international trade).

Why the Sector Breakdown Matters for Evaluation: if total injections exceed total leakages (J > L), national income rises — more money is being added to the circular flow each period than is being withdrawn from it — and vice versa if leakages exceed injections (L > J). This J = L equilibrium condition is the direct link between the circular flow model covered here and the AD/AS and multiplier analysis covered in 3.2 and 3.6: government fiscal policy, for instance, is really just a deliberate attempt to change G (an injection) and T (a leakage) to move national income towards a policy target.

The History and Purpose of National Income Accounting

GDP feels like a natural, timeless statistic, but it is a comparatively recent — and deliberately engineered — invention, built for a specific policy purpose rather than handed down as a neutral fact about the world.

1
Simon Kuznets and the 1934 US national income report

Facing the Great Depression, the US Congress commissioned economist Simon Kuznets (Department of Commerce) to produce the first systematic national income estimates for the United States. Kuznets delivered his report, National Income, 1929–32, in 1934 — but he explicitly warned Congress that "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income," a caveat that would be largely forgotten as GDP grew into the default proxy for national success (see Section 5).

2
Bretton Woods and the demand for comparable statistics (1944)

The 1944 Bretton Woods conference created the IMF and World Bank specifically to manage a new, rules-based international economic order — but doing so required member countries' economic performance to be measured on a broadly comparable basis, driving demand for standardised national accounting methods well beyond the US.

3
The UN System of National Accounts (from 1953)

The United Nations published the first international System of National Accounts (SNA) in 1953, giving countries a common methodology so their GDP figures could be meaningfully compared. It has been revised periodically since — in 1968, 1993 and 2008 — each time updating what counts as economic "output" as the structure of real economies changed.

4
The boundary of GDP keeps shifting

What counts as measurable "output" is a policy choice, not a fixed law of nature. In a widely-discussed 2014 methodology change, the UK's Office for National Statistics began including estimates of illegal drug sales and prostitution in official GDP, in line with the EU's 2010 SNA update — instantly and mechanically adding an estimated £10bn or so to measured UK GDP without a single extra good or service actually being produced, a vivid illustration of how much of "the size of the economy" depends on definitional choices rather than underlying reality.

Why the History Matters for Evaluation: Kuznets' own 1934 warning is one of the sharpest possible pieces of exam evidence against treating GDP as a welfare measure — the statistic's own inventor said explicitly that it should not be used that way, decades before the OECD Better Life Index, Happiness Index or Happy Planet Index existed as alternatives (Section 5).
★ REAL WORLD: The Informal Economy and GDP Undercounting — Nigeria and India
National income accounting relies on being able to record economic transactions, which becomes very difficult where a large share of activity happens in the informal economy — unregistered street vendors, subsistence farming, cash-in-hand labour, and small unregistered firms that never appear in official tax or business records. The International Labour Organization (ILO) has estimated that informal employment accounts for the substantial majority of total employment in Nigeria, and a large share — commonly estimated at somewhere around half — of India's workforce. Because most national income accounting relies on data from registered firms, tax records and formal payrolls, official GDP figures in economies with a large informal sector are widely believed to significantly understate the true scale of economic activity — a recurring evaluation point whenever GDP is used to compare living standards between high-income and developing economies.
★ REAL WORLD: India's 2016 Demonetisation — An Attempt to Force the Informal Economy into View
In November 2016, India's government under Prime Minister Narendra Modi abruptly withdrew ₹500 and ₹1,000 banknotes — around 86% of the cash then in circulation — from legal tender with only a few hours' notice, officially to combat "black money" (untaxed, unrecorded income) and counterfeit currency. The policy was intended, in part, to force informal and undeclared economic activity into the formal, recorded, taxable economy, since holders of large amounts of undeclared cash could not simply deposit it without explaining its source. In practice, economists remain sharply divided on its success: officially measured GDP growth slowed markedly in the following quarters, and independent researchers have since debated how much of the intended shift from informal to formal activity actually took place versus how much economic activity was simply disrupted and lost — a useful, concrete illustration of how difficult and costly it can be for a government to try to "solve" the GDP-undercounting problem described above by administrative force rather than gradual formalisation.

GDP and GNI — Nominal and Real

The expenditure approach gives us Gross Domestic Product (GDP) — by far the most widely used single measure of national output.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period, regardless of who owns the factors of production used.
Gross National Income (GNI)
GDP, adjusted to reflect income earned by a country's residents rather than the geographic location of production: GNI = GDP + net income from abroad (income earned by residents from assets held overseas, minus income earned domestically by foreign residents and repatriated abroad).
Nominal Values
GDP or GNI measured using current prices — not adjusted for inflation, so part of any rise from one year to the next may simply reflect rising prices rather than more output.
Real Values
GDP or GNI adjusted for inflation using a price deflator, isolating the genuine change in the physical quantity of output produced.
Nominal GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating Nominal GDP (Expenditure Approach)

Data: C = $450bn, I = $120bn, G = $200bn, X = $90bn, M = $110bn.

Step 1: Net exports (X − M) = 90 − 110 = −$20bn.

Step 2: Nominal GDP = 450 + 120 + 200 + (−20) = $750bn.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding a Missing Component from GDP

Question: Nominal GDP = $750bn. C = $450bn, I = $120bn, G = $200bn, M = $110bn. Find X.

Step 1: Rearrange: X = GDP − C − I − G + M.

Step 2: X = 750 − 450 − 120 − 200 + 110 = $90bn.

Any single component of GDP can be isolated this way — always rearrange the expenditure formula rather than trying to memorise a separate formula for each missing variable.

GNI = GDP + Net Income From Abroad
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating GNI

Data: GDP = $750bn. Income earned abroad by this country's residents = $40bn. Income earned domestically by foreign residents (and repatriated abroad) = $65bn.

Step 1: Net income from abroad = 40 − 65 = −$25bn.

Step 2: GNI = 750 + (−25) = $725bn.

Here GNI is lower than GDP — a sign that more income generated inside this country's borders is flowing out to foreign owners of capital than the reverse, a pattern typical of economies that host large amounts of foreign-owned production (see the Ireland example below).

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding Net Income From Abroad

Question: GDP = $900bn. GNI = $915bn. Find net income from abroad, and state whether income earned domestically by foreign residents is larger or smaller than income earned abroad by this country's residents.

Step 1: Rearrange: Net income from abroad = GNI − GDP.

Step 2: Net income from abroad = 915 − 900 = +$15bn.

Because net income from abroad is positive, this country's residents earn more from assets held overseas than foreign residents earn domestically and repatriate abroad — the opposite pattern to Ireland (below), and closer to the Qatar/remittance-receiving-country case discussed next.

★ REAL WORLD: Ireland's GDP–GNI Gap ("Leprechaun Economics")
Ireland's GDP figures are dramatically inflated relative to its GNI because many large multinational technology and pharmaceutical firms book global profits through Irish subsidiaries to take advantage of Ireland's low corporate tax rate (see 3.7), even though the underlying production and employment mostly occurs elsewhere. In 2015, Irish GDP grew by an astonishing 26% in a single year — a figure widely mocked by Nobel laureate Paul Krugman as "leprechaun economics" in a 2016 blog post — driven almost entirely by a small number of large multinationals (most prominently, though not officially confirmed at the time, linked to Apple) relocating intellectual property assets onto the Irish balance sheet, not any genuine change in Irish output or living standards. Because this profit is repatriated abroad to foreign shareholders, it counts in Irish GDP (produced within Ireland's borders) but not in Irish GNI (income belonging to Irish residents) — illustrating precisely why the GDP/GNI distinction matters. The distortion became so severe that Ireland's Central Statistics Office (CSO), acting on the advice of a 2016 expert group chaired by Central Bank of Ireland governor Philip Lane, introduced an entirely new modified measure, GNI* (pronounced "GNI-star"), from 2017 onwards — stripping out the depreciation of foreign-owned intellectual property and aircraft leasing assets specifically to give a more realistic picture of the income actually available to the Irish economy. By the early 2020s, Irish GNI* was consistently running at little more than half the level of headline Irish GDP, one of the largest such gaps recorded anywhere in the world.
★ REAL WORLD: The Reverse Case — Qatar and Remittance-Sending Economies
Qatar's economy relies heavily on a very large temporary migrant workforce — migrant workers have made up the large majority of Qatar's total population for years, a share that grew further during the construction boom preceding the 2022 FIFA World Cup — who produce output that counts fully in Qatari GDP, but who send a substantial share of their wages home as remittances rather than spending or investing them domestically. From the perspective of the migrant workers' home countries (including the Philippines, India, Nepal, Bangladesh and several other South and Southeast Asian economies), the opposite GDP/GNI gap occurs: a portion of GNI (income earned abroad by their own residents) exceeds what those countries' own domestic GDP would suggest. According to the World Bank's Migration and Development Brief series, the Philippines and India have for years ranked among the very largest recipients of remittances in the world in absolute dollar terms, and for smaller, more remittance-dependent economies such as Nepal and Tajikistan, remittances have at times been estimated at over a quarter of GNI — a scale large enough to noticeably widen the gap between that country's GDP and its (higher) GNI. Together, the Ireland and Qatar/remittance cases show that the GDP–GNI gap can run in either direction, depending on whether a country is a net exporter of capital income (Ireland) or a net exporter of labour (the Philippines, India, Nepal).
Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ GDP Deflator) × 100
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating Real GDP Using a Price Deflator

Data: Nominal GDP (Year 2) = $800bn. GDP deflator (Year 2, Year 1 = base year of 100) = 125.

Step 1: Real GDP = (800 ÷ 125) × 100 = $640bn.

Nominal GDP rose to $800bn, but once the 25% rise in the price level is stripped out, the economy's genuine (real) output is only $640bn — meaningfully below the nominal headline figure.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the GDP Deflator

Question: Nominal GDP = $800bn. Real GDP = $640bn. Find the GDP deflator.

Step 1: Rearrange: GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100.

Step 2: Deflator = (800 ÷ 640) × 100 = 125.

Paper 3 may give you any two of the three values (nominal GDP, real GDP, deflator) and ask you to find the third — always start from the same triangle of the formula and rearrange.

Exam Tip: the same real-vs-nominal logic applies identically to GNI: Real GNI = (Nominal GNI ÷ GNI deflator, or the same GDP deflator, if instructed) × 100. Don't assume a question about "real" values only ever concerns GDP.

Per Capita and PPP Measures

Total GDP or GNI tells us the size of an economy, but says nothing about the living standards of the average person within it — a large population can produce a large total GDP while individuals remain poor. Per capita measures correct for this by dividing by population.

GDP (or GNI) per capita = Total GDP (or GNI) ÷ Population
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Real GDP Per Capita

Data: Real GDP = $640bn. Population = 32 million.

Step 1: Real GDP per capita = $640,000,000,000 ÷ 32,000,000 = $20,000.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding Population from Per-Capita Data

Question: Real GDP = $450bn. Real GDP per capita = $18,000. Find the population.

Step 1: Rearrange: Population = Real GDP ÷ Real GDP per capita.

Step 2: Population = 450,000,000,000 ÷ 18,000 = 25,000,000 (25 million).

Paper 3 may give any two of total GDP, per-capita GDP, and population and ask for the third — the same rearranging skill used for the deflator triangle applies here too.

★ REAL WORLD: Total GDP vs. Per Capita GDP — China and Luxembourg
China has the world's second-largest total GDP by most measures — in the tens of trillions of dollars, and larger still when converted at PPP — far exceeding that of Luxembourg, a country of under a million people. Yet, because China's population is over a thousand times larger than Luxembourg's, China's GDP per capita sits far below Luxembourg's, which has for years ranked among the very highest GDP-per-capita figures in the world, boosted further by a large share of its workforce commuting in daily from neighbouring France, Belgium and Germany (whose output is counted in Luxembourg's GDP but whose population is not counted in its denominator, mechanically inflating its GDP per capita relative to a country without such heavy cross-border commuting). This is precisely why total GDP and GDP per capita answer two different questions: total GDP is a better guide to a country's overall economic and geopolitical weight (market size, government tax base, military spending capacity — China's total GDP underpins its position as the world's second-largest defence spender), while GDP per capita is a far better guide to the average material living standard of an individual resident.

Comparing GDP per capita across countries using market exchange rates can still be misleading, because the same amount of money buys very different quantities of goods and services in different countries — a haircut or a restaurant meal is far cheaper in Jakarta than in Zurich. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for this by converting GDP using an exchange rate based on the relative cost of an equivalent basket of goods in each country, rather than the market (currency market) exchange rate.

GDP/GNI Per Capita at PPP
GDP or GNI per capita converted using a PPP exchange rate, so that a dollar of PPP-adjusted income reflects an equivalent quantity of real goods and services regardless of where it is earned — correcting for the fact that price levels differ systematically between countries (lower-income countries typically have a lower cost of living).
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Adjusting GDP Per Capita to a PPP Basis

Data: Country A's GDP per capita at the market exchange rate is $5,000. A PPP survey finds that the same basket of goods costing $1 in the United States costs the equivalent of only $0.40 in Country A (its PPP conversion factor is 0.4).

Step 1: PPP GDP per capita = Market-exchange-rate GDP per capita ÷ PPP conversion factor.

Step 2: PPP GDP per capita = 5,000 ÷ 0.4 = $12,500.

Because the cost of living in Country A is much lower than in the United States, $5,000 of market-exchange-rate income buys as much there as $12,500 would in the US — PPP adjustment reveals this and narrows the apparent living-standards gap between rich and poor countries relative to using market exchange rates alone.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the PPP Conversion Factor

Question: Country B's GDP per capita at the market exchange rate is $8,000. Its GDP per capita at PPP is $20,000. Find the PPP conversion factor.

Step 1: Rearrange: PPP conversion factor = Market-exchange-rate GDP per capita ÷ PPP GDP per capita.

Step 2: PPP conversion factor = 8,000 ÷ 20,000 = 0.4.

A conversion factor of 0.4 means the same basket of goods costing $1 in the US costs the equivalent of only $0.40 in Country B — confirming a much lower cost of living, consistent with why its PPP-adjusted GDP per capita is so much higher than its market-exchange-rate figure.

★ REAL WORLD: The Big Mac Index
The Economist magazine invented the Big Mac Index in September 1986, originally intended half as a serious economics tool and half as "a light-hearted guide," in the magazine's own words, to whether currencies are trading at their "correct" level — yet it has since been cited in academic papers and central bank research precisely because it is such a simple, popular illustration of the PPP concept. It compares the price of an identical Big Mac hamburger, converted into US dollars at market exchange rates, across many countries. Because a Big Mac is broadly the same product everywhere (locally-sourced ingredients, similar production process, similar restaurant format), systematic price differences suggest a currency is under- or over-valued relative to PPP. A Big Mac has typically cost noticeably less in economies such as India, Egypt or South Africa than in Switzerland or Norway when converted at market exchange rates — implying, on the Big Mac Index's own simple logic, that the Swiss franc and Norwegian krone are "overvalued" against the dollar while the Indian rupee and Egyptian pound are "undervalued." This is a simple, popular proxy for exactly the kind of adjustment that formal PPP-adjusted GDP per capita statistics make on a far larger, more rigorous scale using a broad basket of goods and services rather than a single product — though critics note a Big Mac's price also reflects non-tradeable local costs (rent, wages, local regulation) as much as pure currency misalignment, a caveat that applies equally to formal PPP statistics.

The Business Cycle

Real GDP does not grow smoothly over time. It fluctuates around its long-run trend in a recurring pattern known as the business (economic) cycle — alternating between periods of above-trend growth (boom) and below-trend growth or outright contraction (recession).

Boom / Peak
Real GDP is above its long-run trend; unemployment is low and inflationary pressure tends to build.
Recession
A period of falling real GDP — conventionally defined as at least two consecutive quarters of negative growth; unemployment rises.
Trough
The low point of the cycle, where output stops falling and begins to recover.
Recovery
Real GDP begins rising again, moving back towards (and eventually above) the long-run trend.
Long-Term (Trend) Growth
The underlying, sustained rise in an economy's potential output over time, driven by growth in the quantity and quality of factors of production — distinct from the short-term fluctuations of the cycle itself.
Fig: the business cycle — actual real GDP fluctuates around a smoother, upward-sloping long-run trend growth path, moving through boom, recession, trough and recovery phases.
Who Actually Decides When a Recession Started and Ended? In the United States, it is not a simple, mechanical "two negative quarters" rule that officially dates a recession, but the National Bureau of Economic Research's (NBER) Business Cycle Dating Committee — a small panel of academic economists who assess a broader range of monthly indicators (employment, industrial production, real income, retail sales) and only formally declare a recession's start and end months, often well after the event. The NBER dated the COVID-19 recession as beginning in February 2020 and ending in April 2020 — just two months — making it, by this measure, the shortest US recession on record, despite its severity, illustrating that "recession" is about the depth and breadth of the downturn as much as its raw length.
★ REAL WORLD: Japan's "Lost Decades" vs. South Korea's Growth Trajectory
Japan experienced a strikingly flat pattern of economic activity from the early 1990s onward — often called the "Lost Decades" — after the collapse of a huge asset price bubble in property and stocks (Japanese property and stock prices roughly quadrupled during the late-1980s "bubble economy," before the Nikkei stock index lost around 60% of its value between 1990 and 1992 alone) left banks, firms and households burdened with debt for years, keeping actual GDP persistently close to, or below, a very slow-growing trend line (see the credit-crunch discussion in 3.5). South Korea, over a similar and slightly earlier period, showed the opposite pattern: it also suffered a sharp, deep recession (the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis, which forced Korea into an IMF bailout program and a sudden, painful restructuring of its heavily-indebted conglomerates, the chaebol), but its longer-run trend growth rate remained far higher, so its business cycle fluctuations occurred around a much steeper underlying trend line, and Korea had returned to strong growth within a few years. Comparing the two shows that the business cycle (short-run fluctuation) and the long-run trend (potential output growth) are genuinely separate concepts: an economy can have severe cyclical volatility with either a strong or a weak underlying growth trend.
★ REAL WORLD: The 2007–09 Global Financial Crisis
The most severe global recession since the Great Depression began with rising defaults on US "subprime" (high-risk) mortgages from 2007, which spread into a full-blown global banking crisis after the collapse of investment bank Lehman Brothers in September 2008 — the largest bankruptcy filing in US history at the time. The NBER dated the US recession itself from December 2007 to June 2009, but its effects were felt for years afterwards across the global economy, prompting an unprecedented coordinated policy response covered in detail in 3.5 (monetary policy) and 3.6 (fiscal policy) — including near-zero interest rates and the first major use of quantitative easing by several major central banks.

Is GDP/GNI a Good Measure of Well-Being?

GDP and GNI are used constantly for two purposes they were never perfectly designed for: comparing a single country's living standards over time, and comparing living standards between different countries. Both uses face significant limitations.

✓ Strengths of GDP/GNI as a Measure

Widely available and comparable

National income statistics are collected using broadly standardised international methodology, making comparisons across countries and over time far more consistent than most alternatives.

Strong correlation with many welfare indicators

Higher GDP per capita is generally correlated with longer life expectancy, higher literacy, and better access to healthcare and clean water, especially at lower income levels.

✗ Limitations of GDP/GNI as a Measure

Ignores income distribution

A rising GDP per capita can mask a growing gap between rich and poor if growth is concentrated among a small share of the population.

Excludes non-market activity

Unpaid domestic labour, caregiving, and subsistence production are not counted, even though they represent genuine economic value and well-being.

Ignores externalities

GDP counts the value of output but not the environmental or social costs (pollution, resource depletion, congestion) that production and consumption may also generate.

Says nothing about quality of life directly

Working hours, job security, political freedom, physical and mental health, and social trust all affect well-being but are not captured by an output statistic.

Comparisons over time distorted by inflation and population change

Nominal GDP growth can overstate genuine progress unless carefully adjusted to real, per-capita terms — and even then captures only material output, not welfare.

Because of these limitations, several alternative measures of well-being have been developed to sit alongside — not necessarily replace — GDP and GNI statistics:

OECD Better Life Index
A composite index combining 11 dimensions of well-being (including housing, income, jobs, health, education, environment and life satisfaction) across OECD member countries, letting users weight dimensions according to their own priorities rather than imposing a single ranking.
Happiness Index
Most prominently the World Happiness Report, which ranks countries by self-reported life satisfaction survey data, alongside supporting factors such as GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom and trust in institutions.
Happy Planet Index
Combines reported well-being, life expectancy and each country's ecological footprint into a single score — explicitly designed to identify countries achieving high well-being with low environmental impact, rather than simply high material output.

★ REAL WORLD: Bhutan's Gross National Happiness
Bhutan's fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, is credited with coining "Gross National Happiness (GNH)" as early as 1972, formally rejecting GDP as the country's primary policy target and instead adopting an index built around psychological well-being, health, education, cultural resilience, environmental sustainability and good governance. Unlike most alternative measures, which exist mainly to rank and compare countries after the fact, GNH functions as a live policy-screening tool within Bhutan's own government: a "GNH Policy Screening Tool" is used to formally assess whether new government policies and projects are likely to help or harm each of GNH's nine domains before they are approved. The concept gained wider international recognition when the UN General Assembly unanimously adopted a Bhutan-sponsored resolution in 2011 inviting member states to give greater consideration to happiness and well-being in pursuing development, which in turn helped inspire the creation of the World Happiness Report the following year. Bhutan's GDP per capita remains low by global standards, but the country consistently emphasises non-material well-being indicators in its own policymaking, making it one of the most-cited real-world examples of a government deliberately prioritising an alternative measure over conventional national income accounting — though critics note that Bhutan still requires substantial GDP growth to fund the healthcare, education and infrastructure that underpin the GNH goals themselves, and that GNH's own methodology has been criticised as more difficult to measure consistently and objectively than GDP.
★ REAL WORLD: Costa Rica and the Happy Planet Index
The New Economics Foundation, a UK-based think tank, first published the Happy Planet Index in 2006, and Costa Rica has ranked at or near the top of it in multiple subsequent editions, despite a GDP per capita far below that of the United States or Western European economies that typically rank much lower on the same index. Costa Rica achieves this through a combination of high reported life satisfaction, above-average life expectancy for its income level, and a comparatively small ecological footprint — supported by an electricity grid that has run on renewable sources (predominantly hydroelectric, with growing geothermal and wind capacity) for well over 98% of the year in several recent years, and by the abolition of its standing army in 1948 under President José Figueres Ferrer, a decision the government has consistently credited with freeing up fiscal resources for health and education spending ever since. Costa Rica is a frequently cited case that a country does not need a high GDP per capita to score well on broader measures of sustainable well-being.
Exam Tip: a strong evaluation answer does not simply state that "GDP is not a perfect measure of well-being" — it should explain the specific mechanism by which GDP fails to capture a particular dimension of well-being (income distribution, non-market activity, externalities, etc.), and should show awareness of at least one named alternative measure and how it attempts to correct for that specific gap.

Exam Technique & Worked Calculations

The Golden Chain: From Raw Data to Real GDP Per Capita

Collect expenditure data (C, I, G, X, M) → sum using the expenditure formula to find nominal GDP → adjust for net income from abroad to find nominal GNI, if required → divide nominal GDP (or GNI) by the GDP deflator and multiply by 100 to find the real value → divide real GDP (or GNI) by population to find the real per-capita value → optionally adjust using a PPP conversion factor to make the figure comparable across countries with different price levels.

Key Calculation Tips (Paper 3 Focus)

1

Always write out the full expenditure formula (GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)) before substituting numbers — this reduces careless sign errors, especially with net exports, which can be negative.

2

Memorise the deflator triangle: Nominal = Real × Deflator ÷ 100. Given any two of nominal GDP, real GDP and the deflator, you can always find the third by rearranging this one relationship.

3

Show every intermediate step and label your units clearly ($bn, per person, index points) — Paper 3 mark schemes typically award marks for correct method even where a final numerical answer is wrong.

4

Don't confuse a rise in the deflator (inflation) with a rise in real output — a country can show strong nominal GDP growth driven almost entirely by rising prices, with real GDP barely moving.

5

When comparing countries, be explicit about which measure you are using and why — total GDP, GDP per capita, and GDP per capita at PPP can each produce a completely different ranking of the same set of countries, exactly as with China and Luxembourg.

6

Use real-world context to earn application marks: reference Ireland's GDP–GNI gap, the informal economy in Nigeria or India, or Bhutan's Gross National Happiness when evaluating whether a given statistic is the right one to use.
