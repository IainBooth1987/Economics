Game Theory Concepts In This Simulation

Bayesian Updating

Everyone starts at the base rate. Each speech, vote and murder carries a likelihood ratio, and Bayes' rule turns these into the probability that each player is a Traitor, given the castle's model of how each type behaves.

Cheap Talk vs Costly Signals

If Traitors can say exactly what Faithfuls say, speeches tell you nothing (pooling). Here, making wrong accusations costly makes Traitors accuse less, so the types partly separate. In theory, that only happens if the cost hits the two types differently.

Prisoner's Dilemma?

Speaking up helps the group but makes you a murder target. With the game's illustrative payoffs, keeping quiet is a dominant strategy and everyone ends up worse off. Whether a real Round Table works like this depends on how big that murder risk is.

Information Cascades

Once a few people accuse the same player, others may follow the crowd rather than their own evidence. Majorities are reliable only when judgements are independent, and cascades destroy that.

Mechanism Design

Plurality, majority and supermajority rules change who gets banished, how often nobody is, and how easily a coordinated minority can swing a split vote.

Backward Induction & Credible Threats

In a known final round, promises can't be enforced. A recruitment ultimatum ("join us or be murdered") works only if the threat is credible, and the payoffs decide whether accepting is rational.

BIZ-OMICS · Teacher guide

The Traitors: a 25-minute game theory lesson

Use the Classroom Lesson preset. Fix a game code (e.g. 4172) if you want every class to face the same castle.

Before the lesson

  • One screen, whole class: project the game; the class decides each move by show of hands and you click. Start with 👁 Numbers off so students reason before they see probabilities.
  • Students on devices: give everyone the same game code and role, then compare outcomes and Brier scores at the end.
  • Play one game yourself first (Quick Play takes about 12 minutes).

Lesson pathway

TimeWhat happensAsk the class
0–3Starter. Before opening the game."3 Traitors among 12 players. What's the chance any one person is a Traitor?" (25%: the base rate, or prior.)
3–5Choose a role, watch the Conclave."What does a Traitor know that you don't? Why does that matter for everything that follows?" (Asymmetric information.)
5–10Round 1, numbers off. Night challenge → breakfast → Round Table speech challenge → vote challenge on voting rules."Is that accusation a credible signal or cheap talk?" Before the reveal, agree a class probability that the banished player is a Traitor.
10–14Round 2: turn 👁 Numbers on. Watch the pillars after the reveal."Whose probability moved most, and why?" (Likelihood ratios; Bayes' rule.) Check the "same slates under each rule" box: mechanism design.
14–20Rounds 3–4 and the Fire."Should we vote to end the game? What would a Traitor vote?" (Weak dominance; backward induction.)
20–25Debrief. Brier score vs the model, Bayes audit, "If you played again".Students pick a change and justify it with one concept. Set a 9-mark question from the debrief as homework.

Timings assume about four minutes per Round Table with class discussion. A Classroom game averages about five Round Tables in simulation. The estimate on the setup screen updates with the settings.

Other presets

  • ⚡ Quick Play (about 12 minutes): a starter or plenary on asymmetric information and voting. No recruitment, no challenges.
  • 📐 Advanced Game Theory (about 35–40 minutes): majority voting and high signal costs. Expect the Traitors to win more often: a failed majority vote leaves them at the table, which is itself the lesson. Good for discussing when costly signals separate types, and why majorities can fail.

What the model assumes

Students should know which parts are modelling choices. Each Theory Spotlight shows "This simulation assumes" and "Game theory shows" separately.

  • Prior: every player starts at the base rate. With a hidden count, the castle believes it could be one fewer or one more Traitor than the true number, each equally likely.
  • Murders: victims are always Faithful. The castle treats the victim as a random Faithful, then applies a likelihood ratio of ×2.5 to anyone the victim had accused.
  • Recruitment: one recruitment night per game, after the first Traitor is banished. The castle assumes 3 in 4 recruits accept; evidence gathered before someone was recruited stays attached to their Faithful past.
  • Speeches: how often each type makes each speech is programmed. At zero signal cost the two types behave identically (likelihood ratio 1).
  • Votes and accusations: likelihood ratios were measured from simulated games of the AI players, then rounded towards 1.
  • Payoff matrices: the numbers are illustrative. The prisoner's dilemma holds only because the numbers make keeping quiet dominant.
  • Checked: the probability engine enumerates every possible set of Traitors. It was tested against a separate brute-force calculation over 818 belief states (murders, banishments, recruitment, hidden counts and each Faithful's own view); the largest difference was 3 × 10⁻¹⁵.
