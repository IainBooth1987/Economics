🎣 Tragedy of the Commons: A Strategic Economics Game

How to Play

You run one of six fishing firms sharing a single fishery. Each round you choose a target catch. You play for up to 10 rounds, trying to earn as much profit as you can — without the fishery collapsing under you.

How the fishery works

Regrowth: the sea can hold at most 1,000 fish. Regrowth is fastest at a stock of 500, when the fishery can supply its Maximum Sustainable Yield of 150 fish per round — about 25 per firm. A full sea hardly grows (no room), and a nearly empty one hardly grows (too few breeding fish).

Costs: you pay £25 for every fish you target (fuel, crew, ice), whether or not you land it.

Landings: when the stock falls below 500, boats find fewer fish. At a stock of 250 you only land half your target — so each landed fish costs twice as much.

Price: set by the total landings of all six firms. The more fish hit the market, the lower the price. A good reputation earns an eco-label premium of up to +20% on your own sales.

Collapse: if the stock falls below the collapse threshold (100 fish on Medium), the fishery closes and the game ends.

Your rivals

Each rival firm follows a hidden strategy. Some copy you, some bear grudges, some never hold back. Watch what they do and try to work out who is who. Your teacher can reveal the strategies with the 🕵️ button.

Each round

1. Drag the slider to set your target catch. The preview estimates your profit, assuming rivals repeat last round.

2. Confirm. All six firms fish, the market sets a price, and the stock regrows.

3. Read the round report. Answer the "Tell me why" questions before revealing the answers.

🎯 Tragedy of the Commons

The idea goes back to William Forster Lloyd (1833), who described villagers overgrazing shared pasture. Garrett Hardin popularised it in Science in 1968. When a resource is shared and access is open, each user gains the full benefit of taking a bit more but bears only a fraction of the cost. So individually rational choices add up to collective ruin.

🏛️ Common Pool Resources

Fish stocks are rivalrous: a fish you catch cannot be caught by anyone else. They are also non-excludable: it is hard to stop boats fishing without regulation. That combination separates them from pure public goods (non-rival and non-excludable). It also makes them vulnerable to overuse.

📈 Logistic Growth and Maximum Sustainable Yield

Fish populations regrow fastest at around half their carrying capacity. In this game that is a stock of 500, where regrowth equals the MSY. Fishing a full stock down towards 500 actually raises the sustainable catch. Fishing below 500 shrinks it, and the further you go the faster it shrinks.

🔄 Negative Externalities and Market Failure

Stock externality: every fish you take leaves a smaller stock for everyone. That lowers rivals' catch rates, raises their costs and slows regrowth. You don't pay for any of it. So the marginal social cost of fishing is higher than the marginal private cost, and the free market overfishes.

H. Scott Gordon (1954) showed that under open access, firms keep fishing until the resource earns no profit at all. In an open-access fishery the value of the stock is wasted.

🎲 Game Theory: the Prisoner's Dilemma

In one round: catching more is tempting whatever rivals do. If they hold back, you gain; if they don't, holding back just leaves fish for them. Everyone overfishing is the Nash equilibrium.

Over many rounds: cooperation becomes possible because rivals can punish defection. Robert Axelrod's tournaments (1984) found that tit-for-tat (start nice, then copy your opponent) did remarkably well. A grim trigger strategy (cooperate until betrayed, then never again) makes defection very costly.

⚠️ Government Failure

Intervention can fail too. Regulators may lack the information to set the right quota or tax. They may be lobbied by the industry they regulate (regulatory capture). Or they may face political pressure to set catches above the scientific advice.

🏢 Free Market (open access)

No rules. This is the baseline Hardin warned about.

🤝 Voluntary Agreement

Firms promise to stay at or below their MSY share. Nobody can fine a firm that breaks the deal; the only punishment is lost reputation and rivals retaliating.

⚖️ Quota Regulation (command and control)

A legal quota per firm. Inspectors catch 75% of breaches, and the fine is £100 per fish over quota. Regulation only works if the expected fine (chance of detection × fine) exceeds the profit from cheating.

🧾 Pigouvian Tax

A tax of £20 on every fish landed, after A.C. Pigou (1920). It puts a price on the external cost so that each firm decides for itself how much to catch. It raises revenue, but setting the right rate requires knowing the size of the external cost.

📜 Tradable Quotas (ITQs)

The total catch is capped at the MSY, and each firm owns permits for its share. Firms can buy or sell permits, so the fish go to whoever values them most. New Zealand introduced a system like this in 1986. It creates property rights: a healthier stock makes your permits worth more.

👥 Community Management (Ostrom)

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize 2009) showed that communities often manage commons well without the state or privatisation. Her design principles include clear boundaries, monitoring by the users themselves, and graduated sanctions. In this scenario, a first breach earns a warning, a second a fine, and a third exclusion from the fishery.

🚨 Ecosystem Crisis

Free-market rules, but disease has already cut the stock to 400. Scarcity pushes prices up, which makes grabbing the last fish even more tempting.

Real-World Fishery Collapses

🇨🇦 Grand Banks Northern Cod (1992 – today)

Location: off Newfoundland and Labrador, Canada.

History: for nearly 500 years the Grand Banks were among the richest fishing grounds on Earth. By the late 1980s, factory trawlers with sonar were operating under total allowable catches of around 250,000 tonnes.

The collapse: on 2 July 1992, Fisheries Minister John Crosbie announced a moratorium after the stock crashed to around 1% of its historical level. Roughly 30,000–40,000 people lost their livelihoods — Canada's largest single mass lay-off.

Cause: better technology, over-optimistic stock assessments and intense competition for the catch. Each fleet had an incentive to take fish before others did.

What happened next: a small inshore "stewardship" fishery was allowed from 2006. In June 2024 Ottawa formally ended the moratorium, setting a catch limit of 18,000 tonnes — a fraction of the 120,000 tonnes allowed in early 1992. The limit rose to 38,000 tonnes in 2025 and 59,000 tonnes in 2026.

Government failure? A leaked briefing note showed that government scientists had recommended keeping the catch at 13,000 tonnes. Political advisers argued that reopening would be "politically a victory". Some scientists warn the stock has not grown since 2016 — a live example of political pressure overriding scientific advice.

🇵🇪 Peruvian Anchoveta (1972)

History: by the late 1960s Peru ran the world's largest fishery, landing around 12 million tonnes of anchoveta a year for fishmeal.

The collapse: in 1972, heavy fishing combined with an El Niño warming event, and catches fell by around three-quarters.

Cause: open access led to far more boats and processing plants than the stock could support. When El Niño hit, the weakened stock could not bounce back.

Recovery: Peru later tightened catch limits and in 2009 introduced Individual Vessel Quotas, ending the "race to fish".

🌍 Lake Victoria Nile Perch (1990s–2000s)

History: Nile perch, introduced in the 1950s, became a major export shared by Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania.

The crisis: intensive fishing for export markets drove down perch numbers and sizes. Many native species had already been wiped out by the perch itself.

Cause: three countries sharing one lake with weak coordination, plus strong international demand. As large fish disappeared, small-scale fishers turned to illegal undersized nets.

✅ Maine Lobster (USA) — Community Management

Maine's lobstermen informally divide the coast into territories, watch each other and punish trespassers. Anthropologist James Acheson documented these "lobster gangs". Combined with rules such as returning egg-bearing females, the fishery has been sustained for over a century — the kind of self-governance Ostrom described.

📊 Common Patterns

• Open access: no effective property rights or access controls.

• Technology: catch capacity grew faster than the stock could regenerate.

• Short-termism: pressure to maximise this year's catch.

• Delayed recognition: by the time collapse was obvious, it was too late.

• Slow recovery: depleted stocks regrow slowly — exactly what the logistic model predicts.
