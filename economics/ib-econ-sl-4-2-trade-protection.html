Tariffs

Tariff
A tax imposed on imported goods, raising their price in the domestic market above the world price.
A tariff raises the domestic price from the world price (WP) to WP+T. Domestic production rises (Q1→Q2), consumption falls (Q4→Q3), imports shrink from Q4−Q1 to Q3−Q2. Government collects tariff revenue; two deadweight loss triangles represent production and consumption inefficiency.
StakeholderEffect of a Tariff
Domestic consumersPay a higher price and consume less — worse off.
Domestic producersReceive a higher price and produce more — better off (producer surplus gain).
GovernmentCollects tariff revenue on the remaining imports.
Foreign exportersSell fewer units into the protected market — worse off.
Society overallExperiences a net welfare loss (deadweight loss) from production and consumption inefficiency.
Country/BlocApprox. Average Applied TariffContext
Singapore~0%One of the world's most open trade regimes — virtually free trade on almost all imports.
United States~3%Low average, but with sharp spikes on specific goods — since 2018, a wide range of Chinese imports under Section 301 (see below).
European Union~5%A common external tariff applied uniformly by all EU member states on non-EU imports (see 4.4 on the EU customs union).
India~15–18%Among the higher averages of major economies, reflecting a long-standing policy emphasis on protecting domestic industry and agriculture.
Reading this table: a low national average can still hide very high tariffs on specific "sensitive" products (agriculture, textiles, autos). Always check whether an exam data set refers to an average tariff or a specific product tariff before drawing conclusions.
1930: Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act (US)→ US average tariff rises to ~59% on covered imports→ Trading partners (Canada, France, others) retaliate→ World trade volumes fall by roughly two-thirds, 1929–1934→ Widely blamed for deepening the Great Depression

Signed into law by US President Herbert Hoover in June 1930 — against the advice of over 1,000 economists who petitioned him not to sign it — the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act remains the textbook cautionary tale of protectionism's second-round effects: even if a tariff helps some domestic producers in isolation, the retaliation and collapse in global trade volumes that followed made the Great Depression measurably worse for almost everyone, including the US industries the Act was meant to protect. Economists cite it today as one of the strongest historical arguments for the rules-based, retaliation-limiting trading system now overseen by the WTO (see 4.4).

Real-world: Nigeria has taxed imported rice at rates of around 70% for years, and in August 2019 President Muhammadu Buhari's government went further, closing land borders with Benin and other neighbours specifically to stop rice smuggling that was undermining the tariff. The closure raised domestic rice prices sharply and was defended by the government as boosting local paddy production, but was also criticised by economists and by neighbouring ECOWAS governments (see 4.4) for disrupting legitimate regional trade far beyond rice alone — a textbook illustration of the producer-gain, consumer-loss trade-off tariffs create, pushed to an extreme where the "tariff" effectively became a near-total border closure.
Real-world — The US–China Trade War (2018–2020): Starting in March 2018, US President Donald Trump used Section 301 of the US Trade Act to impose escalating tariffs on Chinese imports, reaching 25% on some $250bn of goods by 2019, citing intellectual property concerns and the US trade deficit with China. China's government retaliated with its own tariffs, deliberately targeting US exports with strong political symbolism, including soybeans and pork from politically important farm states. After nearly two years of escalation, US Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer and Chinese Vice-Premier Liu He signed a "Phase One" deal in Washington on 15 January 2020, under which China committed to increased purchases of US goods in exchange for a pause in further tariff increases — though most tariffs already imposed remained in place. Economists Mary Amiti, Stephen Redding and David Weinstein later found the tariffs had been passed through almost entirely to US importers and consumers rather than absorbed by Chinese exporters — real-world evidence for the consumer surplus loss quantified in the HL extension file.

Quotas

Quota
A physical limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported over a given period, restricting supply directly rather than through a tax.
A quota restricting imports to Q3−Q2 produces the identical price rise, production increase, and consumption fall as an equivalent tariff — but the middle rectangle becomes "quota rent" (captured by whoever holds the import licences), not government revenue.
TariffQuota
A tax — price rises as a direct result of the taxA physical quantity restriction — price rises indirectly as supply is limited
Government collects revenue"Quota rent" is captured by licence holders (could be domestic government if it auctions licences, or foreign exporters if they hold the licences)
Import quantity can still respond somewhat to price changesImport quantity is fixed regardless of further price changes, making quotas a more rigid restriction
Real-world: Indonesia's Ministry of Trade has periodically restricted imports of staple foods through licensing-based quotas — for example tightening garlic import licences sharply in early 2017, which briefly pushed domestic garlic prices to several times the world price before the restriction was eased later that year — directly capping the quantity that can enter the country regardless of how domestic prices move, a stricter form of protection than a tariff, which merely raises the price without capping quantity outright.
Real-world — The Multi-Fibre Arrangement (1974–2005): From 1974, a web of quotas known as the Multi-Fibre Arrangement (MFA) let developed countries — chiefly the United States and the European Community — restrict textile and clothing imports from developing countries such as India, Bangladesh and Hong Kong to a fixed quantity for each exporting country and product category. Because each quota's import licence was valuable, a lucrative secondary market in licence-trading emerged in exporting countries, with the "quota rent" typically captured by whichever local firm secured the licence rather than by the importing-country government. Developing countries argued for decades that the MFA was a discriminatory exception to normal GATT/WTO trade rules. Following the 1994 Uruguay Round Agreement on Textiles and Clothing, the quota system was phased out in stages and finally abolished entirely on 1 January 2005 — one of the most significant dismantlings of a global quota regime in trade history, and a useful example for 4.4 of how multilateral negotiation can gradually erode long-standing protectionist barriers.

Subsidies

Subsidy
A payment from government to domestic producers, lowering their costs and allowing them to supply more at any given price — reducing reliance on imports without directly taxing consumers.
Export subsidy
A subsidy paid specifically on units sold abroad, intended to make domestic exports more price-competitive in foreign markets.
A production subsidy shifts domestic supply from S to S+subsidy: at the unchanged world price, domestic producers now supply more (Qold→Qnew), reducing imports. The subsidy costs the government money and creates a deadweight loss, since the extra domestic output (Qold to Qnew) costs more in resources than importing at the world price.
StakeholderEffect of a Production Subsidy
Domestic consumersStill pay the (unchanged) world price — largely unaffected directly.
Domestic producersEffectively receive world price + subsidy per unit, so produce and sell more — better off.
GovernmentBears the cost of the subsidy — an opportunity cost in terms of other spending forgone.
Foreign exportersSell fewer units as imports fall — worse off.
Society overallExperiences a deadweight loss, since resources are used to produce domestically what could be imported more cheaply.
Real-world: Egypt has for decades subsidised bread (via subsidised wheat) and fuel. By the mid-2010s these subsidies were consuming a very large share of total government spending, contributing to a budget crisis that led Egypt to agree a $12 billion loan programme with the IMF in November 2016, conditioned partly on gradually reducing fuel subsidies — a politically sensitive process given subsidised "baladi" bread's status as a basic staple sold for a fraction of a US cent per loaf for years, and given memories of the January 1977 "bread riots" under President Anwar Sadat, triggered when an earlier attempt to cut food subsidies was announced.
Real-world — The EU Common Agricultural Policy (CAP): Since its founding in 1962, the EU's Common Agricultural Policy has subsidised European farmers through direct income payments and price supports, and for decades consumed close to 40% of the entire EU budget — still around a third today, even after reform. France has historically been the largest single recipient of CAP payments, reflecting its large agricultural sector. Critics — including many developing-country governments and the WTO — have long argued that CAP subsidies let EU farmers sell surplus produce on world markets below the true cost of production, depressing world prices for competing farmers in developing countries, in much the same way as the India sugar export subsidy dispute below; pressure from the WTO's Doha Round (launched 2001, largely stalled since the 2010s — see 4.4) has gradually reduced the most trade-distorting elements without eliminating the policy.

Export Subsidy

An export subsidy works differently from the production subsidy above. Because producers can always earn the subsidised price by selling abroad, they will not accept less than that price domestically either — so the price paid by domestic consumers rises from the world price (WP) to WP+subsidy too. This simultaneously raises domestic production (Qs₁→Qs₂) and reduces domestic consumption (Qd₁→Qd₂), so the exported quantity grows on both sides at once, unlike a production subsidy aimed only at import replacement.

An export subsidy raises the price domestic producers effectively receive from WP to WP+subsidy. Since they can always earn this by exporting, the domestic price consumers pay rises too — production expands (Qs₁→Qs₂) and consumption contracts (Qd₁→Qd₂), so the quantity exported (Qs₂−Qd₂) grows from both directions at once. The shaded rectangle is the total cost to government: the subsidy per unit multiplied by the quantity exported.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Cost of an Export Subsidy
A government pays a $3 per-unit export subsidy. At the new, higher price this creates, domestic production is 500 units and domestic consumption is 200 units, so 300 units are exported.
Government cost = Subsidy per unit × Quantity exported = 3 × 300 = $900.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the Subsidy Rate
A government's total export subsidy bill is reported as $1,200. Data shows domestic production of 620 units and domestic consumption of 220 units following the subsidy.
Step 1 — quantity exported = 620 − 220 = 400 units.
Step 2 — Subsidy per unit = Government cost ÷ Quantity exported = 1,200 ÷ 400 = $3.
Real-world: India's sugar export subsidies were formally challenged at the WTO by Brazil, Australia and Guatemala, who argued the subsidies let Indian sugar be sold abroad below its cost of production, depressing the world price faced by competing exporters — illustrating how one country's export subsidy can become another country's trade dispute (see 4.3 for anti-dumping arguments) and, ultimately, a WTO ruling (see 4.4) against the exporting country.

Administrative Barriers

Administrative barriers
Non-tariff regulations, standards, and bureaucratic procedures that make it harder or more costly for foreign firms to sell into a domestic market — without necessarily using a tax or quantity limit.
TypeExample
Product standardsHealth, safety, or technical specifications that imported goods must meet — sometimes set deliberately stricter than necessary to disadvantage foreign producers.
Licensing and paperwork requirementsComplex customs procedures, certification requirements, or import licensing that raise the cost and time needed to import.
Labelling and packaging rulesRequirements specific to the domestic market that force foreign producers to adapt products at extra cost.
Exam tip: administrative barriers are harder to quantify than tariffs, quotas or subsidies, but examiners still expect you to recognise them as a form of protection — they raise the effective cost of importing without necessarily appearing as a visible price change.
Real-world: Kenya's Bureau of Standards has at times required additional certification and testing for imported sugar and dairy products, citing food safety standards — timing that critics note has sometimes coincided with periods of oversupply from domestic sugar millers in western Kenya, a policy that also has the effect of protecting domestic sugar and dairy producers from cheaper imports.
Real-world — The EU–US "Beef Hormone" Dispute: In 1989, the European Community banned imports of beef treated with growth hormones, citing consumer health concerns — a ban that also happened to exclude a large share of US beef exports, which routinely used such hormones. The United States challenged the ban at the WTO, which ruled against the EU in 1997–98 for lacking sufficient scientific risk-assessment justification; when the EU nonetheless kept the ban, the WTO authorised the US (joined later by Canada) to impose retaliatory tariffs worth over $100 million a year on selected EU goods, including Roquefort cheese and Dijon mustard. The dispute dragged on for two decades before a negotiated compromise in 2019 gave US hormone-free beef a larger duty-free quota into the EU market. It remains one of the WTO's clearest illustrations of how a genuinely-intended health/safety standard (see 4.3's "health and safety" argument for protection) can also function as an administrative trade barrier, whatever its original motivation.

Where this shows up on the papers: in a Paper 1 essay, part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain the effects of a named form of protection using a correctly drawn and labelled diagram; part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) then asks you to evaluate or discuss — almost always weighing the protection measure against the case for free trade (4.1) and the arguments for/against protection (4.3), using named real-world examples such as those in this chapter. Paper 2 (40 marks; HL and SL sit the same paper and answer ONE of two questions) builds each question from two linked "Text" extracts with numbered paragraphs plus several data tables, and every sub-question cites an exact reference such as "(Text A, paragraph 3)" or "(Table 4)". The mark ladder typically runs: (a)(i)–(ii), two 2-mark "Define the term..." parts on bolded terms taken straight from the text; (b), a 2–3 mark calculation, sometimes paired with a 2-mark "Sketch a [named] diagram..." ("sketch" means a smaller in-text diagram — a larger standalone one is asked for with "draw"); (c)–(f), a run of 4-mark "Using a [named] diagram/information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." questions each anchored to a specific paragraph or table; and (g), a single 15-mark "discuss"/"evaluate" question weighing something specific — often two named things against each other, such as tariffs vs quotas here. HL students go on to meet the full quantitative welfare-effect calculations (consumer surplus, producer surplus, government revenue/cost, deadweight loss) only on Paper 3 — covered in the HL extension file.
Identify what's given→ Choose the correct formula→ Substitute carefully→ State the units (% or $)→ Interpret the answer in context
Tariff-Induced Price Rise (%) = ((Price with Tariff − World Price) ÷ World Price) × 100
Forward — Price Rise from a Tariff
World price = $20; price after a tariff is imposed = $25.
Price rise = ((25 − 20) ÷ 20) × 100 = 25%
Reverse-Direction: Finding the World Price
A tariff raises the domestic price to $36, a rise of 20% above the world price. Find the world price.
World price = 36 ÷ 1.20 = $30
Given: post-tariff price + % rise→ World price = post-tariff price ÷ (1 + % rise)→ check units are $
Exam tips:
  1. Note that calculating the exact welfare effects (consumer surplus loss, producer surplus gain, government revenue, deadweight loss) from a tariff, quota or subsidy diagram is an HL-only skill — covered in the HL extension file.
  2. When comparing a tariff and a quota with identical price/quantity effects, always state explicitly who captures the "middle rectangle" in each case — this is usually the key distinguishing point examiners look for.
  3. For subsidies, remember the price consumers pay does not change — only the price producers effectively receive changes, via the government top-up.
  4. Use specific real-world examples (Nigeria and Indonesia for tariffs/quotas on staple foods; Egypt for subsidy fiscal cost; Kenya for administrative barriers) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays on trade protection.
