The Current Account and the Exchange Rate HL ONLY

Under a floating exchange rate system, the current account is one of the main drivers of currency demand and supply. Exports are current account credits — a foreign buyer must acquire the domestic currency to pay for them, creating demand for the currency. Imports are current account debits — domestic buyers must sell the domestic currency to acquire foreign currency, creating supply of the domestic currency (equivalently, demand for the foreign currency).

Key mechanism: a current account surplus (credits > debits) means net foreign demand for the currency is rising, which — other things equal — causes the currency to appreciate. A current account deficit (debits > credits) means net supply of the currency onto the foreign exchange market is rising, which — other things equal — causes the currency to depreciate.

A current account improvement (e.g. stronger export demand) raises foreign demand for the currency from D₁ to D₂. The equilibrium exchange rate rises from ER₁ to ER₂ — the currency appreciates — while the quantity of currency traded rises from Q₁ to Q₂. A deteriorating current account works in reverse, shifting D left and depreciating the currency.

Current Account MovementEffect on Demand/Supply of CurrencyEffect on Exchange Rate
Current account surplus widens (exports rising faster than imports)Demand for currency rises (D shifts right)Appreciation
Current account deficit widens (imports rising faster than exports)Supply of currency rises (S shifts right)Depreciation
Real-world — the Australian dollar as a "commodity currency": the Australian dollar (AUD) has historically moved closely with Australia's terms of trade and current account position, because iron ore, coal, and natural gas dominate its export mix. When global commodity prices surged in 2021–2022, Australia's current account swung to a record surplus of around 3% of GDP in 2022 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, ABS) — and the AUD appreciated against the US dollar over the same period, consistent with the mechanism above: stronger export earnings raised foreign demand for AUD to pay for Australian commodity exports.
Careful — this assumes a floating exchange rate: this direct link between the current account and the exchange rate weakens considerably under a fixed or heavily managed exchange rate regime, where the central bank intervenes (buying/selling reserve assets — see the SL file, section 2) to hold the rate at a target level regardless of the underlying current account position. China's exchange rate management (SL file, section 2) is the classic illustration.

The Financial Account and the Exchange Rate HL ONLY

The financial account can move the exchange rate independently of — and sometimes in the opposite direction to — the current account. Inflows on the financial account (foreigners buying domestic bonds, shares, or businesses) require foreigners to acquire the domestic currency first, raising demand for it and putting upward pressure on its value, exactly like a current account credit. Outflows (domestic residents buying foreign assets) work in reverse.

Key point: a country can run a persistent current account deficit without its currency depreciating, provided the deficit is fully financed by an equally persistent financial account surplus (net capital inflows) — this is exactly what the "zero balance" identity from the SL file (section 3) requires, and it is central to understanding why some deficit countries see little currency pressure while others face a crisis.
Real-world — the US dollar and "exorbitant privilege": the United States has run a current account deficit for most of the last five decades (around 3% of GDP in 2023, per the US Bureau of Economic Analysis), yet the US dollar has remained strong for long periods. A major reason is financial account inflows: foreign governments and investors — notably the Bank of Japan and China's State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE), among the largest official holders of US Treasury securities — have long purchased US government debt, partly to manage their own exchange rates and partly because Treasuries are seen as the world's safest, most liquid asset. These financial account inflows finance the US current account deficit and support dollar demand, a pattern the French economist Valéry Giscard d'Estaing famously called America's "exorbitant privilege" as far back as the 1960s.
Link to 3.5 (monetary policy): interest rate differentials are a key driver of financial account flows. When a central bank — e.g. the US Federal Reserve — raises interest rates relative to other countries, it attracts short-term portfolio investment ("hot money") seeking higher returns, appreciating the currency and helping to finance a current account deficit. This is one reason interest rate decisions are watched so closely for their exchange rate effects, not just their effect on domestic aggregate demand.

Implications of a Persistent Current Account Deficit HL ONLY

ImplicationExplanation
Exchange ratesSustained downward pressure on the currency if the deficit is not fully financed by financial account inflows — see sections 1–2.
Interest ratesThe central bank may need to keep interest rates relatively high to attract the capital inflows needed to finance the deficit, even if domestic conditions (e.g. weak growth) would otherwise call for lower rates — a policy trade-off (link 3.5).
Foreign ownership of domestic assetsFinancing a deficit through FDI and portfolio inflows means foreign investors accumulate a growing share of domestic businesses, property, bonds, and equities over time.
DebtIf financed through borrowing (official or private) rather than equity-type investment, a persistent deficit raises external debt, which must eventually be serviced (interest paid) and repaid.
Credit ratingsRating agencies (e.g. S&P Global, Moody's, Fitch) may downgrade a country's sovereign credit rating if its external position is judged unsustainable, raising the government's future borrowing costs.
Demand managementGovernments may feel pressure to use expenditure-reducing (contractionary) fiscal or monetary policy to curb import demand — see section 4 — even where this conflicts with domestic growth or employment objectives.
Economic growthImport-led growth can boost living standards in the short run, but correcting a deficit via reduced domestic demand risks slowing growth — a persistent deficit forces a trade-off between short-run consumption and longer-run external sustainability.
Why this matters for evaluation: not every current account deficit is equally risky. A deficit driven by borrowing to fund unproductive consumption (e.g. imported consumer goods) is generally seen as more concerning than one driven by importing capital goods and machinery that raise future productive capacity — a distinction worth making explicit in a 15-mark evaluation.

Crisis Narrative: Turkey's Current Account Deficit and the 2018 Lira Crisis

Years of persistent current account deficits (import-led credit boom)→ Heavy reliance on short-term foreign-currency borrowing→ Aug 2018 — investor confidence collapses, lira falls sharply→ Credit rating downgrades (S&P, Moody's, Fitch)→ Central Bank of the Republic of Turkey (CBRT) raises interest rates sharply to defend the currency
Real-world: Turkey ran persistent, large current account deficits through the 2010s, financed heavily by short-term foreign-currency borrowing by banks and firms rather than stable long-term FDI. In August 2018, a combination of a diplomatic dispute with the United States, worries about central bank independence, and the size of Turkey's external debt triggered a collapse in investor confidence — the Turkish lira lost around 40% of its value against the US dollar over the year. S&P Global, Moody's, and Fitch all downgraded Turkey's sovereign credit rating during the episode, raising future borrowing costs, and the Central Bank of the Republic of Turkey (CBRT) eventually raised its main policy rate to 24% in September 2018 in an attempt to stem capital flight and stabilise the currency — a textbook illustration of several implications in the table above occurring together.

Methods to Correct a Persistent Current Account Deficit HL ONLY

MethodHow It WorksExample Tools
Expenditure switchingShifts spending from imports toward domestically produced goods and services, without necessarily reducing total spending.Currency depreciation/devaluation; tariffs; import quotas (see 4.2/4.3 on trade protection)
Expenditure reducingReduces total (domestic) spending, which lowers demand for imports as part of overall demand.Contractionary fiscal policy — higher taxes/lower government spending (see 3.6); contractionary monetary policy — higher interest rates (see 3.5)
Supply-side policiesImprove the long-run competitiveness of domestic industry so exports grow and imports are substituted domestically, without needing to suppress demand.Investment in education/training, R&D incentives, infrastructure spending, deregulation to raise productivity (see 3.7)
Link to 4.2/4.3, 3.5, 3.6 and 3.7: expenditure-switching tariffs and quotas draw directly on the trade protection tools covered in 4.2/4.3; expenditure-reducing policy draws on the fiscal and monetary policy tools from 3.5/3.6; supply-side correction draws on the policies from 3.7. A strong Paper 1 answer explicitly names the specific tool (e.g. "raising the central bank's policy rate" rather than just "monetary policy") and links it back to these topics.
Real-world — the UK's 1967 devaluation as expenditure switching: facing a persistent current account deficit, Prime Minister Harold Wilson's government devalued the pound sterling by 14.3% (from $2.80 to $2.40) in November 1967 — a classic expenditure-switching policy intended to make UK exports cheaper and imports more expensive. Wilson's now-famous broadcast reassured the public that "the pound in your pocket" had not been devalued in domestic purchasing power terms, even though its international value had fallen sharply.

Effectiveness of Correction Measures HL ONLY

Factors supporting effectiveness

  • High combined price elasticities of demand for exports and imports (see the Marshall-Lerner condition, section 6) mean expenditure-switching policies work quickly and reliably.
  • Credible, well-sequenced supply-side reform can raise competitiveness permanently, without the demand-side pain of expenditure-reducing policy.
  • A government with strong fiscal/monetary credibility can use expenditure-reducing policy without triggering a loss of investor confidence.

Factors limiting effectiveness

  • Low combined elasticities mean depreciation initially worsens the trade balance before it improves — the J-curve effect (section 6) — testing political patience.
  • Expenditure-reducing policy causes unemployment and slower growth in the short run — a politically costly trade-off (link 3.3 macroeconomic objectives).
  • Tariffs/quotas risk retaliation from trading partners, and violate WTO commitments where applicable (see the SL 4.4 file, section 5).
  • Supply-side policies operate with long time lags — years, not months — so they cannot address an urgent, short-term crisis (e.g. a currency crisis like Turkey's, section 3).
Why this matters for evaluation: the "best" correction method depends on the cause and urgency of the deficit. A slow-moving structural deficit (e.g. weak long-run competitiveness) is better addressed with supply-side policy; an acute currency crisis (e.g. Turkey, 2018) typically forces short-run expenditure-reducing measures (sharp interest rate rises) regardless of their growth cost, simply to restore investor confidence.

The Marshall-Lerner Condition and the J-Curve Effect HL ONLY

Marshall-Lerner condition: a depreciation (or devaluation) of the currency will improve the current account balance only if the sum of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is greater than 1: PEDx + PEDm > 1.

If demand for exports and imports is relatively price inelastic (PEDx + PEDm < 1), a depreciation can actually worsen the trade balance in the short run: export and import volumes barely change at first, so the higher price paid for (now more expensive) imports outweighs any small volume gains — the value of the import bill can rise even as export earnings barely move. Only once buyers and sellers have time to adjust — switching suppliers, renegotiating contracts, shifting consumption patterns — do volumes respond enough for the trade balance to improve. This lag produces the J-curve effect.

The J-curve: immediately after a depreciation, the trade balance worsens (the initial dip) because import/export volumes have not yet adjusted, even though the exchange rate has already changed. As time passes and the Marshall-Lerner condition is satisfied, export and import volumes respond and the trade balance improves, eventually moving above its pre-depreciation level — tracing a shape resembling the letter "J".

Marshall-Lerner condition: PEDx + PEDm > 1 for a depreciation to improve the trade balance
Worked example — testing the Marshall-Lerner condition (forward):
A country's price elasticity of demand for exports is 0.6; its price elasticity of demand for imports is 0.3.

PEDx + PEDm = 0.6 + 0.3 = 0.9 (less than 1) → the Marshall-Lerner condition is not satisfied, so a depreciation is likely to worsen the trade balance, at least in the short run (the J-curve dip).
Worked example — reverse-direction:
A country knows its PED for imports is 0.5, and economists estimate the combined elasticities are exactly at the Marshall-Lerner threshold (sum = 1). Find the minimum PED for exports needed for a depreciation to improve the trade balance.

PEDx + 0.5 > 1 → PEDx > 0.5 (export demand must be more than unit elastic relative to this import elasticity for the condition to hold).
Real-world — the UK's 1967 devaluation and the J-curve: after the November 1967 devaluation (section 4), the UK's trade balance did not improve immediately — it took time for export and import volumes to respond to the new, lower value of sterling, an early real-world illustration of the J-curve lag that later became a standard part of the Marshall-Lerner literature.
Exam note: a common error is treating the J-curve as evidence that depreciation "does not work." The J-curve describes a delay, not a failure — provided the Marshall-Lerner condition holds in the medium-to-long run, the trade balance is expected to improve past its original level, exactly as shown in the diagram above.

Implications of a Persistent Current Account Surplus HL ONLY

ImplicationExplanation
Domestic consumption/investmentA persistent surplus can signal that a country is under-consuming and under-investing relative to its output — resources are effectively being lent to (or spent on goods for) the rest of the world rather than raising domestic living standards or domestic capital stock.
Exchange ratesSustained upward pressure on the currency (section 1), which can erode the very export competitiveness that created the surplus in the first place — a self-correcting mechanism, at least under a floating exchange rate.
InflationPersistent net inflows (financial account surpluses that typically accompany the current account surplus) can fuel domestic credit growth and asset-price inflation if not sterilised by the central bank.
EmploymentExport-oriented sectors see strong employment, but appreciation pressure (if allowed) can squeeze employment in other trade-exposed sectors, creating structural imbalance within the economy.
Export competitivenessIf the exchange rate is prevented from appreciating (e.g. through reserve accumulation, as in the SL file's China case study), competitiveness is preserved in the short run, but at the cost of the reserve build-up and international criticism of "currency manipulation."
Why this matters for evaluation: a persistent surplus is sometimes framed as unambiguously "good," but many economists and institutions argue it represents an imbalance just as much as a deficit does — simply the mirror image. A strong evaluative answer treats a surplus symmetrically with a deficit rather than assuming one is a problem and the other is not.
Real-world — international criticism of Germany's surplus: Germany's current account surplus (around 6–8% of GDP through much of the 2010s, per the Deutsche Bundesbank) has repeatedly drawn criticism from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the European Commission, and the US Treasury Department, whose periodic "Report to Congress on... Currency Practices" has named Germany multiple times for running what it considers an excessive, persistent surplus. Critics argue the surplus reflects chronic domestic under-investment (in infrastructure and the public sector) and wage restraint suppressing domestic consumption — both keeping demand for imports low relative to Germany's strong export performance.
Real-world — China's surplus and appreciation pressure: as introduced in the SL file (section 2), China's large 2000s current account surpluses generated sustained appreciation pressure on the renminbi, which the People's Bank of China resisted through large-scale reserve accumulation rather than allowing the currency to float upward — illustrating how a government can suppress the self-correcting exchange rate mechanism described above, at the cost of holding a very large stock of foreign reserves.

Where this shows up on the papers: Paper 1 commonly sets a part (a) ≈10-mark question (e.g. "explain how a persistent current account deficit might affect a country's exchange rate") alongside a part (b) ≈15-mark evaluative essay — "evaluate the methods available to a government to correct a persistent current account deficit" is a natural, frequently examined HL Paper 1 topic, and should weigh expenditure switching, expenditure reducing, and supply-side policy against each other using named country evidence. Paper 2 (40 marks, answer ONE of two questions, built from two linked Text extracts plus tables with exact paragraph/table citations) typically runs: (a)(i)–(ii) two 2-mark "define the term..." sub-parts → (b) a 2–3 mark calculation (e.g. "calculate the current account balance from Table 1"), sometimes paired with a 2-mark "sketch" of a simple diagram → (c)–(f) a run of 4-mark "using [diagram]/[Text/Table], explain..." sub-parts anchored to named paragraphs or tables → (g) a single 15-mark discuss/evaluate question. Paper 3 (HL only, 60 marks, answer ALL, two ~30-mark questions) is where this topic is examined most heavily: one extended data set carries 7–8 climbing 2–4 mark calculation sub-parts, almost always including balance of payments component calculations — the real November 2024 Paper 3 used exactly this format, with its Question 1 built around Chile's balance of payments table, asking candidates to calculate the current account balance and the change in reserve assets from the data provided. Expect one 4-mark diagram-explain sub-part (e.g. "using an exchange rate diagram, explain...") and a final 10-mark question of the precise form "using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [named country] to address [a specific problem]" — note this asks you to recommend, not evaluate or discuss.

Paper 3 regularly asks you to calculate a missing balance of payments figure, or to test whether the Marshall-Lerner condition holds from given elasticity data.

Worked example — Calculating the change in reserve assets (Paper 3 style)
A country's current account balance = –$25bn; capital account balance = +$1bn; net financial account transactions excluding reserve assets = +$30bn. By the zero-balance identity, find the change in reserve assets.
Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account = 0
(–25) + 1 + (30 + Δ Reserve Assets) = 0
6 + Δ Reserve Assets = 0
Δ Reserve Assets = –$6 billion (reserves fall by $6bn — the central bank draws down reserves to help balance the accounts).
Worked example — Reverse-direction: finding the required financial account inflow
A country's current account deficit is $40bn and its capital account balance is $0. Reserve assets are held constant (Δ = 0). What net financial account inflow (excluding reserves) is needed to balance the accounts?
(–40) + 0 + (Net Financial Inflow + 0) = 0 → Net Financial Inflow = +$40 billion — the country needs $40bn of net financial account inflows (FDI + portfolio investment + official borrowing) to finance the deficit without running down reserves.

Exam tips:
  • On Paper 3, always set out the zero-balance identity explicitly before substituting numbers — most marks are for correct method, not just the final figure.
  • When asked to "recommend" a policy (the typical final Paper 3 sub-part), give one clearly named policy and briefly justify it using the data provided — do not hedge with "it depends," and do not write a full evaluation essay where only a recommendation is asked for.
  • In a 15-mark evaluation of correction methods, always reference the Marshall-Lerner condition when discussing expenditure-switching policy — effectiveness depends directly on it.
  • Use specific real-world examples (Turkey 2018 for deficit crisis; the UK 1967 devaluation for expenditure switching and the J-curve; Germany for surplus criticism) to anchor Paper 1 essays and Paper 2's 15-mark question.
