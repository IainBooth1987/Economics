Floating Exchange Rates: Determination, Depreciation and Appreciation

Exchange rate
The price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency.
Floating exchange rate
An exchange rate whose value is determined solely by the demand for, and supply of, that currency in the foreign exchange (FX) market, with no government or central bank intervention.
Appreciation
A rise in the value of a floating currency relative to another currency.
Depreciation
A fall in the value of a floating currency relative to another currency.

Under a floating system, the exchange rate is simply a price — determined, like any other price, by the intersection of a demand curve and a supply curve. The "good" being traded is one currency, priced in units of another.

A fall in demand for sterling (D₁ → D₂) — e.g. because foreigners want fewer UK exports — lowers the equilibrium exchange rate from e₁ to e₂: a depreciation. A rightward shift of demand (not shown) would instead cause an appreciation, and the same logic applies to shifts of supply.

Calculation — the price of a good in different currencies: if a laptop costs $1,200 in the United States and the exchange rate is £1 = $1.25, its price in pounds is $1,200 ÷ 1.25 = £960. If sterling then depreciates to £1 = $1.10, the same $1,200 laptop now costs $1,200 ÷ 1.10 = £1,091 — a depreciation raises the domestic-currency price of imports priced in a foreign currency, even though the foreign-currency price hasn't changed at all.
Real-world: the foreign exchange market is the largest and most liquid market in the world. The Bank for International Settlements' 2022 Triennial Survey recorded average daily global FX turnover of roughly $7.5 trillion, with the US dollar on one side of about 88% of all trades and the pound sterling, euro, and Japanese yen among the other most heavily traded currencies — all of which float freely against one another, their prices reset by market participants essentially continuously, 24 hours a day across global trading centres.
Link to 4.6: the exchange rate is one of the two accounts of the balance of payments most directly connected to this topic — the current account records the trade flows (exports/imports) that drive currency demand and supply, which is covered fully in 4.6.

Changes in Demand and Supply for a Currency

A wide range of factors shift the demand for, or supply of, a currency on the FX market — and therefore shift its floating exchange rate.

FactorEffect on the Currency
Foreign demand for a country's exportsHigher foreign demand for exports → foreigners must buy the domestic currency to pay for them → demand for the currency rises → appreciation.
Domestic demand for importsHigher domestic demand for imports → domestic residents sell their own currency to buy foreign currency → supply of the domestic currency rises → depreciation.
Inward / outward FDIInward foreign direct investment requires buying the domestic currency (appreciation pressure); outward FDI requires selling it (depreciation pressure).
Inward / outward portfolio investmentInflows of "hot money" into domestic bonds/shares raise demand for the currency (appreciation); outflows raise its supply (depreciation).
RemittancesMoney sent home by citizens working abroad is converted into the domestic currency, raising demand for it (appreciation pressure).
SpeculationIf speculators expect a currency to strengthen, they buy it now, raising demand and often causing a self-fulfilling appreciation (and vice versa for expected weakening).
Relative inflation ratesPersistently higher domestic inflation than trading partners erodes competitiveness, reducing export demand and increasing import demand — depreciation pressure over time.
Relative interest ratesHigher domestic interest rates (relative to abroad) attract inflows of portfolio investment seeking a better return, raising demand for the currency — appreciation pressure.
Relative growth ratesFaster domestic growth tends to raise import demand faster than export demand, increasing the currency's supply — depreciation pressure (though it can also attract investment inflows, working the other way).
Central bank interventionA central bank can buy or sell its own currency (using foreign reserves) to directly shift demand or supply and influence the exchange rate.
Link to 3.5: the interest rate channel above is the same interest rate set by a central bank for domestic monetary policy purposes (3.5) — a rate rise aimed at controlling inflation domestically has the side effect of attracting portfolio inflows and appreciating the currency, a form of "interest rate parity" that connects monetary policy directly to the exchange rate.

Interest Rate Differentials in Action: The 2024 Yen Carry-Trade Unwind

Jul 2024 — BoJ raises rate: −0.1% → 0.25%→ Interest rate differential with the US narrows sharply→ Yen "carry trade" positions rapidly unwound→ 5 Aug 2024 — Nikkei falls ~12% in a day
Real-world: for years, ultra-low Japanese interest rates encouraged a "carry trade": investors borrowed yen cheaply and used the funds to buy higher-yielding US dollar assets, continually selling yen and buying dollars. When the Bank of Japan unexpectedly raised its policy rate from −0.1% to 0.25% on 31 July 2024 — its most significant tightening in years — the interest rate differential that had made the carry trade so profitable narrowed abruptly. USD/JPY, which had reached about ¥161.7 per dollar in mid-July 2024, fell to around ¥141.7 by early August as the carry trade rapidly unwound: a roughly 12% appreciation of the yen in under three weeks. The sudden reversal contributed to a global equity sell-off, with Japan's Nikkei 225 index falling by around 12% on 5 August 2024 alone — one of its largest single-day falls on record — before markets stabilised over the following weeks as the BoJ signalled a cautious pace of further tightening.
Real-world — remittances and currency demand: the Philippines received an estimated US$38 billion in personal remittances from overseas Filipino workers (OFWs) in 2023 — equivalent to roughly 8% of GDP. This steady, largely recession-proof inflow is converted into pesos, providing a persistent source of demand for the currency that helps explain why the peso has often proven more stable than a simple trade-balance analysis alone would predict.
Note: speculation driving a currency sharply out of line with its underlying fundamentals is examined in full, with a detailed historical case study, in Section 4 (Fixed Exchange Rates) below — speculative attacks are most dramatic, and most examined, when they force the collapse of a fixed peg.

Consequences of Changes in the Exchange Rate

Economic IndicatorEffect of a DepreciationEffect of an Appreciation
Inflation rateTends to rise — imports (including imported raw materials and fuel) become more expensive in domestic-currency terms; exports become more price-competitive, raising demand and potentially domestic price pressures too.Tends to fall — imports become cheaper, easing cost pressures; export demand may weaken.
Economic growthTends to rise (ceteris paribus) — cheaper exports and dearer imports raise net exports (X−M), boosting AD.Tends to fall — exports become less competitive and imports cheaper, reducing net exports and AD.
UnemploymentTends to fall — export- and import-competing industries expand output and hire more workers.Tends to rise — export- and import-competing industries may contract, particularly in manufacturing.
Current account balanceTends to improve (over time, subject to the Marshall–Lerner condition) — export revenue rises and import spending falls in domestic-currency value.Tends to worsen — export revenue falls and import spending rises.
Living standardsMixed — cheaper exports support jobs and incomes, but dearer imports (especially food, fuel, and imported consumer goods) can squeeze real incomes and raise the cost of living.Mixed — cheaper imports raise real purchasing power, but job losses in exposed industries can lower incomes for affected workers.

A depreciation makes exports more price-competitive and imports more expensive, raising net exports (X−M) and shifting AD right (AD₁ → AD₂): both the price level (P₁ → P₂) and real output (Y₁ → Y₂) rise. An appreciation works in reverse, shifting AD left.

A Crisis Case Study: the UK's September 2022 "Mini-Budget"

23 Sep 2022 — unfunded tax-cut "mini-budget"→ 26 Sep 2022 — £ hits record low, $1.0327→ 28 Sep 2022 — BoE launches £65bn emergency gilt-buying→ 14 Oct 2022 — Kwarteng sacked→ Dec 2022 — £ recovers to ≈$1.20
Real-world: on 23 September 2022, UK Chancellor Kwasi Kwarteng announced a package of large, unfunded tax cuts (the "mini-budget") under Prime Minister Liz Truss. Financial markets reacted with alarm at the implied borrowing, and sterling fell to an all-time low of $1.0327 against the US dollar on 26 September 2022. The sell-off in UK government bonds (gilts) became so severe that it threatened the solvency of some pension funds, forcing the Bank of England to launch an emergency £65 billion gilt-buying programme on 28 September 2022 simply to restore market stability — separate from, and in tension with, its own interest-rate rises aimed at controlling inflation. Kwarteng was dismissed on 14 October 2022, and Truss resigned as Prime Minister on 20 October 2022, becoming the shortest-serving PM in UK history. Sterling gradually recovered to around $1.20 by December 2022 as the new government reversed most of the tax cuts. The episode illustrates the "living standards" row in the table above directly: with the UK importing roughly half its food, the depreciation raised fears of a further squeeze on the cost of living on top of the energy-price shock already underway that winter.
Why this matters for evaluation: the mini-budget crisis shows that exchange rate consequences are not purely mechanical "X−M" arithmetic — a depreciation driven by a collapse in market confidence (rather than by improved trade competitiveness) can coincide with a bond-market crisis and higher borrowing costs, working against growth even as net exports theoretically become more competitive. A strong evaluative answer distinguishes a "competitiveness-driven" depreciation from a "confidence-driven" one.

Fixed Exchange Rates

Fixed exchange rate
An exchange rate set and maintained by the government/central bank at a specific level (or narrow band) against another currency, a basket of currencies, or a commodity such as gold.
Devaluation
A deliberate downward adjustment of a fixed exchange rate by the authorities.
Revaluation
A deliberate upward adjustment of a fixed exchange rate by the authorities.

A fixed rate is maintained through central bank intervention: buying the domestic currency (using foreign reserves) if there is excess supply pushing the rate below the peg, or selling the domestic currency (accumulating reserves) if there is excess demand pushing it above the peg. Interest rate changes can reinforce this — raising rates to attract inflows and defend a peg under downward pressure.

The government fixes the rate above the free-market equilibrium. At e_fixed, quantity supplied (Qs) exceeds quantity demanded (Qd) — an excess supply of £. To hold the peg, the central bank must buy up the excess supply of its own currency using foreign reserves; if it stops (or runs out of reserves), the rate collapses back toward the true equilibrium.

A Fixed Rate Collapsing: the UK's 1992 "Black Wednesday"

Oct 1990 — UK joins ERM at DM 2.95/£→ 16 Sep 1992 — rates raised 10%→12%→15% in one day→ ≈£3.3bn of reserves spent defending the peg→ UK exits the ERM that evening→ £ floats, falls ≈15% against the Deutschmark
Real-world: the UK joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) — a system of semi-fixed rates that later fed into the euro — in October 1990, pegging the pound at around DM 2.95. By September 1992, sterling was widely seen as overvalued within the ERM, and speculators, most famously George Soros's Quantum Fund, bet heavily against the pound. On 16 September 1992 ("Black Wednesday"), Chancellor Norman Lamont raised UK interest rates twice in a single day — from 10% to 12%, then announced a rise to 15% — in a desperate attempt to attract capital inflows and defend the peg, while the Bank of England spent an estimated £3.3 billion of foreign currency reserves buying pounds. Neither measure worked: that evening, the UK withdrew from the ERM altogether, and the pound was allowed to float, falling by around 15% against the Deutschmark over the following weeks. Soros's fund reportedly made close to £1 billion in profit from the episode, earning him the nickname "the man who broke the Bank of England."

A Fixed Rate That Has Survived: the Hong Kong Dollar Peg

Real-world: in stark contrast, the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar since 17 October 1983, currently within a band of HK$7.75–7.85 per US$1 (since 2005). It is maintained not through occasional intervention but through a "currency board" system: the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) must hold US dollar reserves backing the entire stock of HK dollars in circulation, effectively removing discretion over domestic monetary policy — Hong Kong's interest rates move automatically to track those set by the US Federal Reserve. This peg survived the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis and the 2008 Global Financial Crisis without being abandoned, unlike several neighbouring floating and managed currencies that depreciated sharply in 1997.
Why this matters for evaluation: comparing Black Wednesday with the Hong Kong peg shows that whether a fixed exchange rate survives speculative pressure depends heavily on the credibility of the commitment and the scale of reserves/institutional backing (a strict currency board vs a semi-fixed ERM band) — not simply on "how fixed exchange rates are maintained" in the abstract. This comparison is valuable evidence for Paper 1 essays asking how, and how successfully, fixed exchange rates can be defended.

Managed Exchange Rates

Managed (dirty) float
An exchange rate that is allowed to float according to demand and supply, but which the central bank periodically intervenes to influence — without committing to a strict, publicly fixed rate.
Overvalued currency
A currency trading above the level that market fundamentals (demand and supply) alone would produce, often sustained through intervention.
Undervalued currency
A currency trading below the level that market fundamentals alone would produce, often sustained through intervention (e.g. to keep exports artificially competitive).

Under a managed float, the equilibrium rate (from D and S) is left to move within an unannounced target band, but the central bank intervenes — buying or selling its own currency — whenever the rate threatens to move outside that band, nudging it back rather than defending one single fixed number.

Managing a Major Currency: China's Renminbi

PBoC sets a daily reference rate against a currency basket→ Yuan trades within a ±2% band around it→ 11 Aug 2015 — reference rate cut ≈1.9% in one day
Real-world: China's yuan (RMB/CNY) is neither freely floating nor rigidly fixed. The People's Bank of China (PBoC) sets a daily "central parity" reference rate against a basket of currencies, and the yuan is then permitted to trade within a band (currently ±2%) around that rate, with the PBoC intervening as needed. On 11 August 2015, the PBoC cut the reference rate by around 1.9% in a single day — its largest one-day devaluation in over two decades — followed by further cuts over the next two days totalling roughly 3% cumulative depreciation. The "surprise devaluation" triggered a bout of global market turmoil as investors interpreted it as a signal of underlying weakness in the Chinese economy. The yuan's managed status has also made it politically contentious: the US Treasury formally labelled China a "currency manipulator" in August 2019, alleging the PBoC was keeping the yuan undervalued to support export competitiveness (a designation later removed in January 2020).

A Managed Peg Abandoned Overnight: the Swiss Franc "Floor"

Sep 2011 — SNB sets a CHF 1.20/€ floor→ Years of unlimited franc-selling to defend it→ 15 Jan 2015 — floor removed with no warning→ Franc jumps ≈30% against the euro in minutes
Real-world: from September 2011, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) had capped the franc's appreciation with a managed floor of CHF 1.20 per euro, defended through unlimited franc-selling intervention to protect Swiss exporters from a currency seen as dangerously overvalued amid safe-haven demand during the Eurozone debt crisis. On 15 January 2015, SNB Chairman Thomas Jordan abruptly removed the floor without any advance warning. The franc appreciated by roughly 30% against the euro within minutes — an event nicknamed "Francogeddon" — before settling closer to parity; several FX brokers worldwide collapsed from the resulting losses, and Swiss exporters faced a sudden, severe loss of competitiveness. The episode is a rare, clean real-world example of a managed system converting into a freely floating one almost instantaneously.

Why this matters for evaluation: real-world exchange rate systems rarely fit neatly into "fixed" or "floating" — many major currencies, including China's, sit somewhere in between, and even nominally floating currencies (the US dollar, the pound) have occasionally been subject to coordinated intervention. A high-scoring answer identifies exactly where a named currency sits on this spectrum rather than assuming every currency is a pure textbook case.

Exam tips:
  1. Always double-check which currency you are converting from and to — a common error is dividing when you should multiply, or vice versa; write the exchange rate as an equation ("£1 = $X") before substituting.
  2. When calculating a percentage change in currency value, always state clearly whether the result is an appreciation or a depreciation, not just a positive or negative number.
  3. Use specific real-world examples (Black Wednesday and the Hong Kong peg for fixed rates; the 2024 yen carry-trade unwind and the UK's 2022 mini-budget for floating-rate consequences; China's managed band and the Swiss franc floor removal for managed systems) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays and Paper 2 discuss/evaluate questions.
