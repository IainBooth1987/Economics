⚠️ Do banks just lend out savers' money?

Not quite. In the modern economy, when a bank makes a loan it creates a new deposit in the borrower's account; it does not simply hand out money that savers paid in (Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy, 2014). But banks still need liquid assets (cash and central bank reserves) to meet withdrawals and payments to other banks. A run drains exactly that liquidity, which is what this model shows.

Insured savers (blue ring) are covered up to the limit if the bank fails, so they are much less likely to panic, though not immune.

📐 Model assumptions
  • Panic spreads to nearby people with a fixed chance each moment. Insured savers are about 15× less likely to catch it, but not immune.
  • Each depositor withdraws £1; the bank pays one person at a time.
  • The Bank of England lends in £20 deliveries, only up to the value of eligible collateral: the bank's loans after a 25% haircut. Once collateral runs out, it cannot lend more.
  • These are illustrative settings to show the mechanism, not estimates of real behaviour.

🧠 Tell me why…

1 · Banks borrow short and lend long

Deposits can be withdrawn on demand, but mortgages and business loans run for years. This maturity transformation is what banks are for, and it means no bank holds enough cash to repay everyone at once. Note: lending itself creates new deposits rather than passing on existing ones, but banks still need liquid reserves to meet withdrawals and payments.

2 · Illiquid is not the same as insolvent

Insolvent: assets are worth less than liabilities. Illiquid: assets exceed liabilities but can't be turned into cash fast enough. A run can kill a perfectly solvent bank — watch the loans sitting untouched while the vault empties.

3 · A self-fulfilling prophecy

Diamond and Dybvig (1983) showed a bank has two equilibria. If everyone trusts it, nobody runs. If you think others will run, it's rational to run first, because withdrawals are paid first-come, first-served. Belief creates the failure.

4 · The two fixes

Deposit insurance (UK FSCS: up to £120,000 per eligible depositor, per authorised firm, since 1 Dec 2025; brands sharing one banking licence share one limit) greatly reduces the incentive to run, though it does not remove it entirely. The lender of last resort lends cash against good collateral. Walter Bagehot's 1873 rule: lend freely, at a high rate, against good collateral.

🎮 Run the Bank: can you survive the run?

You are the CEO. Each round brings news that moves depositor confidence. You get 2 action points a round to raise cash or calm nerves — then the depositors decide. Run out of cash and you fail (illiquid). Let equity fall below zero and you fail (insolvent). Survive every round to win.

Think as you play: does each action improve liquidity, solvency or confidence, or one at the expense of another? The debrief at the end will ask you.

Balance sheets are scaled so total assets start at 100bn, making the three banks easy to compare. Events are simplified versions of what really happened.

📐 Model assumptions: these numbers are teaching settings, not real data
  • Withdrawal rates rise with falling confidence. Uninsured depositors withdraw roughly 8× faster than insured ones, and social-media "heat" speeds this up.
  • Central bank lending costs a 2% fee. You can borrow against bonds (market value less a 5% haircut) and loans (65% of book value). Each loan has a 35–60% chance of leaking to the press, depending on the scenario.
  • Bonds sell at market value less 1% (worse with each extra sale that round). Loans fire-sell at 60p in the £.
  • Confidence penalties for leaks, losses, failed share sales and statements are fixed rules of thumb chosen to create interesting decisions.
  • Real banks have many more options and much more complex balance sheets. Use the game to reason about trade-offs, not to predict real outcomes.

Key concepts

Bank run: a large number of depositors try to withdraw their money at the same time because they fear the bank will fail.

Liquid reserves: banks hold only a small fraction of their deposits as cash and central bank reserves. No bank can repay all depositors at once.

How lending really works: the models in this resource simplify by showing deposits being "lent out". In practice, when a bank makes a loan it creates a new deposit for the borrower (Bank of England, 2014). Banks still need enough liquidity to meet withdrawals and payments to other banks, and a run drains that liquidity.

Maturity transformation: banks borrow short-term (deposits repayable on demand) and lend long-term (mortgages, business loans). This creates a liquidity mismatch.

Liquidity vs solvency: a bank is insolvent if its assets are worth less than its liabilities (negative equity). It is illiquid if it is solvent but cannot turn assets into cash quickly enough to meet withdrawals. Runs can bring down solvent banks.

Liquidity vs profitability trade-off: cash reserves earn little; loans earn more. Banks that hold too little cash are more profitable in calm times but more fragile.

Self-fulfilling expectations (Diamond–Dybvig, 1983): because withdrawals are paid first-come, first-served, it is individually rational to run if you expect others to run — even if the bank is sound.

Fire sale: selling assets quickly, at below their true value, to raise cash. Fire sales turn liquidity problems into losses that can make a bank insolvent.

Contagion and systemic risk: a run on one bank can spread to others perceived to be similar, threatening the whole financial system.

Policy responses. Deposit insurance — in the UK the FSCS protects up to £120,000 per eligible depositor, per authorised firm (raised from £85,000 on 1 December 2025). The limit applies per banking licence, so brands that share a licence (for example Halifax and Bank of Scotland) share one £120,000 limit. Joint accounts are covered up to £120,000 for each holder. Insurance greatly reduces the incentive to run, but does not remove it: savers above the limit, businesses, and people who fear delays in getting their money may still withdraw. Lender of last resort — the central bank lends to solvent but illiquid banks. Bagehot's rule (Lombard Street, 1873): lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral. Liquidity regulation — Basel III's Liquidity Coverage Ratio requires banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows. Moral hazard — insurance and bailouts can encourage riskier behaviour by banks and less monitoring by depositors.

Case study 1: Northern Rock, 2007

Northern Rock funded around three-quarters of its mortgage lending by borrowing in wholesale money markets rather than from retail deposits. When BNP Paribas froze funds exposed to US sub-prime mortgages on 9 August 2007, interbank lending dried up. On 13 September the BBC reported the bank had sought support from the Bank of England, which announced emergency liquidity support on 14 September. Queues formed outside branches from 14 to 17 September — the first UK bank run in roughly 150 years. Deposit protection at the time covered 100% of the first £2,000 and 90% of the next £33,000, so savers still had reason to run. On 17 September Chancellor Alistair Darling guaranteed all existing deposits and the queues stopped. Northern Rock was nationalised in February 2008.

Case study 2: Silicon Valley Bank, 2023

SVB's deposits came largely from tech start-ups and venture-capital funds, and around 94% were above the US $250,000 insurance limit. It invested heavily in long-dated government bonds when yields were low. As the Federal Reserve raised rates through 2022, those bonds fell in value. On 8 March 2023 SVB announced it had sold $21bn of securities at a $1.8bn loss and planned to raise $2.25bn in new capital. Venture-capital investors told portfolio companies to move their money; about $42bn was withdrawn on 9 March, with more queued for the next day. Regulators closed the bank on 10 March. On 12 March US authorities guaranteed all SVB deposits, and on 13 March HSBC bought SVB UK for £1. The run was faster than Northern Rock's because depositors were concentrated, uninsured, connected by group chats and able to withdraw on their phones.
