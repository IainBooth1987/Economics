How Commercial Banks Create Money HL ONLY

Most of the money in a modern economy is not physical cash issued by the central bank — it is created by commercial banks in the process of lending. Understanding this process explains why the central bank's tools (Section 2) are able to influence the total money supply, not just the policy interest rate.

Fractional Reserve Banking
Commercial banks are required to hold only a fraction of their deposits as reserves (cash or central bank deposits), and are free to lend out the rest.
Money Creation
When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a deposit — new money that did not exist before. That deposit is then spent and re-deposited elsewhere in the banking system, where a fraction is lent out again, and so on.
Minimum Reserve Requirement
The proportion of deposits a commercial bank is legally required to hold back rather than lend out, set by the central bank.
Money (Credit) Multiplier
The theoretical maximum expansion of the money supply from an initial deposit, given by 1 ÷ reserve requirement (expressed as a decimal).
Money Multiplier = 1 ÷ Reserve Requirement (as a decimal)
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: The Money Multiplier
A commercial bank receives a new deposit of $1,000. The minimum reserve requirement is 10% (0.10).

Money multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10

In theory, this single $1,000 deposit could support up to $10,000 of new money in the banking system as it is lent, spent, re-deposited, and lent again through multiple banks. In practice, the actual multiplier is usually smaller than the theoretical maximum — banks may choose to hold reserves above the legal minimum (excess reserves), and not every loan is re-deposited within the same banking system.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the Reserve Requirement
A central bank observes that the banking system's money multiplier is currently 8. Find the minimum reserve requirement implied by this multiplier.

Rearrange: Reserve requirement = 1 ÷ Money multiplier.
Reserve requirement = 1 ÷ 8 = 0.125, or 12.5%.

If the central bank later raises the reserve requirement to 20% to slow credit growth, the new (smaller) money multiplier becomes 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5 — always double-check that a rise in the reserve requirement produces a fall in the multiplier, and vice versa.
1
A customer deposits $1,000 in Bank A

Bank A must hold back the reserve requirement (say 10%, or $100) and is free to lend out the remaining $900.

2
Bank A lends $900 to a borrower

This $900 is new money credited to the borrower's account — it did not exist before the loan was made.

3
The borrower spends the $900

The recipient of that spending deposits it in Bank B.

4
Bank B holds back 10% ($90) and lends out $810

The process repeats at each bank in the chain, with each round of lending slightly smaller than the last.

5
The process continues until reserves are exhausted

Summing every round of new deposits eventually approaches the theoretical maximum given by the money multiplier.

Case Study: When Money Creation Goes Catastrophically Wrong — Zimbabwe, 2007–2009

The money multiplier process above describes commercial banks creating money through ordinary lending. A different, far more dangerous form of money creation occurs when a central bank prints money directly to finance government spending — a practice normally prohibited (Section 4 notes that even QE purchases bonds only in the secondary market, never directly from government). Zimbabwe under central bank governor Gideon Gono shows what happens when that boundary is abandoned entirely.

1
The government runs an unsustainable fiscal deficit

Following land redistribution from 2000 and a collapse in agricultural export earnings, tax revenue could not cover government spending.

2
The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ) prints money to cover the gap

Rather than borrowing from financial markets, the RBZ under Gono created new currency directly to fund the deficit — effectively unlimited "money creation" with no reserve constraint at all.

3
The money supply grows far faster than real output

With no growth in goods and services to match, the extra money chased the same (shrinking) quantity of goods.

4
Hyperinflation takes hold

By November 2008, economists Steve Hanke and Alex Kwok estimated Zimbabwe's monthly inflation rate at 79.6 billion percent — a price-doubling time of roughly 24.7 hours. The RBZ issued a 100-trillion-Zimbabwean-dollar banknote in January 2009.

5
Money velocity rises further, worsening the spiral

As people rushed to spend currency before it lost more value, velocity of circulation increased, adding further upward pressure on prices — a self-reinforcing spiral rather than a one-off shock.

6
The currency collapses and is abandoned

In April 2009, Zimbabwe formally abandoned its own currency, adopting the US dollar (and other foreign currencies) for domestic transactions — a direct consequence of the central bank having destroyed confidence in the money it created.

Why This Matters for Exam Evaluation: Zimbabwe is the clearest illustration of why central bank independence (introduced in the SL chapter) and legal limits on direct monetary financing of government exist in the first place — the money creation process this section describes as a source of credit and growth becomes destructive once it is used, unconstrained, to fund government deficits rather than to support ordinary bank lending. Venezuela's hyperinflation under President Nicolás Maduro from around 2016 followed a broadly similar pattern — a fiscal deficit financed by money printing at the central bank, feeding a self-reinforcing inflationary spiral — showing this is a repeatable failure mode, not a one-off historical curiosity.

Tools of Monetary Policy HL ONLY

The SL chapter treats "the policy interest rate" as the central bank's main lever. At HL, this needs unpacking into the specific tools a central bank actually uses — of which changing the policy rate is only one.

Open Market Operations (OMOs)
The central bank buys or sells government bonds in the open market. Buying bonds injects new reserves into the banking system (expansionary); selling bonds withdraws reserves (contractionary). This is the routine, day-to-day tool used to keep short-term market interest rates close to the announced policy rate.
Minimum Reserve Requirements
Raising the reserve requirement forces banks to hold back more of each deposit, shrinking the money multiplier and reducing the money supply (contractionary); lowering it has the opposite, expansionary effect.
Changes in the Central Bank's Minimum Lending Rate
The headline policy rate (called the base rate, discount rate, or refinancing rate in different countries) — the rate at which the central bank lends to, or accepts deposits from, commercial banks, which anchors the whole structure of interest rates in the economy (covered in depth in the SL chapter).
Quantitative Easing (QE)
A large-scale, sustained version of expansionary open market operations, used when the policy rate is already close to zero and conventional rate cuts are no longer available. The central bank creates new central bank reserves electronically to buy large quantities of government (and sometimes corporate) bonds, pushing bond prices up and long-term yields down across the economy.
ToolExpansionary UseContractionary Use
Open market operationsCentral bank buys bonds → reserves ↑Central bank sells bonds → reserves ↓
Minimum reserve requirementRequirement lowered → multiplier ↑Requirement raised → multiplier ↓
Policy (lending) rateRate cut → borrowing cheaperRate raised → borrowing dearer
Quantitative easingLarge-scale bond purchases → long-term yields ↓(Reverse: quantitative tightening — bond sales / non-reinvestment)
★ REAL WORLD: The Reserve Requirement in Practice — China
Not every major central bank relies mainly on interest rate changes. The People's Bank of China makes frequent, active use of the reserve requirement ratio (RRR) as a primary policy tool, adjusting it for banks system-wide (or for specific categories of bank) several times a year to fine-tune the amount of credit available in the economy — cutting the RRR to release liquidity and support growth, or raising it to cool an overheating credit cycle. This is a useful contrast to the US, UK and eurozone, where the reserve requirement is rarely changed and open market operations and the policy rate do most of the work.
★ REAL WORLD: Quantitative Easing After 2008
Facing policy rates already near 0%, the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan all launched large-scale QE programmes following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, buying trillions of dollars/pounds/euros/yen worth of government bonds to push down long-term borrowing costs and support aggregate demand when conventional rate cuts had been exhausted. The Bank of Japan's experience is the longest-running of the four: it had already been experimenting with large-scale asset purchases since the early 2000s in response to its own prolonged stagnation and deflation, more than half a decade before the term "quantitative easing" entered common use elsewhere.

The Money Market — Equilibrium Interest Rates HL ONLY

HL students are expected to show how the equilibrium interest rate is determined using a demand and supply of money diagram, and how central bank tools shift it.

Demand for Money
Downward sloping against the interest rate. At lower interest rates, the opportunity cost of holding money (rather than interest-bearing assets) is lower, so people and firms choose to hold more money.
Supply of Money
Drawn as a vertical line — at any point in time the money supply is fixed by the central bank's policy decisions (via the tools in Section 2), independent of the interest rate.
Equilibrium Interest Rate
The interest rate at which the quantity of money demanded equals the fixed quantity of money supplied — where the demand and supply of money curves intersect.
Fig: the money supply (MS) is fixed by the central bank at any point in time — drawn as a vertical line at Q*. Money demand (MD) is downward sloping. The equilibrium interest rate r* is set where MD intersects MS. An open market purchase or QE shifts MS(vertical) right, lowering r* towards the new intersection with MD.
1
Central bank buys bonds (OMO or QE)

New reserves are created and enter the banking system.

2
The money supply curve shifts right (MS1 → MS2)

At the fixed quantity axis, more money is now available at every interest rate.

3
At the old equilibrium rate, there is now excess supply of money

People and firms hold more money than they wish to at the old interest rate.

4
The interest rate falls (r1 → r2) to restore equilibrium

A lower rate increases the quantity of money demanded until it matches the new, larger money supply.

Reverse (contractionary): central bank sells bonds or raises the reserve requirement → money supply curve shifts left → at the old rate there is now excess demand for money → the interest rate rises to restore equilibrium at the new, smaller money supply.
Paper 3 Skill — Solving the Money Market Algebraically: HL students must be able to find the equilibrium interest rate from linear money demand and money supply functions, not just read it off a diagram — this is examined on Paper 3, the HL-only quantitative skills paper (worth 30% of the HL grade).
Md = a − br   |   Ms = fixed (set by the central bank)   |   Equilibrium: Md = Ms
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Equilibrium Interest Rate from Linear Functions
The demand for money in an economy is given by Md = 500 − 20r (where r is the interest rate in %), and the central bank has fixed the money supply at Ms = 300.

Step 1 — set Md = Ms: 500 − 20r = 300
Step 2 — solve for r: 20r = 500 − 300 = 200
Step 3: r = 200 ÷ 20 = 10%

The equilibrium interest rate is 10%. If the central bank then conducts an open market purchase, raising the money supply to Ms = 380: 500 − 20r = 380 → 20r = 120 → r = 6% — confirming algebraically what the diagram shows visually: a rightward shift in Ms lowers the equilibrium interest rate.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding Ms from a Given Equilibrium Rate
The demand for money is given by Md = 640 − 25r. The central bank wants the equilibrium interest rate to settle at exactly 4%. Find the money supply it must set.

Step 1 — substitute the target rate into Md: Md = 640 − 25(4) = 640 − 100 = 540.
Step 2 — set Ms = Md at equilibrium: Ms = 540.

The central bank must set the money supply at 540 for the market-clearing interest rate to be 4%. This reverse-direction skill — starting from a target interest rate and working back to the required Ms (or, equally, back to a missing coefficient in Md) — is just as examinable as solving forward from a given Ms.
1

Always state which value is being solved for and show each algebraic step — Paper 3 markschemes award method marks for correct working even if the final answer is wrong.

2

Sense-check the direction: an increase in Ms should produce a lower equilibrium r; if your algebra gives a higher r, re-check your signs.

Quantitative Easing (QE) and Quantitative Tightening (QT) HL ONLY

Quantitative easing (QE) is the flagship example of the Section 2 tools taken to their unconventional extreme. It is used when the policy rate is already at (or very near) the zero lower bound and conventional rate cuts are no longer available, but the economy still needs further stimulus.

Zero Lower Bound (ZLB)
The point at which the policy interest rate cannot practically be cut any further (at or close to 0%) without disrupting the banking system. Once at the ZLB, conventional rate cuts are exhausted.
Quantitative Easing (QE)
A large-scale, sustained programme of central bank asset purchases. The central bank creates new electronic reserves and uses them to buy large quantities of government bonds (and sometimes corporate bonds) from financial institutions, aiming to push down long-term interest rates directly, bypassing the now-exhausted policy rate.
Portfolio Rebalancing Channel
Having sold their bonds to the central bank, financial institutions "rebalance" their portfolios by reinvesting the proceeds in other assets — corporate bonds, equities, property — pushing up the prices of those assets too and spreading the stimulus well beyond the government bond market.
Quantitative Tightening (QT)
The reverse of QE — the central bank shrinks its balance sheet, either passively (letting bonds mature without reinvesting) or actively (selling bonds back into the market), withdrawing money from the financial system and pushing long-term yields back up.
Fig: at the zero lower bound, QE expands the money supply from MS₁ to MS₂ despite the policy rate having no further room to fall conventionally — the equilibrium rate falls from I%₁ to I%₂ via large-scale asset purchases rather than a policy rate announcement.

The QE Mechanism

1
Policy rate reaches the zero lower bound

Conventional rate cuts are exhausted, but the economy still needs further stimulus (a large negative output gap).

2
Central bank creates new electronic reserves

No physical money is printed — new reserves are created digitally within the banking system.

3
Central bank uses reserves to buy government bonds

Purchases are made from financial institutions in the secondary market, not directly from government (direct monetary financing is prohibited in most systems).

4
Increased demand for bonds pushes bond prices up

Higher bond prices mean lower bond yields (yield and price move inversely).

5
Long-term borrowing costs fall economy-wide

Government bond yields act as a benchmark for corporate borrowing costs and mortgage rates.

6
Portfolio rebalancing spreads the effect further

Institutions that sold bonds reinvest in corporate bonds and equities, pushing those asset prices up too.

7
Wealth effect and cheaper borrowing raise C and I

Higher asset prices increase household wealth; cheaper long-term borrowing supports investment.

8
Aggregate demand rises

The combined effect of the wealth effect and cheaper long-term credit stimulates AD despite the policy rate having no further room to fall.

The QT Mechanism (reverse): economy overheating / inflation above target, but rate rises alone judged insufficient → central bank allows bonds to mature without reinvestment, or actively sells bonds (QT) → bond supply in the market increases → bond prices fall → bond yields rise → long-term borrowing costs rise economy-wide → asset prices fall (negative wealth effect) → consumption and investment fall → aggregate demand contracts, reinforcing the effect of policy rate rises.
QE ≠ Guaranteed Lending: QE creates central bank reserves, not loans. Whether that translates into actual new lending and spending depends on bank and borrower confidence — in a severe downturn, newly created reserves may simply sit idle as excess bank reserves rather than being lent out. This is sometimes described as "pushing on a string": if nobody wants to borrow, creating more money to lend does not, by itself, guarantee more borrowing happens.

✓ Strengths of QE

Works when conventional policy cannot

QE gives central banks a way to keep stimulating the economy even once the policy rate has hit the zero lower bound.

Prevents deflationary spirals

By supporting asset prices and lending conditions, QE has been credited with helping prevent a much deeper downturn and outright deflation in several major economies.

✗ Limitations of QE

More reliable at preventing deflation than generating inflation

The scale of QE required to meaningfully raise inflation or growth has often proven larger than expected.

Distributional concerns

Because QE inflates asset prices (bonds, equities, property), it disproportionately benefits those who already own such assets, potentially worsening wealth inequality.

QT carries financial stability risks

Because QT has been used far less often than QE, its effects are less well understood — unwinding a large asset purchase programme too quickly can disrupt bond markets.

★ REAL WORLD: Quantitative Easing Across Major Economies
Following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan all launched large-scale QE programmes once their policy rates reached (or neared) zero — collectively purchasing trillions of dollars/pounds/euros/yen of government bonds. QE was expanded again by most major central banks during the COVID-19 pandemic. From 2022, several of these central banks began quantitative tightening — allowing bond holdings to run off or actively selling them — as a complement to policy rate rises in the fight against post-pandemic inflation.

The scale of balance-sheet expansion differed sharply between central banks, reflecting both the severity of the shock in each economy and how long each had already been experimenting with the tool:

True or False

Choose True or False for each statement — the explanation only appears once you've answered.

1

Commercial banks can create new money through the lending process.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Under fractional reserve banking, a bank credits a borrower with a new deposit when it makes a loan — this is newly created money, not a transfer of existing money.
2

The money multiplier is calculated as the reserve requirement divided by 1.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. The money multiplier is 1 ÷ reserve requirement (as a decimal), not the reserve requirement divided by 1.
3

Raising the minimum reserve requirement is an expansionary tool.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Raising the reserve requirement forces banks to hold back more of each deposit, shrinking the multiplier and reducing the money supply — a contractionary effect.
4

Open market operations involve the central bank buying or selling government bonds.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. OMOs are the central bank's routine tool for injecting or withdrawing reserves by buying or selling bonds in the open market.
5

Quantitative easing is typically used when the policy interest rate is already close to zero.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. QE is an unconventional tool used once conventional rate cuts have been exhausted at (or near) the zero lower bound.
6

On a money market diagram, the supply of money curve is upward sloping.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. The money supply curve is drawn as vertical — fixed by the central bank's policy decisions, independent of the interest rate.
7

An increase in the money supply, with demand for money unchanged, raises the equilibrium interest rate.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. An increase in money supply shifts the MS curve right; at the old rate there is excess supply, so the equilibrium interest rate falls, not rises.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. At lower interest rates, the opportunity cost of holding money rather than interest-bearing assets falls, so more money is demanded.

The Golden Chain: Explaining Quantitative Easing (HL)

Policy rate already close to the zero lower bound / conventional cuts exhausted → central bank launches a large-scale asset purchase (QE) programme → central bank creates new electronic reserves → uses them to buy government (and sometimes corporate) bonds from financial institutions → increased demand for bonds pushes bond prices up → bond yields (long-term interest rates) fall → cost of long-term borrowing falls for households and firms → on a money market diagram, the money supply curve shifts right (MS1 → MS2) → equilibrium interest rate falls → consumption and investment rise → aggregate demand increases → supports growth and the inflation target.

The Golden Chain: Raising the Minimum Reserve Requirement

Central bank judges the economy is overheating → raises the minimum reserve requirement → commercial banks must hold back a larger share of every deposit → the money multiplier falls → less new money is created through the lending process for a given level of deposits → the money supply curve shifts left → at the old interest rate there is now excess demand for money → the equilibrium interest rate rises → consumption and investment fall → aggregate demand contracts → inflationary pressure eases.

Key Exam Tips

1

Name specific tools: a top answer distinguishes open market operations, the reserve requirement, the policy lending rate, and QE — rather than treating "monetary policy" as a single undifferentiated lever.

2

Use the money market diagram deliberately: label MS (vertical) and MD (downward sloping) explicitly, and show the correct direction of shift for the tool being used.

3

Show the multiplier calculation with the formula stated explicitly (1 ÷ reserve requirement), and note that the actual multiplier is usually below the theoretical maximum in practice.

4

Connect back to the SL chain: HL tools ultimately work by shifting the money supply and equilibrium interest rate, which then feeds into the same consumption/investment/AD chain covered in the SL chapter — always finish an HL answer by linking back to AD and the output gap.

5

Use real-world QE examples (the Federal Reserve, Bank of England, ECB and Bank of Japan after 2008, and again during the COVID-19 pandemic) to earn application marks in essay and data-response questions.
