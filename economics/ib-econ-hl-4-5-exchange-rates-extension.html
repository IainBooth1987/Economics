Advantages and Disadvantages of Floating Exchange Rates HL ONLY

A freely floating exchange rate is not merely "unmanaged" — it performs a specific economic function: it automatically adjusts to absorb shocks, without a government having to hold reserves or set policy around defending a particular number.

Advantages

  • Automatic correction of a balance of payments disequilibrium — a current account deficit tends to depreciate the currency, which then improves competitiveness and helps correct the deficit, without deliberate policy action (see 4.6).
  • No need to hold large foreign currency reserves — since the rate is not defended at a specific level, the central bank does not need reserves purely to intervene.
  • Independent monetary policy is retained — interest rates can be set entirely for domestic objectives (inflation, output, employment — see 3.5) rather than to defend a peg.
  • Insulation from imported shocks — if a trading partner suffers high inflation, a floating currency can appreciate against it, partly shielding the domestic economy from imported inflation.
  • No risk of a costly, forced abandonment — there is no peg to defend, so no risk of the kind of reserve-draining speculative attack that can force a fixed rate to collapse (see the SL file's Black Wednesday case study).

Disadvantages

  • Exchange rate uncertainty — unpredictable rate movements raise the risk firms face on cross-border contracts, potentially deterring trade and foreign direct investment.
  • Vulnerability to destabilising speculation — self-fulfilling speculative swings can move a floating rate sharply away from its fundamentals in the short run (see the SL file's 2024 yen carry-trade unwind).
  • Can still transmit external shocks into domestic inflation — a sharp, confidence-driven depreciation raises import prices regardless of the underlying trade position (see the SL file's UK 2022 mini-budget case study).
  • Risk of competitive depreciation ("currency wars") — if several countries simultaneously try to depreciate to gain an export advantage, none may gain a lasting advantage while global trade is disrupted.
  • Complicates business planning — firms trading internationally must hedge currency risk (at a cost) or accept exposure to unpredictable swings in revenue and costs.

Automatic Adjustment in Action: the Australian Dollar and the Commodity Cycle

Real-world: the Australian dollar (AUD) is a heavily floated, commodity-linked currency. As global iron ore and coal prices fell sharply from 2011–2016 (the end of the "mining boom"), the AUD depreciated from around parity with the US dollar (roughly $1.05–1.10 in 2011) to around $0.70–0.72 by early 2016 — a fall of over 30%. This depreciation automatically cushioned Australia's mining-exposed economy: as commodity revenue fell, the weaker currency simultaneously made Australia's non-mining exports and tourism more competitive and raised the domestic-currency value of remaining commodity export earnings, helping offset the income shock without the Reserve Bank of Australia needing to spend reserves defending a peg, and without the kind of prolonged recession a fixed-rate economy facing the same shock might have needed to "internally devalue" through (compare the Eurozone periphery case in the SL 4.4 HL extension file).
Link to 4.6: this automatic correction mechanism is the standard theoretical answer to a persistent current account deficit under a floating exchange rate, covered in full in 4.6 — a floating rate is often described as providing a built-in "safety valve" that a fixed rate does not have.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Fixed Exchange Rates HL ONLY

A fixed exchange rate trades away the automatic adjustment floating provides in exchange for certainty and, potentially, imported monetary credibility — but only if the peg is genuinely sustainable.

Advantages

  • Certainty for trade and investment — firms can plan cross-border contracts without exchange rate risk, which can encourage trade and inward FDI.
  • Imported monetary discipline and credibility — pegging to a currency with a credible low-inflation record (e.g. the US dollar) can anchor inflation expectations in an economy whose own central bank lacks that credibility.
  • Avoids destabilising speculative volatility — a credible peg removes the day-to-day exchange rate swings that speculation can cause under a float.
  • Discourages competitive depreciation — a fixed rate cannot be used opportunistically to gain a short-term trade advantage.

Disadvantages

  • Loss of independent monetary policy — interest rates must be set to defend the peg (attracting or repelling capital flows), not to manage the domestic business cycle.
  • Requires large reserves to defend — and can still fail expensively if a peg is not credible (see the SL file's Black Wednesday case study).
  • Risk of a currency becoming misaligned — if underlying fundamentals shift but the peg does not, the currency can become persistently overvalued or undervalued, building pressure for a disorderly adjustment.
  • No automatic correction of external imbalances — a current account deficit does not self-correct through depreciation, so painful internal adjustment (wage and price cuts — "internal devaluation") may be the only route back to competitiveness.

When a Peg Fails: Argentina's Convertibility Plan (1991–2002)

Apr 1991 — peso pegged 1:1 to the US dollar→ Hyperinflation ends; peg holds through the 1990s→ Late 1990s — peso becomes overvalued→ Dec 2001 — bank deposits frozen ("corralito"); debt default→ Jan 2002 — peg abandoned; peso loses ≈70% of its value
Real-world: in April 1991, Argentine Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo introduced the "Convertibility Plan," pegging the peso 1:1 to the US dollar via a currency-board-style law requiring every peso in circulation to be backed by dollar reserves. It rapidly ended Argentina's hyperinflation, which had exceeded 3,000% in 1989, and the peg held through most of the 1990s. But Argentina's fiscal position deteriorated, and after Brazil devalued its currency in 1999 and the US dollar strengthened globally, the peso — rigidly locked to the dollar — became increasingly overvalued, eroding Argentine export competitiveness. Argentina had no ability to devalue without abandoning the law entirely, and confidence collapsed: the government froze bank deposits (the "corralito") in December 2001 and defaulted on roughly $95 billion of debt. The peg was finally abandoned in January 2002, and the peso lost around 70% of its value within months, accompanied by a deep recession and unemployment surging past 20%. This is the standard real-world case against a rigid fixed rate imposed without the fiscal discipline and institutional strength to sustain it — directly contrasting with the Hong Kong peg's survival, covered in the SL file.
Key HL evaluation point: comparing Hong Kong (peg survives, 1983–present) with Argentina (peg collapses, 1991–2002) shows that the sustainability of a fixed rate depends less on the exchange rate regime itself than on whether the underlying fiscal and monetary institutions are credible and disciplined enough to support it — precisely the kind of specific, evidenced comparison a top-band Paper 1 essay needs.

The Impossible Trinity and Choosing a System HL ONLY

Economists summarise the fundamental trade-off between exchange rate regimes with the impossible trinity (also called the "Mundell–Fleming trilemma," after economists Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming): a country can only ever have two of the following three things at once — it cannot have all three.

The impossible trinity: a country cannot simultaneously maintain (1) a fixed exchange rate, (2) free movement of capital across its borders, and (3) an independent monetary policy. Choosing any two means sacrificing the third.

Each side of the triangle represents choosing the two goals at its endpoints, sacrificing the one at the opposite corner. Hong Kong sits on the fixed-rate/free-capital-mobility side, sacrificing an independent monetary policy (it imports US interest rates). Historically capital-controlled China sat on the fixed-rate/independent-monetary-policy side, sacrificing free capital mobility. The UK, US, and Eurozone sit on the free-capital-mobility/independent-monetary-policy side, sacrificing a fixed rate (they float).

Why this table matters for evaluation: "should a government intervene to manage its exchange rate?" cannot be answered with a single universal rule — the right answer depends on a named country's specific circumstances: the credibility of its institutions (Hong Kong vs Argentina), how exposed it is to asymmetric shocks (Australia's commodity cycle vs a diversified economy), and how much it values monetary independence for its own business cycle (contrast with the Eurozone's monetary union trade-offs in the 4.4 HL extension file).
Bringing the case studies together for Paper 1: a strong answer to "to what extent should a government intervene to manage its exchange rate?" draws on multiple named, dated cases across the trinity — Hong Kong's currency-board peg surviving since 1983 by sacrificing monetary independence; Argentina's 1991 peg collapsing in 2001–02 once its institutions could not sustain it; the UK's 1992 Black Wednesday exit under speculative pressure; the Swiss National Bank's 2015 abrupt floor removal; and China's actively managed, partially capital-controlled yuan — rather than asserting in the abstract that "intervention is good" or "intervention is bad."

Where this shows up on the papers: the fixed-vs-floating evaluation is examinable on Paper 1 as an AO3 essay — "to what extent should a government intervene to manage its exchange rate?" or "evaluate the view that a fixed exchange rate is preferable to a floating one for a developing economy" are natural titles here, ideally supported by the named case studies above. On Paper 2, this HL content can appear in a 4-mark "using information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." sub-question anchored to a named paragraph, feeding into the paper's single 15-mark discuss/evaluate question. Paper 3 is HL-only and quantitative: each of its two ~30-mark questions is built from one extended data set with 7–8 short calculation sub-parts (mostly 2 marks, occasionally 4) climbing in difficulty — typically currency price-conversion arithmetic and calculating the percentage change in the value of a currency from a data table, plus one 4-mark diagram-explain sub-part (e.g. "using a diagram, explain how a central bank would defend a fixed exchange rate under speculative pressure"). The paper's final sub-part is usually a single 10-mark question of the form "using the text/data provided and your knowledge of economics, recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [a named country] to [address a named exchange rate problem]" — note this asks you to recommend, not evaluate or discuss, though top answers still justify the recommendation and note at least one limitation (e.g. recommending that a country facing chronic currency instability adopt a firmer managed band, while noting the cost in lost monetary policy flexibility).
% Change in Value of a Currency = [(New Rate − Old Rate) ÷ Old Rate] × 100
Worked example — change in currency value from a data set (forward):
A Paper 3 data table shows a currency moving from 1 unit = $0.85 to 1 unit = $0.68 over one year.

% change = [(0.68 − 0.85) ÷ 0.85] × 100 = −20.0% — a depreciation of 20%.
Worked example — change in currency value from a data set (reverse):
A currency appreciates by 25% to a new rate of 1 unit = $1.50. Find the original rate.

Old rate × (1 + 0.25) = 1.50 → Old rate = 1.50 ÷ 1.25 = 1 unit = $1.20
Price in Domestic Currency = Price in Foreign Currency ÷ Exchange Rate (foreign currency per unit of domestic)
Worked example — price of a good in different currencies (forward):
A machine costs $45,000 in the US. The exchange rate is 1 domestic unit = $0.75.

Price in domestic currency = 45,000 ÷ 0.75 = 60,000 units
Worked example — price of a good in different currencies (reverse):
The same machine costs 50,000 domestic units once converted, and its dollar price is known to be $40,000. Find the exchange rate (1 domestic unit = $X).

1 unit = $ (40,000 ÷ 50,000) = $0.80

Exam tips:
  • When asked to "recommend a policy" on Paper 3, always identify which of the impossible trinity's three goals the recommended policy would require sacrificing — this demonstrates the HL depth examiners are looking for, rather than a generic SL-level policy list.
  • In a fixed-vs-floating evaluation essay, always name specific countries and dates (Hong Kong since 1983; Argentina 1991–2002; the UK's 1992 Black Wednesday; Switzerland's 2015 floor removal) rather than asserting the trade-offs in the abstract.
  • Remember that a country's exchange rate choice is not fixed forever — several of the case studies here (the UK, Switzerland, and, temporarily, Argentina) show real transitions between regimes, often triggered by a specific, dateable crisis.
