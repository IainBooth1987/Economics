What is fiscal drag?

Fiscal drag happens when tax thresholds are frozen, or rise more slowly than incomes. As pay rises — whether through inflation or real growth — more of each person's income falls into taxable bands, and more people cross into higher bands. Government revenue rises automatically, even though no tax rate has changed.

Key definition
Fiscal drag is the increase in tax revenue that occurs when tax thresholds and allowances are not raised in line with inflation or earnings growth, pulling more income — and more people — into tax and into higher tax bands. It is often called a "stealth tax" because it raises revenue without any announced rise in tax rates.

The UK freeze: a timeline

March 2021 · Rishi Sunak's Budget. The personal allowance (£12,570) and higher-rate threshold (£50,270) are frozen from April 2021 until April 2026. At the time inflation was low, so the freeze was expected to raise a modest sum.
2022 · Inflation surges past 10%. With wages rising quickly, the freeze raises far more than planned. By March 2022 the IFS estimated it would raise about £20.5bn — around two and a half times the original estimate.
November 2022 · Jeremy Hunt's Autumn Statement. The freeze is extended to April 2028.
April 2023. The additional-rate (45%) threshold is cut from £150,000 to £125,140, pulling more people into the top band.
November 2025 · Rachel Reeves's Budget. The freeze is extended by a further three years to April 2031, including National Insurance thresholds.
October 2026 · John Healey's first Budget. Despite calls to raise the personal allowance, the main thresholds stay frozen. A separate relief from April 2027 means pensioners whose only income is the full new state pension will not pay income tax.

How it works: a worked example

Year 1

Salary £30,000. Taxable income = £30,000 − £12,570 = £17,430. Tax at 20% = £3,486 (11.6% of pay).

Year 2 · 5% inflation

Salary rises 5% to £31,500 — no real pay rise. Taxable income = £18,930. Tax = £3,786 (12.0% of pay).

The drag

If the allowance had risen 5% to £13,199, tax would be £3,660. The freeze costs this worker £126 — and the gap grows every year.

Notice
The worker's tax rose by 8.6% while their pay rose by 5%. In a progressive tax system, frozen thresholds make tax revenue grow faster than incomes. Economists say the income elasticity of tax revenue is greater than 1.

The 60% trap

Between £100,000 and £125,140, every extra £2 earned removes £1 of personal allowance. That £1 is then taxed at 40%, so each extra £100 earned costs £40 + £20 = £60 in tax. The £100,000 taper point has not changed since 2010, so fiscal drag pulls ever more doctors, head teachers and engineers into it. Many respond by paying more into pensions or cutting their hours.

Tell me why…

Why?Why did the freeze raise so much more than the government expected in 2021?

The freeze was announced when inflation was close to 1%. When inflation surged above 10% in 2022, nominal wages rose quickly too, so far more income was pulled into tax than forecast. The faster pay rises, the more a fixed threshold "drags".

Why?Why is fiscal drag called a "stealth" tax?

No rate rises and nothing new is announced each year — the government simply does nothing. Workers see their gross pay rise and often don't notice that a larger share is going in tax.

Why?Why can fiscal drag occur even when inflation is zero?

If real wages grow, incomes still rise relative to fixed thresholds, so more income is taxed at higher rates. Inflation makes the effect much bigger and faster, but real earnings growth on its own also causes drag.

Why?Why does the 60% band discourage some high earners from working more?

Keeping only £40 of every extra £100 earned weakens the incentive to take on extra hours, a promotion or overtime. Some workers, such as NHS consultants, have been reported to turn down extra shifts or pay more into pensions to stay below £100,000.

Below £12,570

No income tax, so no drag — but as pay rises, low earners are pulled into tax for the first time. That is where most of the 5.2 million new taxpayers come from.

Basic-rate taxpayers

The frozen allowance costs every basic-rate taxpayer the same cash sum. As a share of income, that hits lower earners harder — the regressive part of the freeze.

Around £50,000 and £100,000+

Watch the jumps: above the frozen higher-rate threshold the drag is several times larger, because income is pushed from 20% to 40% tax. The frozen £100,000 taper creates the largest cash hit of all.

Exam insight: progressive or regressive?
In cash terms the freeze costs higher earners more. As a percentage of income, the effect is largest for some low earners just above the allowance and for those in the taper zone. A strong answer separates the two and uses evidence, rather than calling it simply "regressive".

Tell me why…

Why?Why is the cash cost of the frozen personal allowance the same for a £20,000 and a £45,000 earner?

Both pay 20% on the slice of income that a higher allowance would have protected. The size of that slice depends on how far the allowance has fallen behind prices, not on how much they earn, so the cash loss is identical. As a share of income, it is more than twice as large for the £20,000 earner.

Why?Why does the line jump at the higher-rate threshold?

Above £50,270, two frozen thresholds bite at once: the allowance and the higher-rate threshold. Income that would still have been taxed at 20% under indexed thresholds is now taxed at 40%.

Why?Why might fiscal drag widen regional inequality?

National pay scales (nurses, teachers) and regional living costs differ. A frozen higher-rate threshold pulls in many London and South East professionals whose high nominal pay buys less, while low earners everywhere see the allowance shrink in real terms.

Evaluating fiscal drag

✓ Arguments for freezing thresholds

  • Raises large sums without breaking manifesto pledges not to raise income tax, NI or VAT rates.
  • Helps reduce the deficit and meet fiscal rules, funding public services.
  • Spreads the burden widely across millions of taxpayers in small amounts.
  • Acts as contractionary fiscal policy when inflation is high, supporting the Bank of England.
  • Unlike rate rises, it does not raise marginal rates for most taxpayers.

✗ Arguments against

  • Opaque: a tax rise that voters never see announced, which undermines trust and accountability.
  • Erodes real take-home pay during a cost-of-living crisis.
  • Pulls low earners into tax and shrinks the real value of the allowance.
  • Creates high marginal rates (40% and 60% bands) that weaken work incentives.
  • Reduces disposable income and consumption, so lower aggregate demand and growth.

The macroeconomic picture

  • Aggregate demand: higher tax takes reduce disposable income and consumption (C), shifting AD left — similar to an explicit tax rise, though households may not notice immediately.
  • Inflation: during 2022–24 fiscal drag tightened fiscal policy while the Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 5.25%, so fiscal and monetary policy pulled in the same direction against inflation.
  • Automatic stabiliser: a progressive tax system takes a larger share of income in booms and less in slumps. Freezing thresholds strengthens this effect when nominal incomes are rising fast.
  • Public finances: fiscal drag is one of the main reasons the UK tax burden has risen to its highest level since the Second World War.
  • Supply side: higher marginal tax rates may reduce labour supply at the margin — fewer hours, earlier retirement or more pension saving to avoid the 60% band.

Tell me why…

Why?Why might a government prefer fiscal drag to an explicit income tax rate rise?

It avoids breaking pledges and the headline "income tax up", and the cost is spread thinly and invisibly across millions of people. Politically it is far less costly, even if the revenue raised is similar.

Why?Why could fiscal drag help the Bank of England bring inflation down?

By reducing disposable incomes, it lowers consumer spending and demand-pull inflationary pressure. The Bank then does not need to raise interest rates as far to hit its 2% target.

Why?Why might the effect on consumer spending be smaller than an equivalent announced tax rise?

Because households may not notice it, they may not cut spending in anticipation; and because the extra tax is spread thinly, it may come partly out of saving. On the other hand, it continues and grows every year the freeze lasts.
