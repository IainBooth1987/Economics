Aggregate Demand (AD)

Aggregate demand (AD) is the total quantity of goods and services demanded in an economy at a given price level, over a given period of time. Unlike a demand curve for a single good, AD represents spending across the entire economy.

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
Consumption (C)
Household spending on goods and services — typically the largest component of AD in most economies.
Investment (I)
Firms' spending on capital goods (machinery, buildings, technology) intended to expand or maintain future productive capacity.
Government Spending (G)
Government current and capital expenditure on goods and services (excludes transfer payments, which are not a purchase of output).
Net Exports (X − M)
The value of exports (foreign spending on domestic output) minus imports (domestic spending on foreign output).

The AD curve slopes downward — a fall in the price level raises AD — for three distinct reasons, none of which is the same as the reason an individual demand curve slopes downward:

EffectMechanism
Wealth effectA lower price level raises the real value of household savings and other fixed-value assets, making consumers feel wealthier and spend more.
Interest rate effectA lower price level reduces the demand for money, lowering interest rates, which increases both consumption and investment.
Exchange rate / international trade effectA lower domestic price level makes exports relatively cheaper and imports relatively more expensive, raising net exports.

Each component of AD has its own determinants — factors, other than the price level, that cause the whole AD curve to shift:

ComponentKey Determinants
CConsumer confidence, interest rates, household wealth, income taxes, level of household indebtedness, expectations of future price levels.
IInterest rates, business confidence, technology, business taxes, level of corporate indebtedness.
GPolitical and economic priorities of the government in power.
X − MIncome of trading partners, exchange rates, trade policies.
Fig: a rightward shift of AD (AD₁ → AD₂) raises both the price level (P₁ → P₂) and real output (Y₁ → Y₂) in the short run. A leftward shift produces the reverse.
★ REAL WORLD: India's Consumption-Driven Growth Model
India's AD is unusually dominated by household consumption (C), which typically accounts for around 55–60% of GDP — a considerably higher share than in export-oriented economies such as Germany or South Korea. This means Indian AD is especially sensitive to determinants of C: rural incomes (heavily influenced by monsoon rainfall and agricultural output), consumer confidence, and the availability of consumer credit. When India experienced a sharp but temporary AD contraction after its 2016 demonetisation policy (which withdrew high-value banknotes from circulation to combat unaccounted "black money"), the effect on growth was significantly larger than in economies where trade or investment plays a bigger role in AD — illustrating that the same shock can have very different AD consequences depending on an economy's underlying expenditure structure.
★ REAL WORLD: Confidence Collapse and AD — Argentina's Recurring Currency Crises
Argentina has experienced repeated bouts of collapsing consumer and business confidence tied to currency crises and very high inflation (exceeding 100% annually at various points in the 2020s). When households and firms lose confidence that the peso will hold its value, consumption and investment plans are disrupted — people rush to convert pesos into US dollars or physical assets rather than spend or invest domestically in peso terms, and businesses postpone investment amid extreme uncertainty. This illustrates that "consumer and business confidence" is not simply a vague psychological factor in the AD determinants table — in economies with a history of currency instability, confidence effects on C and I can be dramatic and immediate, unlike in economies with a longer track record of price stability.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

The short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve shows the total quantity of goods and services firms are willing to supply at each price level, in the short run — a period in which at least one factor of production (conventionally, wages) is fixed or slow to adjust.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)
The relationship between the price level and the quantity of real output firms are willing to supply, holding wages and other input costs fixed. Slopes upward: a higher price level, with costs fixed, raises firms' profit margins, encouraging more output.

The SRAS curve shifts due to changes in the cost of production, independent of the price level of final output:

DeterminantEffect
Costs of factors of productionRising wages, energy prices, or raw material costs shift SRAS left; falling costs shift SRAS right.
Indirect taxesA rise in indirect taxes (VAT, excise duties) raises firms' effective costs at each price level, shifting SRAS left; a cut shifts SRAS right.
Fig: a leftward shift of SRAS (SRAS₁ → SRAS₂), caused by rising production costs, raises the price level (P₁ → P₂) and lowers real output (Y₁ → Y₂) simultaneously — cost-push "stagflation", the opposite pattern to an AD shift.
★ REAL WORLD: Sri Lanka's 2022 Economic Crisis — Cost-Push Inflation from a Foreign Exchange Shortage
Sri Lanka experienced a severe foreign exchange shortage in 2022, driven by a combination of depleted reserves, high external debt, and a sudden loss of tourism revenue. Because Sri Lanka imports the great majority of its fuel and much of its fertiliser and other production inputs, the shortage of foreign currency directly raised the domestic cost of imported inputs, sharply shifting SRAS left. Inflation exceeded 50% at its peak while the economy simultaneously contracted — a textbook example of cost-push stagflation, and one considerably more severe than the cost-push episodes typically cited in higher-income economies, since Sri Lanka lacked the foreign currency reserves to cushion the shock.
★ REAL WORLD: Turkey's Currency Depreciation and Imported Cost-Push Inflation
Turkey experienced a sharp depreciation of the lira through the early 2020s, driven partly by an unorthodox monetary policy of cutting interest rates despite already-high inflation. A weaker currency directly raises the domestic-currency cost of imported inputs (energy, raw materials, intermediate goods), shifting SRAS left even without any change in world commodity prices. Turkish inflation exceeded 60% at points during this period — illustrating that a domestic policy choice (an unusually loose monetary stance) can itself become a source of cost-push pressure by driving the exchange rate channel, rather than cost-push inflation always originating from an external commodity shock.

Alternative Views of Aggregate Supply

While SRAS describes the short run, economists disagree fundamentally about the shape of aggregate supply in the long run — and this disagreement is one of the central theoretical debates in the entire macroeconomics unit.

Monetarist / New Classical LRAS
A vertical line at the full-employment (potential) level of output, Yf. In this view, output in the long run is determined entirely by the quantity and quality of factors of production — not by the price level — because wages and prices are assumed to be fully flexible, adjusting to clear all markets.
Keynesian AS Curve
A curve with three distinct sections: a flat (horizontal) section at low output levels where there is substantial spare capacity, a rising middle section as the economy approaches full capacity and firms compete for increasingly scarce resources, and a vertical section once full capacity is reached. Reflects the assumption that wages and prices are "sticky" downward, especially when there is spare capacity.
Fig: the Keynesian AS curve. With substantial spare capacity (the flat section), an AD shift changes output but barely affects the price level — the opposite of the monetarist/new classical prediction, where any AD shift only ever affects the price level in the long run.
Two Schools of Thought: the disagreement is not really about the diagram — it is about whether wages and prices are flexible enough, in practice, to clear markets quickly. Monetarists/new classical economists argue that flexible wages mean the economy always returns to full employment (Yf) on its own — recessions are self-correcting, and government intervention is unnecessary or even harmful. Keynesians argue that wages are sticky, especially downward, so an economy can remain stuck below full employment for a prolonged period without active demand-side intervention — hence the case for counter-cyclical fiscal and monetary policy.

When AD and SRAS intersect away from the full-employment level of output, an output gap exists:

Fig: a positive (inflationary) output gap — actual output (Y1) exceeds potential output (Yfe). The economy is producing beyond its sustainable capacity, generating inflationary pressure.
★ REAL WORLD: Turkey's Positive Output Gap Under "Erdoganomics"
Turkey's government pursued a deliberate strategy of very low interest rates through the early 2020s to maximise short-run growth, despite already-high inflation — sometimes called "Erdoganomics" after President Erdoğan's stated belief that lower rates would ultimately reduce, rather than raise, inflation. The result was a economy consistently operating with a positive output gap: strong demand-driven growth well beyond what could be sustained without accelerating prices, alongside the cost-push pressure described in Section 2. Turkish inflation persisted above 60% for an extended period, illustrating the standard monetarist/new classical prediction that attempting to hold output above potential indefinitely generates accelerating inflation rather than a permanent gain in output.
Fig: a negative (deflationary/recessionary) output gap — actual output (Y₂) falls short of potential output (Y*), following a leftward AD shift. Spare capacity and unemployment result.
★ REAL WORLD: Japan's Persistent Negative Output Gap
Japan spent long stretches of the 1990s and 2000s with real GDP persistently below most economists' estimates of its potential output, following the collapse of its asset price bubble (see the credit-crunch discussion in 3.5). Unlike the short, sharp negative output gaps typical of a standard recession, Japan's gap proved remarkably persistent — a central piece of evidence Keynesian economists cite against the monetarist/new classical prediction that wage and price flexibility should close such a gap reasonably quickly. Japan's experience of very low, sometimes negative, inflation for an extended period is broadly consistent with an economy stuck for years with AD undershooting its productive capacity.

Shifts of AS Over the Long Run

Whichever model of aggregate supply is used, the curve representing an economy's productive capacity can itself shift over the long run — a genuine change in what the economy is capable of producing, distinct from a movement caused by a change in AD.

Changes in the Quantity of Factors of Production
More labour (population growth, higher participation, immigration), more capital (investment), or more land/natural resources discovered or made usable shifts LRAS/Keynesian AS to the right.
Changes in the Quality of Factors of Production
A more skilled workforce (education and training) or more productive capital raises output per unit of input, shifting AS right without necessarily using more resources.
Improvements in Technology
New production methods or products allow more output from the same quantity of factors of production.
Increases in Efficiency
Better allocation of existing resources — through competition, deregulation, or improved management practice — raises output without requiring new resources.
Changes in Institutions
Stronger property rights, more effective and less corrupt legal and regulatory institutions, and political stability all lower the cost and risk of production and investment, supporting long-run AS growth.
Fig: a rightward shift of LRAS (LRAS₁ → LRAS₂) — long-term economic growth. Potential output rises (YF₁ → YF₂) and, at a given AD, the equilibrium price level actually falls (P₁ → P₂), unlike the effect of an AD-driven expansion.
★ REAL WORLD: Vietnam's Factor-Accumulation-Driven Growth
Vietnam has sustained rapid LRAS growth since the 1990s largely through a huge increase in the quantity of factors of production available for manufacturing: a young, growing, increasingly urbanised workforce moving out of subsistence agriculture, combined with very large inflows of foreign direct investment funding new factories, ports and industrial zones (particularly in electronics assembly for firms such as Samsung). This is a useful contrast with growth driven mainly by rising quality of factors of production (education-led growth, as in South Korea) or by institutional improvements alone — Vietnam's experience shows that simply mobilising more labour and capital into higher-productivity sectors can itself generate substantial LRAS growth, even before major gains in worker skill levels or domestic technology have occurred.
★ REAL WORLD: Botswana's Institutions-Driven Growth
Botswana is one of the most frequently cited African development success stories, having sustained one of the world's fastest long-run growth rates since independence in 1966 — despite, like several of its neighbours, being highly dependent on a single natural resource (diamonds). Economists commonly attribute Botswana's outperformance relative to other resource-rich developing economies to strong institutions: comparatively low corruption, secure property rights, prudent management of resource revenue (including a sovereign wealth fund), and political stability. This is a leading real-world illustration of "changes in institutions" as a distinct determinant of LRAS growth — the same natural resource endowment produced very different LRAS growth trajectories depending on the quality of the institutions managing it.

Macroeconomic Equilibrium

Macroeconomic equilibrium occurs where AD intersects AS, determining the economy's price level and level of real output simultaneously.

Fig: short-run macroeconomic equilibrium, where AD intersects SRAS, determining P1 and Y1 simultaneously. This equilibrium is temporary — it need not coincide with the full-employment level of output.

Equilibrium in the Monetarist/New Classical Model

1
The economy operates below full employment (a negative output gap)

AD intersects SRAS to the left of the vertical LRAS.

2
Spare capacity means unemployed workers compete for jobs

This puts downward pressure on wages, since labour supply exceeds labour demand.

3
Falling wages lower firms' costs, shifting SRAS right

Lower costs allow firms to supply more output at every price level.

4
The economy automatically returns to full-employment equilibrium at Yf

No government intervention is required — wage flexibility alone restores full employment. Unemployment at this final equilibrium equals the natural rate of unemployment (frictional plus structural unemployment) — not zero, but the rate consistent with a fully "cleared" labour market.

Equilibrium in the Keynesian Model

If wages and prices are sticky downward — particularly because of contracts, minimum wage laws, or workers' resistance to nominal pay cuts — the automatic adjustment mechanism described above breaks down. A negative output gap can then persist indefinitely: the equilibrium level of output does not have to equal the full-employment level of output. This is the theoretical foundation for active government intervention (fiscal or monetary demand stimulus) covered in 3.5 and 3.6 — in the Keynesian model, waiting for automatic adjustment can mean an unnecessarily long and costly period of unemployment.
Exam Tip — Assumptions and Implications: a strong evaluation answer identifies that this entire debate reduces to a single empirical question: how quickly, and how completely, do wages and prices adjust? If wages are flexible (monetarist/new classical assumption), intervention is unnecessary and may even be destabilising. If wages are sticky (Keynesian assumption), intervention can shorten a costly recession. Real-world evidence is mixed and often depends on the specific labour market and time period being examined — this is precisely why the debate has persisted for nearly a century.

Where the Debate Came From: A Century of Macroeconomic Argument

📖 History: From the Great Depression to Stagflation

The Keynesian model is not an abstract textbook invention — it emerged from a specific historical failure. During the Great Depression of the 1930s, unemployment in the United States and United Kingdom exceeded 20% for years on end, apparently contradicting the pre-existing "classical" view that flexible wages would automatically restore full employment. In 1936, the British economist John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, arguing that wages were "sticky" downward (workers strongly resist nominal pay cuts, and contracts/minimum wage laws reinforce this) and that a market economy could therefore settle into a persistent negative output gap with no automatic tendency to full employment. His policy prescription — active government spending to boost AD directly — shaped Western economic policy for the next four decades, underpinning the US New Deal's later phases and post-war British and American fiscal management.

The monetarist/new classical challenge came later. Through the 1950s–60s, the American economist Milton Friedman (University of Chicago) argued that Keynesian demand management would only work temporarily: workers and firms adjust their expectations of inflation, and any government attempt to permanently push unemployment below its "natural rate" through demand stimulus would eventually just generate accelerating inflation, not extra output. The 1970s appeared to prove this point emphatically: the OPEC oil price shocks of 1973 and 1979 produced stagflation — simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment — in the US, UK and much of Western Europe, a combination the simple Keynesian model struggled to explain (since it treated inflation and unemployment as an inverse trade-off, the Phillips Curve). US Federal Reserve Chair Paul Volcker's sharp interest rate rises in 1979–82 (raising the federal funds rate above 19%) broke US inflation at the cost of a deep recession, an episode widely read as vindicating the monetarist emphasis on controlling the money supply and expectations.

The 2008 Global Financial Crisis then swung professional and political opinion back towards Keynesian demand management: most major economies (including the US under the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, and the UK) ran large fiscal stimulus programmes, and central banks used near-zero interest rates plus quantitative easing, treating sticky wages and a persistent negative output gap as the dominant short-run risk. The debate remains genuinely live in current policy: it reappeared in 2021–23 as economists disagreed over whether post-pandemic inflation was better tackled by tightening demand (a monetarist-style response) or judged transitory and best left to fade as supply chains recovered (a view closer to the Keynesian instinct to avoid unnecessary output loss).

Exam Tip: naming this history (Keynes/1936/Depression; Friedman/1970s stagflation; Volcker disinflation) is exactly the kind of real-world example that earns application marks in a Paper 1 essay evaluating demand-side versus supply-side/automatic adjustment views of the macroeconomy — far stronger than an unsupported assertion that "economists disagree".

Where this shows up on the papers: in a Paper 1 essay, part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain how a named factor shifts AD or AS — reward comes from a correctly drawn and labelled diagram plus a clear step-by-step mechanism; part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) then asks you to evaluate or discuss, almost always by weighing the Keynesian vs monetarist/new classical adjustment debate, or the short-run vs long-run distinction, using named real-world examples. Paper 2 has no (a)/(b) essay split — it is a single data-response question built from two linked "Text" extracts with numbered paragraphs plus data tables, and every sub-question cites an exact reference (e.g. "Text A, paragraph 2" or "Table 1"); expect two 2-mark "define the term..." parts on bolded terms from the text, then a calculation (2–3 marks) often paired with a 2-mark "sketch a [named] diagram to show..." part (a small in-text diagram — "draw" is reserved for a larger standalone request), then a run of 4-mark "using [a named diagram]/information from [Text/Table], explain how/why..." parts each anchored to a specific paragraph or table, ending in one 15-mark "discuss" or "evaluate" question that weighs something specific — often framed around AD/AS adjustment, self-correction, or a named policy response using the extract's data. HL students also meet AO4 output-gap calculations, including reverse-direction ones, on Paper 3 — an extended data set of 2-mark (occasionally 4-mark) calculations climbing in difficulty, usually including a 4-mark "using a [named] diagram, explain..." part, and finishing with a single 10-mark question that asks the candidate to "recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to address [a named problem]" — not "evaluate" or "discuss", though a top-mark answer still justifies the recommendation and acknowledges a limitation or counterargument.

The Golden Chain: From an AD Shock to a New Equilibrium

Identify the shock and which component of AD (or SRAS) it affects → determine the direction of the shift (state the specific determinant, e.g. a rise in consumer confidence shifts AD right) → draw the correct diagram, shifting the correct curve → identify the new equilibrium price level and output → state whether an output gap exists, and if so, whether it is positive or negative → evaluate: would a monetarist/new classical economist and a Keynesian economist disagree about whether the economy will self-correct, and if so, why?
Output Gap (%) = ((Actual GDP − Potential GDP) ÷ Potential GDP) × 100
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Size of an Output Gap

Data: Actual real GDP = $980bn. Potential (full-employment) GDP = $1,000bn.

Step 1: Output gap = ((980 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000) × 100 = −2%.

A negative sign means this is a negative (deflationary/recessionary) output gap — the economy is producing 2% below its potential.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding Actual GDP from a Stated Output Gap

Question: An economy has a potential GDP of $500bn and a positive output gap of +3%. Find actual GDP.

Step 1: Rearrange: Actual GDP = Potential GDP × (1 + output gap as a decimal).

Step 2: Actual GDP = 500 × (1 + 0.03) = $515bn.

Key Exam Tips

1

Always specify which curve is shifting and why — a change in AD is a completely different diagram, and a completely different set of policy implications, from a shift in SRAS or LRAS. Never just say "supply and demand changed".

2

When asked to evaluate, engage explicitly with the monetarist/new classical vs Keynesian disagreement — this is one of the most heavily rewarded evaluation angles across the whole macroeconomics unit.

3

Distinguish carefully between a movement along a curve (caused by a change in the price level) and a shift of the curve (caused by a change in one of its determinants) — a very common source of lost marks.

4

Distinguish a short-run shift (SRAS, caused by input costs) from a long-run shift (LRAS/Keynesian AS at full capacity, caused by changes in the quantity, quality or efficiency of factors of production, or institutions) — these are different curves representing different time horizons.

5

Use real-world context to earn application marks: reference India's consumption-driven AD, Sri Lanka's or Turkey's cost-push crises, Japan's persistent negative output gap, or Vietnam's and Botswana's contrasting paths to LRAS growth.
