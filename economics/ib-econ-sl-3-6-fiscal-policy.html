What Is Fiscal Policy? — Revenue and Expenditure

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the level of aggregate demand (AD) in the economy. Unlike monetary policy (Section 3.5), which is conducted by an independent central bank, fiscal policy is conducted directly by the government — the Treasury, Ministry of Finance, or equivalent — and is announced through an annual budget.

Every government budget has two sides: money coming in (revenue) and money going out (expenditure). The difference between the two determines whether the government runs a budget deficit (spending exceeds revenue) or a budget surplus (revenue exceeds spending).

Sources of Government Revenue

Direct Taxation
Taxes levied directly on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm being taxed — e.g. income tax, corporate tax, capital gains tax. Usually progressive (the rate rises with income).
Indirect Taxation
Taxes levied on spending, collected by an intermediary (typically a retailer) and passed on to the government — e.g. value-added tax (VAT), sales tax, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco. Usually regressive as a share of income.
Sale of Goods and Services from State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs)
Revenue the government earns from businesses it owns or part-owns — a national oil, telecoms, rail or postal company, for example — either from their trading profits or from selling shares in them.
Sale of Government Assets
One-off revenue from selling state-owned assets such as land, buildings, spectrum licences, or shares in a previously state-owned company (privatisation).
★ REAL WORLD: How Revenue Mix Differs Between Countries
Most high-income economies rely overwhelmingly on direct and indirect taxation for government revenue. Several oil- and gas-rich economies rely far more heavily on state-owned enterprises instead: Saudi Arabia's national oil company, Saudi Aramco, and Gulf-region equivalents fund a large share of government spending directly from oil export revenue, allowing several Gulf states to charge little or no personal income tax at all. Norway takes a related but distinct approach — its state-owned oil revenue is channelled into a sovereign wealth fund (the largest in the world) rather than spent immediately, smoothing government revenue across booms and busts in the oil price. This matters for evaluation: an economy whose revenue depends heavily on one export commodity is more exposed to global price swings than one with a diversified tax base.

Types of Government Expenditure

Current Expenditure
Day-to-day spending on the ongoing running of government services — public sector salaries (teachers, doctors, civil servants), medical supplies, and other recurring costs. Consumed within the year it is spent.
Capital Expenditure
Spending on long-term physical assets that continue to provide a return for years — roads, railways, hospitals, schools, and other infrastructure. Directly adds to the economy's capital stock and productive capacity.
Transfer Payments
Payments made without any good or service being produced in return — unemployment benefits, pensions, disability benefits, and other welfare payments. Because nothing is produced in exchange, transfer payments are not counted as part of government spending (G) in the expenditure-method calculation of GDP.
Exam Tip: a common error is treating all government spending as equivalent. Distinguish clearly between capital expenditure (which builds long-run productive capacity — link this to supply-side policy in 3.7) and transfer payments (which redirect existing income for equity reasons but are not part of G in the GDP identity).

The Goals of Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy shares some goals with monetary policy, but — because it operates through the government's own spending and tax decisions rather than only through interest rates — it is also used to pursue two goals monetary policy cannot reach as directly: equity and the composition of demand and supply in specific sectors.

Low and Stable Inflation
Fiscal policy can be tightened (spending cuts, tax rises) to cool demand-pull inflationary pressure, or loosened to support demand when inflation is undesirably low.
Low Unemployment
Expansionary fiscal policy raises AD, encouraging firms to hire; capital spending on infrastructure can also directly create jobs.
A Stable Environment for Long-Term Growth
Avoiding extreme swings in government spending and taxation helps firms and households plan investment and consumption with confidence.
Reducing Business Cycle Fluctuations
Using expansionary policy in downturns and contractionary policy in booms to smooth the amplitude of the business cycle — reducing both the depth of recessions and the excesses of booms.
Equitable Distribution of Income
Progressive taxation and transfer payments (welfare, pensions) redistribute income from higher to lower earners — a goal monetary policy has almost no direct tool to pursue.
External Balance
Fiscal policy affects the trade balance indirectly: expansionary policy raises incomes and therefore import spending (worsening the trade balance, other things equal); contractionary policy has the reverse effect.
★ REAL WORLD: Fiscal Policy and Equity — The Nordic Model
The Nordic countries (Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Finland) combine high direct tax rates — often 50%+ at the top marginal rate — with extensive transfer payments and public services, producing some of the lowest income inequality (Gini coefficient) of any economies in the world. This illustrates the equity goal in its purest form: fiscal policy, not monetary policy, is the tool actually used to compress the income distribution. The trade-off is a live debate in the literature: critics argue very high marginal tax rates blunt incentives to work and invest, while defenders point to the Nordic countries' consistently high rankings on both growth and living-standard measures as evidence the trade-off is smaller than often assumed.
Cross-Reference: the goals listed above are the same macroeconomic objectives introduced in 3.3 (low unemployment, low and stable inflation, economic growth, external balance) — fiscal policy is simply one of the tools (alongside monetary policy, 3.5) governments use to pursue them. The equity goal specifically connects to 3.4: progressive direct taxation and transfer payments are the main fiscal mechanisms for reducing income inequality (compressing the Lorenz curve and lowering the Gini coefficient) discussed in that sub-topic.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy — Closing a Recessionary Gap

Expansionary (loose) fiscal policy involves increasing government spending (↑G) and/or cutting taxation (↓T) to raise aggregate demand. It is used to close a deflationary/recessionary gap — a negative output gap where real GDP sits below the full-employment (potential) level.

1
Government increases spending or cuts taxes

Higher capital spending (infrastructure), higher current spending (public sector jobs and wages), or lower direct/indirect tax rates all put more spending power directly into the economy.

2
Households and firms have more disposable income, or receive direct injections

Tax cuts raise take-home pay and after-tax profits; new government contracts and jobs raise household incomes directly.

3
Consumption and/or investment rises

Higher disposable income raises consumption (C); some capital spending is itself classified as investment-like injection.

4
Aggregate demand shifts right

The initial injection is also amplified by the multiplier — covered in the HL extension — as newly-earned income is re-spent through the economy.

5
Real GDP rises towards the full-employment level

The recessionary gap narrows or closes, and unemployment falls as firms respond to stronger demand by hiring.

Reverse chain (contractionary in a boom): government cuts spending or raises taxes → disposable income and/or direct injections fall → consumption and/or investment fall → AD shifts left → real GDP falls back towards potential, easing an inflationary gap.

Two Schools of Thought: Keynesian vs Monetarist/New Classical

The IB syllabus specifically expects you to be able to show expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy using AD/AS diagrams from both the Keynesian and the monetarist/new classical perspective — because economists disagree sharply about how effective (and how necessary) fiscal intervention actually is.

Fig: Keynesian model. With spare capacity (the flat section of the Keynesian AS curve), expansionary fiscal policy raises real GDP (Y₁ → Y₂) with only a modest rise in the price level (P₁ → P₂).
Fig: Monetarist/new classical model. Read right-to-left: the economy begins at AD₂ with a recessionary gap (Y₂ below Y*). Expansionary fiscal policy shifts AD back right (AD₂ → AD₁), closing the gap and restoring output at potential (Y*, P₁).
Why This Distinction Matters: the two models disagree about more than just the shape of a curve. Keynesians argue that economies can get stuck below full employment for a long time (sticky wages and prices prevent automatic self-correction), so active fiscal intervention is both effective and necessary. Monetarists and new classical economists argue that markets self-correct relatively quickly through wage and price flexibility, that fiscal stimulus is often offset by crowding out or by rational households anticipating future tax rises (Ricardian equivalence — covered in the HL extension), and that the economy naturally returns to potential output (Y*) without government intervention. This is one of the most examinable debates in macroeconomics — a top-mark answer should show awareness that "does fiscal policy work?" has no single agreed answer among economists.
★ REAL WORLD: China's 2008 Fiscal Stimulus
Following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, China launched a 4 trillion yuan (roughly $586 billion) fiscal stimulus package — overwhelmingly capital expenditure on infrastructure (railways, roads, airports, public housing) rather than tax cuts or transfer payments. Because the spending was heavily weighted towards capital projects with a high domestic multiplier, China's GDP growth barely slowed during the crisis while many Western economies fell into deep recession. Critics point out the stimulus also left many local governments and state-owned enterprises heavily indebted, and directed investment into some projects with low long-run returns — illustrating that the composition of expansionary fiscal policy (capital spending vs. tax cuts vs. transfers) matters as much as its size.

Case Study: The 2008–09 Global Financial Crisis Response

The Global Financial Crisis is the single most-cited real-world case of expansionary fiscal policy in the IB syllabus, precisely because so many governments responded at once, in such different ways. Tracing the crisis → response → outcome sequence for the United States shows the chain of reasoning in Step 1–5 above playing out in real data.

1
Crisis (Sept 2008)

The collapse of Lehman Brothers on 15 September 2008 triggered a global financial panic. US real GDP fell for four consecutive quarters, and unemployment rose from 5% in early 2008 to a peak of 10% in October 2009 — the deepest recessionary gap in decades.

2
Response — the United States (Feb 2009)

President Barack Obama signed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA), an approximately $787 billion package (later re-estimated at around $831 billion) split roughly into thirds between tax cuts, direct aid to state governments, and capital spending on infrastructure, energy and education.

3
Response — the United Kingdom (Nov 2008)

Chancellor Alistair Darling's Pre-Budget Report cut the standard rate of VAT from 17.5% to 15% for 13 months and brought forward capital spending — a much smaller stimulus relative to GDP (roughly 1.5%) than the US package.

4
Outcome

The US Council of Economic Advisers estimated ARRA raised employment by 1.5–3.5 million jobs at its peak effect; unemployment fell steadily after October 2009. But the recovery was slow, and critics (including some Keynesians, such as Paul Krugman) argued the package was too small relative to the size of the output gap, while others argued it was poorly targeted or partly crowded out by falling private investment — a debate that continues in the economics literature.

A strong evaluative answer does not just describe one country's response; it compares the size of the stimulus relative to GDP, its composition (tax cuts vs. capital spending vs. transfers), and whether the underlying political and institutional constraints (see Section 5) allowed the "correct" size of response to actually be delivered.

Contractionary Fiscal Policy — Closing an Inflationary Gap

Contractionary (tight) fiscal policy — often called austerity when sustained — involves cutting government spending (↓G) and/or raising taxation (↑T) to reduce aggregate demand. It is used to close an inflationary gap, where real GDP sits above the full-employment (potential) level, or to bring down an unsustainable budget deficit.

1
Government cuts spending or raises taxes

Lower capital or current spending, or higher direct/indirect tax rates, remove spending power from the economy.

2
Households and firms have less disposable income

Higher taxes reduce take-home pay and after-tax profits; reduced government contracts and public sector jobs lower household incomes directly.

3
Consumption and/or investment falls

Lower disposable income reduces consumption; reduced government demand for goods and services can also reduce related private investment.

4
Aggregate demand shifts left

The multiplier also amplifies the contraction — a given spending cut reduces GDP by more than the cut itself (covered in the HL extension).

5
Demand-pull inflationary pressure eases

Real GDP falls back towards the full-employment level, closing the inflationary gap — but at the cost of slower growth and, potentially, rising unemployment.

Reverse chain (expansionary): government raises spending or cuts taxes → disposable income and/or direct injections rise → consumption and/or investment rise → AD shifts right → real GDP rises, easing a recessionary gap but risking demand-pull inflation if pushed beyond potential output.
Fig: Keynesian model. Contractionary fiscal policy shifts AD left (AD₁ → AD₂). If this happens within the flat, spare-capacity section of the Keynesian AS curve, real GDP falls with almost no effect on the price level — a caution that cutting spending too far below potential output mainly destroys jobs and output rather than fighting inflation.
Fig: Monetarist/new classical model. The economy starts at AD₁ with an inflationary gap — real GDP (Y1) above potential output (Y*). Contractionary fiscal policy shifts AD left back towards Y*, easing inflationary pressure and closing the gap.
★ REAL WORLD: Fiscal Consolidation — Greece and Argentina
Greece's 2010–2015 debt crisis is the most widely studied recent example of severe fiscal contraction: as part of EU/IMF bailout conditions, Greece cut public spending and raised taxes repeatedly, and its economy contracted by roughly a quarter over the period, with unemployment peaking above 27%. This is frequently cited as evidence that the multiplier effect of austerity was much larger than policymakers originally assumed. Argentina offers a recurring, non-European example: it has entered IMF-backed austerity programmes multiple times since the 1990s (most recently from 2018 and again 2023–24), typically involving sharp cuts to energy and transport subsidies and public sector employment to control persistent budget deficits and inflation — illustrating that the trade-off between fiscal discipline and short-run economic pain recurs across very different economies and political systems.
5

Evaluating Fiscal Policy

✓ Strengths of Fiscal Policy

Can target specific sectors

Government spending can be directed at particular industries, regions or groups (e.g. green energy subsidies, regional infrastructure, a specific welfare programme) far more precisely than a single economy-wide interest rate.

Effective in a deep recession

When the policy rate is already near the zero lower bound and monetary policy has little room left to work, government spending can still directly inject demand into the economy — many economists see this as fiscal policy's strongest comparative advantage over monetary policy.

✗ Constraints on Fiscal Policy

Political pressure

Tax rises and spending cuts are unpopular with voters, and politicians may delay necessary contractionary policy, or use expansionary policy for electoral rather than economic reasons (a "political budget cycle").

Time lags

Recognising a problem, deciding on a policy response, legislating it, and having it take effect can together take well over a year — by which point the economic conditions the policy was designed for may have changed.

Sustainable debt

Persistent deficits accumulate as government debt. If debt grows faster than the economy for long enough, investors may demand higher interest rates to hold government bonds, or (in extreme cases) refuse to lend at all, forcing a sudden and painful fiscal contraction.

★ REAL WORLD: Not All High Debt Is Equally "Unsustainable" — Japan vs Sri Lanka
Japan has the highest government debt-to-GDP ratio of any major economy — above 260%, roughly double the level often cited as a danger threshold — yet it has never faced a debt crisis, borrows extremely cheaply, and its currency has not collapsed. This is because most Japanese government debt is held domestically (much of it by the Bank of Japan itself and Japanese pension funds and insurers) and denominated in its own currency, which Japan's own central bank can always create more of if needed. Sri Lanka's 2022 sovereign default illustrates the opposite case: a much lower debt-to-GDP ratio, but debt owed substantially in foreign currency to foreign creditors, meant that once foreign exchange reserves ran low, the government could not simply create more of the currency its debts were owed in, and it was forced to default and seek an emergency IMF programme. The lesson for evaluation: "is a debt level sustainable?" depends heavily on who owns the debt and in what currency it is denominated, not on the debt-to-GDP ratio alone.
Note: two further important constraints and features of fiscal policy — crowding out and automatic stabilisers — are HL-only content and are covered in full in the HL extension to this chapter, alongside the Keynesian multiplier calculation required for Paper 3.

True or False

Choose True or False for each statement — the explanation only appears once you've answered.

1

Fiscal policy is controlled by a country's central bank.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Fiscal policy is controlled by the government (Treasury/Ministry of Finance); the central bank controls monetary policy.
2

Direct taxes are levied on income or wealth, while indirect taxes are levied on spending.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Direct taxes (income tax, corporate tax) fall on income/wealth; indirect taxes (VAT, excise duty) fall on spending.
3

Capital expenditure and transfer payments are the same thing.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Capital expenditure builds long-term assets (roads, hospitals); transfer payments redistribute income (pensions, benefits) with no good or service produced in exchange.
4

An inflationary gap means real GDP is above the full-employment (potential) level.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. An inflationary gap is a positive output gap — real GDP sits above potential.
5

To close a recessionary gap, the government should cut spending and raise taxes.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. A recessionary gap calls for expansionary policy — raising spending and/or cutting taxes, not the reverse.
6

Keynesians and monetarist/new classical economists disagree about how quickly economies self-correct to full employment.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Keynesians see sticky wages/prices preventing quick self-correction, justifying intervention; monetarists/new classical economists see markets self-correcting relatively quickly.
7

A budget deficit occurs when government spending exceeds government revenue in a given year.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. That's the definition of a budget deficit; a surplus is the reverse (revenue exceeds spending).
8

A high government debt-to-GDP ratio is always a sign of an unsustainable, high-risk fiscal position.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Sustainability depends heavily on who owns the debt and in what currency (e.g. Japan's very high, largely domestically-held debt vs. countries with lower debt owed mainly in foreign currency).
9

Fiscal policy can target specific sectors or groups more precisely than monetary policy.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Government spending can be directed at particular sectors, regions or groups, unlike a single economy-wide policy interest rate.
10

Fiscal policy decisions take effect immediately, with no significant time lags.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Recognising a problem, legislating a response, and having it take effect can together take well over a year — a significant time lag.

The Golden Chain: Expansionary Fiscal Policy to Close a Recessionary Gap

Economy has a negative output gap (recessionary/deflationary gap) → government increases spending and/or cuts taxes → households and firms have more disposable income and/or receive direct injections → consumption and/or investment rise → aggregate demand shifts right (AD₂ → AD₁, amplified by the multiplier) → firms respond to higher demand by increasing output and employment → real GDP rises towards the full-employment level (Y*) → the recessionary gap narrows.

The Golden Chain: Contractionary Fiscal Policy to Close an Inflationary Gap

Economy has a positive output gap (inflationary gap) → government cuts spending and/or raises taxes → households and firms have less disposable income and/or receive fewer direct injections → consumption and/or investment fall → aggregate demand shifts left (amplified by the multiplier) → demand-pull inflationary pressure eases → real GDP falls back towards the full-employment level → the inflationary gap narrows.

Key Exam Tips

1

Always identify the gap first: state clearly whether the economy has a recessionary/deflationary gap or an inflationary gap before choosing between expansionary and contractionary policy.

2

Show awareness of both models: if a question asks you to evaluate fiscal policy, mention that Keynesians and monetarist/new classical economists disagree about how effective it is — this is a high-value evaluation point.

3

Draw the correct AD/AS diagram for the model you are using: the kinked Keynesian AS curve (label the flat "spare capacity" section) for a Keynesian answer, or the vertical LRAS with an upward-sloping SRAS for a monetarist/classical answer — labelling AD1, AD2, Y* (or Yfe), and the output gap clearly in both cases.

4

Distinguish revenue sources and expenditure types precisely: a top answer separates direct from indirect taxation, and capital expenditure from current expenditure and transfer payments — don't just say "government spending".

5

Evaluate, don't just describe: weigh strengths (targeting, effectiveness in deep recession) against constraints (political pressure, time lags, sustainable debt) for higher-mark questions.

6

Use real-world context: reference actual episodes (China's 2008 stimulus, Greece's austerity, Japan's high but stable debt) to earn application marks — examiners reward country-specific evidence over generic statements.
