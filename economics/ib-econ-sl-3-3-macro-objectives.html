The Complete Guide

STANDARD LEVEL

IB Real-World Issue: Why do governments pursue economic growth, low unemployment and low, stable inflation — and why can these goals conflict with one another?
Conceptual understanding: governments intervene in the economy to achieve macroeconomic objectives, but political, economic, social and environmental factors are interdependent, and pursuing one objective can undermine another.
Key concepts: scarcity, choice, efficiency, equity, economic well-being, sustainability, change, intervention.

Economic Growth

Economic growth — an increase in a country's real output over time — is usually the first macroeconomic objective governments pursue, since it is closely linked to rising living standards. The IB distinguishes carefully between short-term and long-term growth.

Short-Term (Actual) Growth
A rise in real output achieved by using existing spare capacity more fully — a movement from inside the production possibility frontier (PPF) towards it, or a rightward shift of AD along a given SRAS/LRAS.
Long-Term (Potential) Growth
A rise in an economy's productive capacity itself — an outward shift of the PPF, corresponding to a rightward shift of LRAS (or the Keynesian AS curve at full capacity).
Fig: short-term (actual) growth — a movement from a point inside the PPF (A, using spare capacity) towards the frontier (B) — no change in the economy's underlying productive capacity.
Fig: long-term (potential) growth — the entire PPF shifts outward (PPF₁ → PPF₂), reflecting a genuine increase in productive capacity, illustrated in the AD/AS model as a rightward LRAS shift.

The PPF diagrams above show growth as a production-possibilities story; the same two ideas can equally be shown on the AD/AS model used throughout this unit.

Fig: an increase in AD (AD₁ → AD₂) raises real output from Y₁ to Y₂ along a given SRAS — the AD/AS picture behind short-term (actual) growth, provided the economy still has spare capacity below full-employment output (LRAS).
Fig: an increase in LRAS (LRAS₁ → LRAS₂), typically accompanied by a rightward shift of SRAS (SRAS₁ → SRAS₂) as productive capacity and productivity rise, raises full-employment output and shifts the equilibrium from Y₁ to Y₂ — the AD/AS picture behind long-term (potential) growth, alongside a falling price level.
Measurement of Economic Growth
The percentage change in real GDP (or real GNI) from one period to the next — see the worked calculation in Section 7.

Consequences of economic growth are not uniformly positive — a strong evaluation answer weighs several dimensions:

ConsequenceExplanation
Impact on living standardsRising real output generally raises average incomes and access to goods and services, but the benefit depends heavily on how growth is distributed.
Impact on the environmentGrowth often increases resource extraction, energy use and pollution, particularly where growth is driven by heavy industry or resource extraction rather than services or high-value manufacturing.
Impact on income distributionGrowth can be broad-based or narrowly concentrated — the same growth rate can leave a country more equal or dramatically less equal, depending on which sectors and asset owners capture the gains.
★ REAL WORLD: Ethiopia's Infrastructure-Led Growth and Its Debt Trade-Off
Ethiopia sustained one of the world's fastest economic growth rates for over a decade, driven substantially by large state-led investment in infrastructure (roads, the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, industrial parks) and expanding manufacturing for export. Living standards rose significantly by many measures — poverty rates fell and life expectancy increased — but the growth model relied heavily on external borrowing, leaving Ethiopia among several African economies that later faced debt sustainability concerns (a theme explored further in the HL extension file). This illustrates that the living-standards benefit of growth can be real, while still coming with longer-run costs that only become visible once the growth-financing model is tested by a shock (falling export prices, rising global interest rates, drought).
★ REAL WORLD: Growth vs. the Environment — Deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon
Brazil's agricultural export growth — particularly soy and cattle farming — has been a major driver of GDP growth in the country's interior, but has also been the single largest driver of Amazon rainforest deforestation, with knock-on consequences for global carbon emissions and biodiversity loss that extend far beyond Brazil's own borders. This is a clear illustration of why "economic growth" cannot be assessed by the GDP growth rate alone — the same growth figure can represent very different environmental outcomes depending on which sectors are expanding, a point examiners frequently reward when candidates move beyond simply stating that growth "harms the environment" to explaining the specific mechanism (land-use change, resource depletion) involved.

Low Unemployment

Low unemployment is a core macroeconomic objective because unemployment represents wasted productive potential and imposes direct costs on the individuals affected and on society as a whole.

Unemployment Rate
The number of people unemployed as a percentage of the labour force (those employed plus those unemployed and actively seeking work) — see the worked calculation in Section 7.

Difficulties of Measuring Unemployment

Discouraged Workers
People who want a job but have stopped actively searching, and so are not counted as "unemployed" in most official definitions — meaning the official rate can understate the true number of people without work who would take a job if offered one.
Underemployment
People working fewer hours than they would like, or in jobs below their skill level — counted as "employed" even though they are not fully utilised, again understating the true scale of labour market slack.
The Informal Economy
In economies with a large informal sector (see 3.1), official employment and unemployment statistics may not capture a large share of actual economic activity, making the headline unemployment rate an unreliable guide to true labour market conditions.
★ REAL WORLD: Japan's Low Headline Unemployment and Hidden Labour Market Slack
Japan has long reported one of the lowest official unemployment rates among major economies, often below 3%. Economists have pointed out that this headline figure can understate genuine labour market slack: a substantial number of Japanese workers — historically, disproportionately women — have withdrawn from the labour force entirely (rather than being counted as unemployed) due to childcare responsibilities, limited part-time opportunities, or discouragement, and are therefore invisible in the standard unemployment rate. This is a clear real-world illustration of why a low official unemployment rate does not always mean an economy has little spare capacity in its labour market.

Causes of Unemployment

Cyclical (Demand-Deficient) Unemployment
Caused by a fall in AD during a downturn — the labour market equivalent of a negative output gap. Rises in recessions, falls in booms.
Structural Unemployment
Caused by a persistent mismatch between the skills workers have and the skills employers need, or by industries and regions in long-term decline — the demand for labour falls in a particular market or geographical area, even while the wider economy grows.
Seasonal Unemployment
Caused by predictable, recurring fluctuations in labour demand across the year, common in agriculture, tourism and construction.
Frictional Unemployment
Short-term unemployment as workers move between jobs — searching for a better match of skills, pay and location — an unavoidable feature of a dynamic labour market.
Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU)
The sum of structural, seasonal and frictional unemployment — the rate that persists even when the economy is at full employment, since it excludes only cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
Fig: a fall in the demand for labour in a particular market or geographical area (DL₁ → DL₂) — for example, a declining industry or region — leaving unemployed workers whose skills or location no longer match available jobs: structural unemployment.

Minimum Wage and Unemployment

Minimum Wage
A legally-imposed wage floor — a price floor in the labour market. If set above the market-clearing equilibrium wage, it raises pay for those who keep their jobs, but can also cause unemployment by creating an excess supply of labour at that wage.
Fig: a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage (Qe) raises the quantity of labour supplied (Qs) while lowering the quantity demanded (Qd) — the gap between them is real-wage (classical) unemployment.

Because a minimum wage is a legally-imposed price floor, it behaves exactly like the price floors studied in microeconomics: at the higher wage, more people want to work (Qs rises) while firms are willing to hire fewer of them at the higher cost (Qd falls), so real-wage unemployment persists for as long as the floor stays above the market-clearing wage.

★ REAL WORLD: Indonesia's Regional Minimum Wage System
Indonesia sets minimum wages regionally (by province and even by district) rather than nationally, reflecting large differences in living costs and productivity across the archipelago. Sharp annual increases in some provinces — intended to raise living standards for low-paid workers — have been linked by some studies to slower formal-sector hiring and a shift of some employment into the informal economy, where the wage floor cannot be enforced. This illustrates the real-wage unemployment risk from setting a minimum wage well above a region's equilibrium wage, and why many economists advocate setting it modestly and adjusting it gradually.

Costs of unemployment fall into three categories:

Personal Costs
Lost income, reduced self-esteem, loss of skills over time, and negative effects on physical and mental health associated with prolonged joblessness.
Social Costs
Higher rates of family breakdown, crime, and social exclusion associated with concentrated, persistent unemployment in particular communities.
Economic Costs
Lost output (the economy produces below its potential — a negative output gap), lower tax revenue, higher government spending on unemployment benefits, and a risk that skills atrophy the longer someone remains out of work (hysteresis), permanently raising the NRU.
Fig: a negative (deflationary) output gap, caused by a fall in AD, is the economy-wide picture behind cyclical unemployment — actual output falls below potential, and unemployment rises above the natural rate.
★ REAL WORLD: Denmark's "Flexicurity" Model — Managing Frictional Unemployment
Denmark is well known for its "flexicurity" labour market model, which combines relatively easy hiring and firing (flexibility) with generous unemployment benefits and heavy investment in retraining and job-matching services (security). The explicit aim is to keep frictional unemployment — workers between jobs — as short and low-cost as possible, on the theory that some frictional unemployment is an unavoidable and even healthy feature of a dynamic, adaptable labour market, so policy should focus on speeding up the matching process rather than preventing job losses in the first place.
★ REAL WORLD: South Africa's Persistent Structural Unemployment
South Africa has recorded one of the highest structural unemployment rates in the world for an extended period, frequently above 30% (and far higher among young people). Much of this is attributed to a severe and persistent mismatch between the skills of the workforce — a legacy in part of unequal access to education under apartheid — and the skills demanded by a modern, increasingly capital-intensive economy, alongside the geographic legacy of townships built far from centres of employment. This illustrates structural unemployment operating at a scale and persistence rarely seen in higher-income economies, and shows why the appropriate policy response (education, training, transport infrastructure — supply-side policy, covered in 3.7) is very different from the response to cyclical unemployment (demand stimulus).

Low and Stable Inflation

A low and stable rate of inflation is the third core macroeconomic objective, since both very high inflation and deflation impose significant economic costs.

Consumer Price Index (CPI)
A measure of the average price level, calculated by tracking the cost of a fixed "basket" of goods and services typically bought by households, relative to a base year.
Inflation Rate
The percentage change in the CPI (or another price index) from one period to the next — see the worked calculation in Section 7.

Limitations of the CPI

A Single "Average" Household
The basket reflects average spending patterns, but individual households (e.g. renters vs. homeowners, or low-income vs. high-income households) can face very different actual inflation rates depending on their own spending mix.
Quality Changes
If a product's price rises but its quality also improves, some of the price rise reflects genuine value added, not "pure" inflation — a difficult adjustment to make accurately.
Substitution Bias
A fixed basket does not account for consumers substituting away from goods that have become relatively more expensive towards cheaper alternatives, which can overstate the true cost-of-living increase.
Excludes the Informal Economy
In economies with large informal sectors, a substantial share of transactions and price changes may not be reflected in the official CPI basket at all.

Causes of Inflation

Fig: demand-pull inflation — a rightward shift of AD (AD₁ → AD₂) raises the price level (and real output) as the economy's spending outpaces its capacity to supply goods and services.
Fig: cost-push inflation — a leftward shift of SRAS (SRAS₁ → SRAS₂), caused by rising production costs, raises the price level while lowering real output — "stagflation".

Costs of a high inflation rate:

CostExplanation
UncertaintyUnpredictable prices make it harder for firms and households to plan spending, saving and investment decisions.
Redistributive effectsInflation redistributes real income and wealth from savers and lenders (whose money loses value) to borrowers (whose debts shrink in real terms) — and hurts those on fixed incomes most.
Effects on savingRising prices erode the real value of savings, discouraging saving and potentially reducing funds available for investment.
Damage to export competitivenessIf domestic inflation exceeds that of trading partners, domestic goods become relatively more expensive abroad, worsening the trade balance.
Impact on economic growthVery high or unstable inflation can discourage investment and long-run growth by adding risk and uncertainty to business planning.
Inefficient resource allocationPrice signals become distorted and less informative when the general price level is rising rapidly, making it harder for markets to allocate resources efficiently.
★ REAL WORLD: Lebanon's Currency and Inflation Collapse
Lebanon experienced one of the most severe peacetime currency and banking collapses of the 21st century beginning in 2019, with the Lebanese pound losing over 90% of its value against the US dollar and inflation exceeding 100% for several consecutive years. The redistributive effects were dramatic and highly visible: Lebanese citizens who had saved in local-currency bank accounts saw their life savings become nearly worthless in real terms, while the government's debt burden (denominated partly in local currency) was eroded — a stark illustration of how high inflation redistributes wealth from savers to borrowers, including the government itself.

Why "Low and Stable" Became the Goal: The Historical Origin of Inflation Targeting

Before the late 1980s, most governments did not commit publicly to a numerical inflation goal at all — monetary policy chased several objectives at once (growth, employment, the exchange rate) with no clear anchor for what counted as an acceptable inflation rate. The idea that a central bank should be given a single, explicit, published inflation target emerged from a specific set of failures, then spread rapidly around the world.

1
1970s–80s: stagflation discredits "fine-tuning"

Repeated attempts to use demand-side policy to buy lower unemployment produced high, unstable inflation without a lasting employment gain (a pattern examined via the Phillips curve in the HL extension), discrediting governments' informal, unstated inflation goals.

2
1989–90: New Zealand legislates a numerical target

The Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 made price stability the central bank's primary legal objective and required an explicit, published target range — the world's first formal inflation-targeting regime, agreed between the Reserve Bank Governor and the Minister of Finance.

3
Early-to-mid 1990s: rapid adoption elsewhere

Canada (1991), the United Kingdom (1992, after sterling's exit from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism) and several other economies adopted their own inflation targets in quick succession, each citing the credibility gained from a transparent, numerical commitment.

4
Anchoring expectations becomes the mechanism

The theoretical case for a published target is that it anchors households' and firms' inflation expectations directly, so wage- and price-setting behaviour itself becomes less inflationary — a credible target can do some of monetary policy's work before a single interest-rate decision is made.

5
Becomes the global norm

By the 2020s, dozens of central banks — from the European Central Bank's "close to, but below, 2%" to the Bank of England's 2% target — had adopted broadly similar frameworks, making "low and stable inflation" a specific, numerical, publicly-accountable objective rather than a vague aspiration.

★ REAL WORLD: New Zealand's Reserve Bank Act 1989
New Zealand's decision to legislate a numerical inflation target came after a period of double-digit inflation in the 1970s and 1980s that had badly eroded confidence in the currency and in monetary policy generally. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989 was unusually explicit for its time: it specified that the Bank's primary function was price stability, and made the Governor personally accountable if an agreed target range (originally 0–2%, later widened to 1–3%) was missed. Other central banks — including the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England — studied New Zealand's framework closely before adopting their own versions within a few years, illustrating how a single country's policy innovation can reshape a macroeconomic objective worldwide once it is seen to work.

Deflation and Disinflation

Disinflation
A fall in the rate of inflation — prices are still rising, just more slowly than before. Not the same as deflation.
Deflation
A fall in the general price level — prices are actually lower than the previous period, corresponding to a negative inflation rate.

Deflation can arise from either side of the AD/AS model, with very different implications:

Fig: benign deflation — falling prices caused by rightward shifts of SRAS and LRAS (rising productivity, falling input costs). Output rises alongside falling prices — the opposite of a demand collapse.
Malevolent vs. Benign Deflation: malevolent (bad) deflation is caused by a collapsing AD (see the malevolent deflation diagram in 3.5) — prices and output both fall, and risks a self-reinforcing deflationary spiral. Benign (good) deflation, shown above, is caused by improvements on the supply side — falling prices accompany rising output and living standards. Distinguishing between the two is one of the most important applied evaluation skills in this topic: the correct policy response to each is completely different.

Costs of deflation — even benign deflation carries risks if it becomes severe or prolonged:

Uncertainty and Redistributive Effects
Deflation redistributes real wealth from borrowers to savers/lenders (the opposite of inflation) — real debt burdens rise as the value of money increases.
Deferred Consumption
If consumers expect prices to keep falling, they may delay purchases, further reducing AD — a self-reinforcing spiral if the deflation is malevolent.
Cyclical Unemployment and Bankruptcies
Malevolent deflation, driven by falling AD, is strongly associated with rising cyclical unemployment and business failures.
Increase in the Real Value of Debt
Fixed nominal debts become harder to repay in real terms as the price level falls — "debt deflation", which can trigger defaults and a credit crunch (see 3.5).
Policy Ineffectiveness
Central banks may be unable to cut nominal interest rates enough to stimulate AD once rates are already near zero (the zero lower bound / liquidity trap — see 3.5), limiting the tools available to fight a deflationary spiral.
★ REAL WORLD: Japan's "Lost Decades" — Deflation and the Zero Lower Bound
Following the collapse of a major asset-price bubble in 1991, Japan experienced repeated bouts of mild but persistent deflation from the mid-1990s through the 2010s, with consumer prices falling in many individual years. The Bank of Japan cut its policy interest rate to effectively zero as early as 1999 — the first major central bank to hit the zero lower bound in the modern era — and later pioneered "quantitative easing" (large-scale asset purchases) precisely because conventional interest-rate cuts had run out of room. Deferred consumption compounded the problem: consumers and firms who expected prices to keep falling had a rational incentive to delay spending and investment, further weakening AD in a self-reinforcing pattern economists call a deflationary spiral. Japan's experience became the reference case examiners expect candidates to cite whenever a question asks why deflation, not just inflation, can be a serious policy problem.

Relative Costs of Unemployment vs. Inflation

Governments rarely have the luxury of a single, dominant macroeconomic problem — a strong evaluation answer weighs which cost (unemployment or inflation) is more damaging in a given context.

When Unemployment Is the Bigger Concern

Persistent, concentrated joblessness

Long-term unemployment causes lasting damage to individuals (skills loss, hysteresis) and communities that low, stable inflation does not — and inflation at a moderate rate is far less economically damaging than deep, sustained unemployment.

Below-target inflation with spare capacity

When inflation is already low and there is significant spare capacity (a negative output gap), the case for prioritising demand stimulus over inflation-fighting is strong.

When Inflation Is the Bigger Concern

High or accelerating inflation

Very high inflation imposes costs on the entire population simultaneously (eroding all real incomes and savings) and can spiral if inflation expectations become unanchored, whereas unemployment costs are concentrated on a smaller group.

Inflation near or above full employment

Once the economy is close to full employment, further demand stimulus mainly raises prices rather than output or jobs — the case for prioritising inflation control over unemployment strengthens.

Exam Tip: there is no universally "correct" answer to which cost is worse — examiners reward candidates who make this judgement contingent on context: the current position relative to full employment, the rate and trend of inflation, and the distributional pattern of who bears each cost.

Potential Conflicts Between Objectives

Macroeconomic objectives frequently conflict — achieving one can make another harder to achieve, which is precisely why macroeconomic policy involves difficult trade-offs rather than simple "more is always better" choices.

Low Unemployment vs. Low Inflation
Policies that expand AD to reduce unemployment (fiscal or monetary stimulus) tend to raise the price level, since they push the economy along the upward-sloping SRAS curve — a basic version of the trade-off explored in depth via the Phillips curve at HL.
High Growth vs. Low Inflation
AD-driven growth that pushes the economy towards or beyond full employment risks demand-pull inflation, as seen in Turkey's overheating economy (3.2) — growth achieved through supply-side improvement avoids this trade-off, but is typically slower to materialise.
High Growth vs. Environmental Sustainability
Growth driven by resource-intensive or high-emission industries can conflict directly with environmental sustainability, as with deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon (Section 1) — though growth in green technology and efficiency can, in principle, support both goals simultaneously.
High Growth vs. Equity in Income Distribution
Growth does not automatically benefit all groups equally — it can be captured disproportionately by owners of capital or by a small skilled workforce, widening inequality even as average incomes rise (explored in full in 3.4).
★ REAL WORLD: South Africa's Growth-Equity Conflict
South Africa has recorded positive GDP growth over extended periods since the end of apartheid, yet remains one of the most unequal societies in the world by measures such as the Gini coefficient, with growth benefits concentrated substantially among already-wealthier groups. This is one of the clearest illustrations that the headline growth rate alone says nothing about who actually benefits from it — a distinction the IB syllabus treats as central enough to warrant its own dedicated subtopic (3.4, Economics of Inequality and Poverty).

The Golden Chain: From a Shock to an Evaluated Policy Judgement

Identify which macroeconomic objective(s) the shock affects (growth, unemployment, inflation) → determine which curve moves and in which direction (AD, SRAS/LRAS, or labour supply/demand in a specific market) → draw the correct diagram and identify the new equilibrium (output, price level, or wage/employment level) → state explicitly which objective(s) improve and which worsen as a result → evaluate, using a named real-world example, which cost matters most in this specific context and why a different context could flip the judgement.
Unemployment Rate (%) = (Number Unemployed ÷ Labour Force) × 100
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Unemployment Rate

Data: Number unemployed = 2.4 million. Labour force = 32 million.

Step 1: Unemployment rate = (2.4 ÷ 32) × 100 = 7.5%.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the Number Unemployed

Question: A country's unemployment rate is 6%. Its labour force is 40 million. Find the number of people unemployed.

Step 1: Rearrange: Number unemployed = Unemployment rate × Labour force.

Step 2: Number unemployed = 0.06 × 40 million = 2.4 million.

Growth Rate (%) = ((Real GDP this year − Real GDP last year) ÷ Real GDP last year) × 100
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Rate of Economic Growth

Data: Real GDP last year = $900bn. Real GDP this year = $945bn.

Step 1: Growth rate = ((945 − 900) ÷ 900) × 100 = 5%.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding This Year's GDP from a Growth Rate

Question: Real GDP last year was $800bn. The growth rate was 4%. Find real GDP this year.

Step 1: Rearrange: Real GDP this year = Real GDP last year × (1 + growth rate as a decimal).

Step 2: Real GDP this year = 800 × 1.04 = $832bn.

Inflation Rate (%) = ((CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year) × 100
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Inflation Rate from CPI Data

Data: CPI last year = 105. CPI this year = 110.25.

Step 1: Inflation rate = ((110.25 − 105) ÷ 105) × 100 = 5%.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding This Year's CPI from an Inflation Rate

Question: CPI last year was 120. The inflation rate was 3%. Find this year's CPI.

Step 1: Rearrange: CPI this year = CPI last year × (1 + inflation rate as a decimal).

Step 2: CPI this year = 120 × 1.03 = 123.6.

Key Exam Tips

1

Always show the full formula before substituting numbers, and label every figure with its unit (%, $bn, index points) — Paper 3 rewards clear method even when a final answer is wrong.

2

The same "percentage change" formula structure underlies the growth rate, inflation rate and (with rearrangement) the unemployment rate — learn the structure once and apply it flexibly, in either direction.

3

Never conflate the unemployment rate with the number of people unemployed, or the inflation rate with the price level itself — these are related but distinct figures, and mixing them up is a common source of lost marks.

4

When asked to evaluate a policy against "macroeconomic objectives", explicitly name which objective(s) are helped and which are put at risk — a strong answer always identifies the specific trade-off, not just "there are pros and cons".

5

Use real-world context to earn application marks: reference Ethiopia's growth-debt trade-off, South Africa's structural unemployment and growth-equity conflict, Lebanon's inflation collapse, or Denmark's flexicurity model.
