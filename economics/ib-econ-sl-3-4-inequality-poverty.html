Equality, Equity, and the Meaning of Economic Inequality

Equality
Everyone receiving or having access to the same outcomes (e.g. the same income, or the same slice of a resource) — a state of sameness.
Equity
Fairness — resources or opportunities distributed according to need or contribution, which may mean treating people differently rather than identically.
Economic inequality
The extent to which income and/or wealth are unevenly distributed among individuals or households in an economy.

Equality and equity are easily confused but pull in different directions. A perfectly equal outcome (everyone gets exactly the same) may not feel fair if people have contributed very differently or have very different needs; a perfectly equitable outcome (resources allocated by need) will usually not be equal. Most real policy debates about inequality are really debates about what counts as equitable, not simply whether outcomes are equal.

Income
A flow — the money households or individuals receive over a period of time (wages, rent, interest, profit, transfer payments).
Wealth
A stock — the total value of assets owned at a point in time (property, savings, shares, pensions) minus liabilities.
Income vs wealth inequality: wealth is almost always distributed more unequally than income, because wealth compounds over time (assets generate more assets through interest, dividends, and capital gains) and can be inherited across generations, while income is earned fresh each period and is more directly tied to current work.
Real-world: in Namibia, one of the most unequal countries in the world by both measures, the top 10% of earners receive over half of national income, while wealth inequality (concentrated largely in land ownership dating from the colonial and apartheid-era land allocation system) is even more skewed than income inequality.

Measuring Economic Inequality: The Lorenz Curve and Gini Coefficient

Lorenz curve
A graph plotting the cumulative percentage of income (or wealth) received against the cumulative percentage of the population, ordered from poorest to richest.
Line of perfect equality
The 45° diagonal line the Lorenz curve would trace if every person received an identical share of income — the further the actual curve bows away from this line, the more unequal the distribution.
Gini coefficient
A single number between 0 (perfect equality) and 1 (maximum inequality, one person has all the income) summarising how far the Lorenz curve bows away from the line of perfect equality.
Two Lorenz curves against the line of perfect equality. Lorenz₂ bows further away from the diagonal than Lorenz₁, so a country with Lorenz₂ has a higher Gini coefficient — more unequal income distribution.

The Gini coefficient is calculated (at HL, via construction from quintile data) as the ratio of the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, to the total area under the line of perfect equality. At SL, you need to interpret a given Gini coefficient or Lorenz curve rather than construct one from raw data.

Real-world: Iceland has one of the lowest Gini coefficients in the world (around 0.26), reflecting a compressed wage structure, a strong welfare state, and heavy redistribution through taxation — contrasted with Namibia's Gini of roughly 0.59, among the highest recorded anywhere.
Limitation: the Gini coefficient is a single summary number, so two very differently-shaped Lorenz curves can produce the same Gini value — it tells you how unequal a distribution is overall, but not where in the income distribution that inequality is concentrated.
Max Lorenz, 1905 — American economist (then a graduate student at the University of Wisconsin) publishes "Methods of Measuring the Concentration of Wealth", introducing the cumulative-share curve→ Corrado Gini, 1912 — Italian statistician, in "Variabilità e Mutabilità", derives a single summary number from the area under Lorenz's curve→ Mid-20th century — the World Bank, UN, and OECD adopt the Gini coefficient as the standard cross-country inequality measure→ Today — national statistics offices worldwide publish an annual Gini figure, enabling the country comparisons below

These two tools were developed independently, seven years apart, and later combined: Gini's coefficient is, in effect, a single number extracted from the shape of Lorenz's curve (formally, the ratio of the area between the curve and the diagonal to the total area under the diagonal). The HL extension file shows how to construct the curve itself directly from quintile data.

The Meaning and Measurement of Poverty

Absolute poverty
Living below a fixed threshold of income or consumption needed to meet basic survival needs (food, shelter, clothing), regardless of what others in society have.
Relative poverty
Having significantly less income or resources than the typical (median) standard of living in one's own society, even if basic survival needs are met.

Measuring poverty — single indicators:

International poverty line
A fixed monetary threshold (e.g. the World Bank's $2.15/day, PPP-adjusted) used to compare absolute poverty across countries.
Minimum income standard
A country-specific threshold, often set relative to median income (e.g. 60% of median household income), reflecting what is needed to participate in that society — a relative poverty measure.

Measuring poverty — composite indicators:

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)
Measures poverty across multiple dimensions simultaneously — health, education, and standard of living indicators (e.g. nutrition, years of schooling, access to clean water, electricity) — rather than income alone.
Difficulties in Measuring PovertyExplanation
Informal economyIncome earned outside formal record-keeping (subsistence farming, informal trade) is easily missed by income-based surveys.
Non-monetary deprivationA household above the income poverty line may still lack clean water, healthcare access, or safe housing — income alone misses this.
Choice of thresholdWhere the poverty line is drawn is a value judgement — a small change in the threshold can dramatically change measured poverty rates.
Data quality and frequencyMany low-income countries lack the administrative capacity to survey households regularly or accurately, especially in remote or conflict-affected regions.
Intra-household distributionHousehold-level data can hide unequal distribution of resources within the household itself (e.g. by gender or age).
Real-world: Rwanda has cut its multidimensional poverty rate substantially since the early 2000s through a national strategy combining universal health insurance (Mutuelle de Santé), rural electrification, and targeted anti-poverty transfer programmes (Vision 2020 Umurenge Programme) — the MPI captures these health/education/living-standard gains in a way a purely income-based poverty line would miss.
Real-world: Bangladesh has similarly reduced its MPI faster than its income-poverty rate alone would suggest, driven by large gains in girls' education, sanitation access, and child mortality reduction — illustrating why composite indicators can tell a fuller story than a single income threshold.

Causes of Economic Inequality and Poverty

CauseExplanation
Inequality of opportunityUnequal access to education, healthcare, and networks from birth restricts some individuals' ability to earn high incomes regardless of effort or ability.
Different levels of resource ownershipUnequal ownership of land, capital, and financial assets means unequal access to rental income, dividends, and capital gains.
Different levels of human capitalDifferences in education, skills, and training directly affect productivity and therefore earning potential in the labour market.
DiscriminationUnequal treatment based on gender, race, ethnicity, disability or other characteristics restricts access to jobs, credit, or fair pay regardless of ability.
Unequal status and powerDifferences in bargaining power (e.g. between employers and workers, or between large and small firms) shape how the gains from production are distributed.
Government tax and benefits policiesThe extent of redistribution through progressive taxation and welfare transfers directly affects post-tax-and-benefit inequality.
Globalisation and technological changeTrade and automation can raise returns to highly skilled workers and capital owners while displacing lower-skilled workers, widening the income gap.
Market-based supply-side policiesDeregulation, privatisation, and reduced union power (SL 3.7) can increase efficiency but may also widen wage and wealth gaps if the gains are not evenly shared.
Real-world: Brazil's historically high inequality (referenced in the SL 3.3 file for its Amazon growth-vs-environment trade-off) is rooted partly in highly unequal land ownership dating from colonial-era plantation agriculture, compounded by large gaps in access to quality education between urban and rural, and Black and white, Brazilians.
Real-world: Chile's 2019 social unrest ("estallido social") was triggered by a subway fare rise but rapidly became a nationwide protest against deep inequality in pensions, healthcare, and education — despite Chile's relatively strong GDP growth record, illustrating that growth alone does not guarantee falling inequality.

The Impact of Inequality on Growth, Living Standards, and Social Stability

Potential Costs of High Inequality

  • Can depress long-run growth if it limits access to education/credit for the poor, wasting human capital
  • Weaker aggregate demand if lower-income households (who spend a higher share of income) cannot spend as much
  • Lower standards of living and higher rates of ill-health, crime, and reduced social mobility for the poorest
  • Social and political instability — protest, unrest, and reduced trust in institutions (e.g. Chile, above)

Possible Arguments in Favour of Some Inequality

  • Income differences can incentivise effort, risk-taking, entrepreneurship, and skill acquisition
  • A degree of inequality may be needed to reward innovation and attract investment
  • Excessive redistribution (very high marginal tax rates) can reduce incentives to work or invest
Exam tip: the strongest evaluative answers weigh how much inequality and what kind (inequality of opportunity vs inequality of outcome), rather than treating "inequality" as uniformly good or bad. A moderate income gap that rewards effort is very different from entrenched, inherited inequality of opportunity.

Taxation and Policies to Reduce Poverty and Inequality

Progressive tax
The average tax rate rises as income rises — higher earners pay a larger proportion of their income in tax.
Regressive tax
The average tax rate falls as income rises — lower earners pay a larger proportion of their income in tax (common with flat-rate indirect taxes, since poorer households spend a larger share of income on taxed goods).
Proportional tax
The average tax rate stays constant regardless of income level — everyone pays the same percentage.
Average tax rate
Total tax paid ÷ total income, expressed as a percentage.
Marginal tax rate
The proportion of the next dollar/pound/rand of income that is paid in tax.
TypeExamples
Direct taxesPersonal income tax, corporate income tax, wealth (or property/estate) tax — levied directly on income or assets, usually progressive.
Indirect taxesVAT/sales tax, excise duties — levied on spending, often regressive since lower-income households spend a higher share of income on taxed consumption.
Worked Example — Average Tax Rate (Single Bracket)
A worker earns $40,000 and pays $8,000 in income tax.
Average tax rate = (8,000 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = 20%
Reverse-Direction: Finding Tax Paid
A worker earning $55,000 faces an average tax rate of 24%. Find the tax paid.
Tax paid = 0.24 × 55,000 = $13,200
Worked Example — Marginal Tax Rate
A worker's income rises from $50,000 to $60,000 (an extra $10,000), and their tax bill rises from $10,000 to $13,500 (an extra $3,500).
Marginal tax rate = (3,500 ÷ 10,000) × 100 = 35% — noticeably higher than the average rate on the original $50,000 (20%), consistent with a progressive tax system.
Note: calculating total tax and average tax rates from a full data set across multiple income brackets, and calculating the amount of indirect tax paid from a given tax rate and expenditure level, are both HL-only skills — covered in the HL extension file.

Further policies to reduce poverty, income, and wealth inequality:

PolicyHow it works
Investment in human capital / reducing inequality of opportunityExpanding access to quality education and training raises future earning potential for disadvantaged groups.
Transfer paymentsDirect government payments (unemployment benefits, pensions, child benefit) to low-income households, redistributing income without requiring work in return.
Targeted spending on goods and servicesGovernment provision of merit goods (healthcare, housing, education) directly to those in need, reducing the cost of essentials for low-income households.
Universal basic income (UBI)An unconditional, regular cash payment to all citizens regardless of income or employment status, intended to guarantee a minimum standard of living.
Policies to reduce discriminationAnti-discrimination legislation and enforcement aimed at equalising access to jobs, pay, and credit.
Minimum wagesA legal wage floor intended to raise the incomes of the lowest-paid workers (see SL 3.7 for the labour-market effects and evaluation of minimum wages).
Real-world: Mexico's IVA (VAT) exempts basic foodstuffs and medicines specifically to reduce its regressive impact on low-income households, while its Prospera/Bienestar cash-transfer programmes have directly targeted rural poverty through conditional and unconditional transfers.
Real-world: several pilot UBI schemes have been trialled in Kenya (by the NGO GiveDirectly, one of the largest and longest-running UBI trials in the world) as a test of whether unconditional cash transfers reduce poverty as effectively as, or more cost-effectively than, targeted in-kind aid.
Real-world: Brazil's Bolsa Família conditional cash transfer programme, launched in 2003 under President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, pays low-income families a monthly cash transfer conditional on children attending school and completing vaccination schedules. Combined with a rising minimum wage and pension expansion, it is widely credited by economists as a major contributor to the fall in Brazil's Gini coefficient during the 2000s and 2010s. It was briefly rebranded and expanded as Auxílio Brasil in 2021 before being reinstated under its original name and expanded further in 2023 — a reminder that anti-poverty transfer programmes can persist across changes of government even as their design and generosity shift.
Real-world: the UK's Universal Credit, introduced from 2013 and rolled out nationally through the following decade, merged six separate means-tested benefits (including Jobseeker's Allowance and Housing Benefit) into a single monthly payment, intended to simplify the welfare system and strengthen work incentives. Critics have pointed to its initial five-week wait for a first payment as a cause of hardship and increased reliance on food banks for some claimants — a reminder that the design details of a transfer payment system (timing, conditionality, withdrawal rates), not just its headline existence, shape its actual effect on poverty.
StrategyExampleHow it targets poverty/inequality
Conditional cash transferBrazil's Bolsa Família (2003–)Cash paid only if households meet conditions (school attendance, health checks) — targets both current poverty and future human capital.
Universal basic income (trial)Kenya, GiveDirectly (2016–)Unconditional, regular cash transfer regardless of income or behaviour — tests whether trust in recipients outperforms conditionality.
Consolidated means-tested benefitUK Universal Credit (2013–)Merges multiple benefits into one, withdrawn gradually as earnings rise, aiming to preserve work incentives while still targeting the poorest.
Universal welfare state (tax-and-transfer)Sweden/DenmarkHigh progressive taxation funds universal (not means-tested) services and transfers, reducing both relative poverty and the Gini coefficient (Section 2).
Cross-reference: the choice between these approaches is really a question of fiscal policy design (3.6) — how much revenue a government can raise through taxation, and how it chooses to spend it, shapes which of these strategies is affordable. Evaluating any one of them also connects back to the macroeconomic objectives in 3.3: a policy that reduces poverty but significantly weakens work incentives or slows growth may improve one objective (equity) at the expense of another (efficiency/growth).

Where this shows up on the papers: in a Paper 1 essay, part (a) (≈10 marks, AO1/AO2) typically asks you to explain a cause of inequality/poverty or how a named tax works; part (b) (≈15 marks, AO3) then asks you to evaluate government policies to reduce poverty or inequality, almost always rewarding named real-world examples (Bolsa Família, Universal Credit, the Nordic welfare states). Paper 2 has no (a)/(b) essay split — instead you answer one of two 40-mark questions, each built from two linked, numbered "Text" extracts (Text A ¶1, ¶2…) plus data tables, with every sub-question citing an exact reference such as "(Text A, paragraph 3)" or "(Table 2)". The mark ladder is consistent: two 2-mark "define the term…" questions on bolded terms from the text (e.g. Gini coefficient, relative poverty), then a 2–3 mark calculation — often paired with a 2-mark "sketch a Lorenz curve to show…" request (note: "sketch" for this small in-text diagram, not "draw", which IB reserves for a larger standalone diagram question) — then a run of 4-mark "using [a named diagram]/information from [Text/Table], explain how/why…" questions each anchored to a specific paragraph or table, finishing with one 15-mark "discuss" or "evaluate" question that weighs something specific, often across "two" named things (e.g. "discuss the impact of the tax reform on the country's ability to achieve two sustainable development goals"). HL students meet the Lorenz-curve-construction and multi-bracket tax calculations only on Paper 3, where the final sub-part is framed as "recommend a policy" rather than "evaluate" — see the HL extension file.

Exam tips:
  1. Never confuse the average tax rate (total tax ÷ total income) with the marginal tax rate (extra tax ÷ extra income) — a progressive system has a marginal rate above the average rate.
  2. When asked whether a tax is progressive, regressive or proportional, compare average tax rates at two different income levels rather than looking at the tax rate alone.
  3. A Lorenz curve or Gini coefficient question at SL will usually ask you to interpret a given curve/value (e.g. "explain what a rightward shift of the Lorenz curve indicates") rather than calculate one from raw data — that construction skill is reserved for HL.
  4. Use specific real-world examples (Namibia/Iceland for Gini contrast; Rwanda/Bangladesh for MPI progress; Chile for inequality-driven unrest) to anchor Paper 1 evaluative essays.
  5. Always state whether a policy targets the causes of inequality (e.g. education access) or the symptoms (e.g. cash transfers) — examiners reward this distinction in evaluation.
