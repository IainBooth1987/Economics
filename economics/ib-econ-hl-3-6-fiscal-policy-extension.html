The Keynesian Multiplier HL ONLY

The SL chapter treats a change in government spending or taxation as if it changes GDP by exactly that amount. In reality, an initial injection of spending triggers successive further rounds of spending as newly-earned income is re-spent through the economy — the multiplier effect. This is core Paper 3 (HL-only) content: you are expected to calculate the multiplier and use it to find the effect on GDP of a given change in an injection.

Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)
The proportion of an extra unit of income that a household spends on domestic goods and services, rather than saving, paying in tax, or spending on imports.
Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)
The proportion of an extra unit of income that is saved rather than spent — a "leakage" from the circular flow of income.
Marginal Propensity to Tax (MPT)
The proportion of an extra unit of income that is paid in tax — another leakage from the circular flow.
Marginal Propensity to Import (MPM)
The proportion of an extra unit of income spent on imported goods and services — a third leakage, since this spending flows abroad rather than round the domestic economy.
The Multiplier (k)
The factor by which a change in an injection (government spending, investment, or exports) is multiplied to give the total, final change in GDP, once every round of re-spending is accounted for.
Multiplier (k) = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM)
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Multiplier and the Effect on GDP
A country's MPC is 0.6.

Step 1 — find the multiplier: k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5

Step 2 — apply it to a change in an injection: if government spending rises by $200 million (ΔG = $200m), the total change in real GDP is:
ΔGDP = k × ΔG = 2.5 × $200m = $500 million

Alternative method using the leakages directly: suppose instead we are told MPS = 0.2, MPT = 0.15, and MPM = 0.15 (which together sum to 0.4, consistent with MPC = 0.6, since MPC + MPS + MPT + MPM = 1).
k = 1 ÷ (0.2 + 0.15 + 0.15) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5 — the same answer, confirming the two formulae are equivalent.
Fig: successive rounds of an injection push AD from AD₁ to AD₂ to AD₃. The multiplier is largest while the economy has spare capacity (Y₁→Y₂, mostly extra output, little extra inflation) and smallest as the economy nears full capacity (Y₂→Y₃, where a growing share of the extra AD instead raises the price level).
Exam Tip (Paper 3): always show your working in the order: (1) state the formula, (2) substitute the given values to find k, (3) multiply k by the given change in the injection, (4) state the final answer with correct units (e.g. "$500 million," not just "500"). Markers award process marks even if the final figure is wrong, so never skip straight to an answer.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Working Backwards from GDP Data
Paper 3 does not always hand you MPC directly — it often gives you the before-and-after GDP figures and the size of the injection, and expects you to derive the multiplier (and sometimes MPC) yourself.

Question: Government spending rises by $200 million. As a result, real GDP rises by $800 million. Calculate the value of the multiplier, and hence the country's MPC.

Step 1 — find the multiplier from the data given: k = ΔGDP ÷ ΔG = $800m ÷ $200m = 4

Step 2 — work backwards to MPC using k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC):
4 = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC)  →  (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25  →  MPC = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75

The Tax Multiplier and the Balanced Budget Multiplier

The syllabus calculation is scoped to a change in an injection — government spending (G), investment (I), or exports (X). A change in taxation works differently, and it is worth understanding why, since Paper 3 stimulus material sometimes combines a spending change with a tax change in the same question.

Tax Multiplier
The multiplier that applies to a change in taxation: ktax = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC). It is smaller in size (and opposite in sign) than the spending multiplier, because a tax change first has to pass through the marginal propensity to consume before it starts circulating — some of the tax cut is saved, taxed again, or spent on imports before it becomes new domestic spending.
Balanced Budget Multiplier
If government spending and taxation both rise by the same amount (ΔG = ΔT), real GDP still rises — by exactly that same amount. The balanced budget multiplier always equals 1, regardless of the size of MPC.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Comparing the Spending and Tax Multipliers
A country has MPC = 0.75 (so k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4, as in the example above).

Spending multiplier: kG = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 4
Tax multiplier: ktax = −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC) = −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3

So a $100m rise in G raises GDP by 4 × $100m = +$400m, but a $100m cut in T (a tax cut, so ΔT = −$100m) raises GDP by only (−3) × (−$100m) = +$300m — smaller than the spending effect, because part of the tax cut is saved rather than spent.

Balanced budget check: if G rises by $100m and T rises by $100m in the same year: ΔGDP = (kG × ΔG) + (ktax × ΔT) = (4 × $100m) + (−3 × $100m) = $400m − $300m = +$100m — confirming the balanced budget multiplier of exactly 1, however large MPC is.
Exam Tip (Paper 3): the balanced budget multiplier result (always exactly 1) is a popular "show that" style question — you may be asked to demonstrate it algebraically or numerically rather than just state it. Note also that it only holds when ΔG = ΔT exactly; if the two differ in size, calculate each effect separately using kG and ktax and add them together, taking care with signs.
★ REAL WORLD: Why Multiplier Size Varies Between Countries
The multiplier is not a fixed number — it depends on an economy's marginal propensities, which vary considerably by country. Economies with a high marginal propensity to import (small, very open economies such as Singapore or the Netherlands) tend to have smaller multipliers, because more of each round of new spending immediately leaks abroad rather than circulating domestically. Larger, more closed economies (such as the United States, or China, where investment is also heavily state-directed) tend to have comparatively larger domestic multipliers. This is one reason a $1 stimulus package does not have the same GDP impact in every country, and why examiners reward answers that note the multiplier depends on the specific leakages (MPS, MPT, MPM) of the economy being discussed, not a universal constant.
ContextTypical Multiplier EstimateWhy

Case Study: The IMF's 2013 Multiplier "Mea Culpa"

Perhaps the most examinable real-world test of the multiplier concept came from the very institution that had been using it to design bailout programmes.

2010–12: the IMF and European Commission designed austerity programmes for Greece, Portugal, Ireland and Spain assuming a fiscal multiplier of around 0.5 — implying that spending cuts would shrink GDP by only half as much as the cuts themselves. → 2010–13, in practice: the target economies contracted far more sharply than forecast — Greek real GDP fell by roughly a quarter over the period, with unemployment peaking above 27%. → January 2013: IMF Chief Economist Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh published an IMF working paper, "Growth Forecast Errors and Fiscal Multipliers," conceding that actual multipliers during the crisis had been in the range of 0.9 to 1.7 — up to three times larger than assumed. → Interpretation: because interest rates across the Eurozone were already near zero, monetary policy could not cut rates further to offset the fiscal tightening, so each euro of spending cuts destroyed far more output than the standard "normal times" multiplier predicted — a textbook illustration of why the multiplier is context-dependent, not a fixed number.
Why This Matters for Evaluation: this episode is one of the strongest pieces of real-world evidence IB examiners look for in a top-band evaluation of fiscal policy — it shows that the same policy (spending cuts) can have a much larger, more damaging effect than models predict when the multiplier is under-estimated, especially when monetary policy (3.5) has no room left to cushion the blow. It also strengthens the case, discussed in Section 2 below, that discretionary austerity timed badly (near the zero lower bound) can be self-defeating if it shrinks GDP — and therefore tax revenue — by more than it cuts spending.

Crowding Out HL ONLY

One of the key constraints on fiscal policy introduced at SL is sustainable debt. At HL, you are expected to explain the specific mechanism by which government borrowing can undermine the effectiveness of expansionary fiscal policy — crowding out.

Crowding Out
A rise in government borrowing to finance a budget deficit increases demand for loanable funds, pushing up the interest rate and reducing private sector investment — partly or fully offsetting the intended expansionary effect of the fiscal stimulus.
Loanable Funds Market
The market in which savers (the supply of loanable funds) and borrowers, including firms seeking investment finance and now government seeking to finance its deficit (the demand for loanable funds), interact to determine the market interest rate.
Fig: government deficit spending adds to the demand for loanable funds (D₁ → D₂), raising the equilibrium interest rate (r₁ → r₂). The higher interest rate makes some private investment projects unprofitable — private investment is "crowded out."
1
Government runs a larger budget deficit

To finance increased spending or tax cuts without immediately raising other taxes, the government borrows by issuing more bonds.

2
Demand for loanable funds rises

Government borrowing adds to existing private-sector demand for loanable funds in the market for savings.

3
The equilibrium interest rate rises

With supply of loanable funds (savings) unchanged, higher demand pushes up the price of borrowing — the interest rate.

4
Private investment falls

Some investment projects that were profitable at the old, lower interest rate are no longer profitable at the new, higher rate, so firms cancel or postpone them.

5
The net effect on AD is smaller than the government spending increase alone would suggest

In the extreme "full crowding out" case, the fall in private investment (ΔI) exactly cancels the rise in government spending (ΔG), leaving GDP unchanged.

✓ When Crowding Out Is Weak

Spare capacity in the economy

In a recession, savers have surplus funds seeking a home and interest rates are typically already low, so government borrowing can be absorbed without pushing rates up much.

Central bank accommodation

If the central bank is simultaneously holding rates low or purchasing government bonds itself (quantitative easing), the rise in the interest rate that crowding out depends on may not happen at all.

✗ When Crowding Out Is Strong

Economy at or near full employment

With little spare saving capacity, government borrowing competes directly with private borrowers for a limited pool of funds, pushing rates up more sharply.

Persistently high deficits

Large, sustained deficits (rather than a one-off stimulus) raise the risk that investors demand a higher return to keep holding government debt, reinforcing the rate rise.

★ REAL WORLD: Why Japan Has Avoided Severe Crowding Out
Textbook crowding-out theory would predict that Japan — which runs the highest government debt-to-GDP ratio of any major economy — should have seen very high interest rates as government borrowing competed for loanable funds. In practice, Japanese government bond yields have stayed extremely low for decades. A major reason is that the Bank of Japan itself has purchased a very large share of outstanding Japanese government bonds as part of its own monetary policy (quantitative easing, covered in the 3.5 HL extension), directly absorbing much of the government's borrowing rather than leaving it to compete with private firms in the loanable funds market. This is a valuable evaluation point: crowding out is a theoretical prediction of the standard loanable funds model, but its real-world size depends heavily on what the central bank is doing at the same time.

Automatic Stabilisers HL ONLY

So far, this chapter has focused on discretionary fiscal policy — deliberate decisions by government to change spending or tax rates. Automatic stabilisers are a different mechanism: features of the tax and benefits system that automatically reduce the severity of the business cycle without any new legislation being passed.

Automatic Stabilisers
Elements of government spending and taxation that change automatically as the economy moves through the business cycle, without any new policy decision, acting to reduce (dampen) the size of economic fluctuations.
Progressive Taxation (as a Stabiliser)
As incomes rise in a boom, a progressive tax system takes a rising share of income in tax automatically, cooling demand; as incomes fall in a recession, the tax take falls automatically, cushioning the fall in disposable income.
Unemployment Benefits (as a Stabiliser)
In a downturn, rising unemployment automatically triggers higher total benefit payments, replacing some of the lost income and partly supporting consumption; in a boom, falling unemployment automatically reduces total benefit payments.
1
The economy enters a downturn

Incomes fall and unemployment rises — with no new government policy decision required.

2
Tax revenue falls automatically

Lower incomes mean lower income tax paid (especially under a progressive system, where falling into a lower tax bracket reduces the average tax rate too); lower spending means lower indirect tax revenue (e.g. VAT).

3
Benefit payments rise automatically

More people become eligible for unemployment benefit as job losses rise, without any change in the benefit rate or eligibility rules.

4
Disposable income falls by less than it otherwise would

The combination of lower tax and higher benefit payments replaces part of the lost market income.

5
The fall in consumption, and therefore in AD, is smaller than it would be without stabilisers

The downturn is dampened — shallower and often shorter than it would otherwise be — even though the government has taken no new discretionary action.

Reverse chain (in a boom): incomes and employment rise → tax revenue rises automatically (progressive tax bracket effects) → benefit payments fall automatically as fewer people claim unemployment benefit → disposable income rises by less than the increase in market income → the rise in consumption and AD is dampened — reducing the risk of an unsustainable, overheating boom.
Automatic vs. Discretionary — A Common Exam Confusion: automatic stabilisers require no new policy decision — they are built into the existing structure of the tax and benefits system and respond mechanically to the business cycle. Discretionary fiscal policy (the rest of this chapter) requires an active government decision to change a spending or tax rate. A government can rely on both at once: automatic stabilisers work continuously in the background, while discretionary policy is used for a deliberate, additional push when the automatic response alone is judged insufficient.
★ REAL WORLD: How Strong Automatic Stabilisers Are Varies Across Welfare Systems
The strength of automatic stabilisers depends directly on how generous and how progressive a country's tax and benefits system is. Continental European welfare states — Denmark's "flexicurity" model is a widely-cited example, combining flexible hiring/firing rules with generous unemployment benefits and active retraining support — have historically had strong automatic stabilisers, cushioning downturns significantly without any new legislation. The United States, by contrast, has traditionally had less generous and shorter-duration unemployment insurance (though it was topped up substantially, on a discretionary basis, during the 2020 pandemic), meaning a larger share of any US stabilisation in a downturn has to come from discretionary policy rather than automatic stabilisers. This is a useful comparative evaluation point: countries are not equally reliant on automatic stabilisers, and this affects how much discretionary fiscal policy they subsequently need.
Cross-Reference: progressive taxation's role as an automatic stabiliser is the same mechanism examined for its equity effects in 3.4 (redistributing income and lowering the Gini coefficient) — here the emphasis is macroeconomic stabilisation rather than equity. More generally, how well automatic stabilisers and discretionary fiscal policy together deliver the objectives from 3.3 (low unemployment, low and stable inflation, growth) is exactly what "effectiveness of fiscal policy" questions (evaluated in full in the SL chapter's Section 5, alongside crowding out here) are asking you to weigh — often set alongside the monetary policy tools of 3.5.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5, not 0.2.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Government borrowing adds to demand for loanable funds, raising the equilibrium interest rate.
4

Crowding out is always strongest during a deep recession.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Crowding out is typically weakest in a recession (spare saving capacity, low rates) and strongest near full employment.
5

Automatic stabilisers require no new government legislation to take effect.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. They are built into the existing tax and benefits system and respond mechanically to the business cycle.
6

In a recession, tax revenue automatically rises due to automatic stabilisers.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. In a recession, falling incomes mean tax revenue automatically falls, cushioning the fall in disposable income.
7

The size of the multiplier varies between countries depending on their marginal propensities to save, tax and import.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Very open economies with high MPM tend to have smaller multipliers than larger, more closed economies.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. If the central bank buys government bonds itself, it can hold down the interest rate rise that crowding out depends on — Japan is the clearest real-world example.
9

Discretionary fiscal policy requires a deliberate government decision, unlike automatic stabilisers.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Discretionary policy is an active choice (e.g. a new spending programme); automatic stabilisers respond mechanically without new legislation.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Countries with more generous, more progressive tax and benefits systems (e.g. Denmark) have stronger automatic stabilisers than those with less generous systems.

Where this shows up on the papers: the multiplier calculation is Paper 3 content (HL only, 60 marks; two ~30-mark structured questions, answer both) and appears nowhere else. Each Paper 3 question is built around one extended data set with 7–8 short calculation sub-parts (usually 2 marks, occasionally 4) climbing in difficulty — e.g. multiplier calculations, crowding-out effects, budget deficit/debt-to-GDP arithmetic — with one 4-mark "using a [named] diagram, explain…" sub-part near the end, and a final 10-mark sub-part that asks you to "recommend a policy which could be introduced by the government of [country] to address/reduce [a specific problem]" — note this commands "recommend", not "evaluate"/"discuss", though a top answer still justifies the recommendation and notes a limitation. Crowding out and automatic stabilisers can also appear as AO2/AO3 content within a Paper 1 essay evaluating fiscal policy (commonly paired with the constraints introduced at SL — political pressure, time lags, sustainable debt), or in Paper 2 (40 marks; answer one of two questions, each built from two linked Text extracts with numbered paragraphs and data tables) as a 4-mark "using [a diagram]/information from [Text/Table], explain how/why…" question anchored to a specific paragraph reference. A high-scoring Paper 1 evaluation essay on fiscal policy almost always compares it explicitly against monetary policy (3.5) — noting, for example, that fiscal policy can be undermined by crowding out in a way monetary policy cannot, but that fiscal policy can act even when interest rates are already at the zero lower bound.

The Golden Chain: Calculating the Multiplier and Its Effect on GDP

Identify the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), or the leakages (MPS, MPT, MPM) → calculate the multiplier: k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), or equivalently k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM) → identify the change in the injection (ΔG, ΔI, or ΔX) → multiply: ΔGDP = k × Δinjection → state the final answer with correct units and a one-sentence interpretation of what it means for the economy.

The Golden Chain: Crowding Out

Government runs a larger budget deficit → government borrows more, raising demand for loanable funds → the equilibrium interest rate rises → the cost of borrowing rises for private firms → some previously-profitable investment projects are cancelled or postponed → private investment falls, partly or fully offsetting the rise in government spending → the net effect on AD and GDP is smaller than the headline fiscal stimulus alone would suggest.

Key Exam Tips

1

For any multiplier question, always show the formula, the substitution, and the final calculation explicitly — Paper 3 awards process marks at each stage.

2

Remember MPC + MPS + MPT + MPM = 1 — if a question gives you three of the four propensities, you can always find the fourth by subtraction.

2b

Be ready to work backwards: if you're given ΔGDP and the size of the injection rather than MPC, find k first (k = ΔGDP ÷ Δinjection), then rearrange k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) to find MPC itself.

2c

If a question involves both a spending change and a tax change, use the spending multiplier (1 ÷ (1 − MPC)) for ΔG and the smaller tax multiplier (−MPC ÷ (1 − MPC)) for ΔT — do not apply the same multiplier to both, and watch your signs on a tax cut versus a tax rise.

3

When evaluating fiscal policy at HL, always weigh discretionary policy against crowding out and against automatic stabilisers — a top answer distinguishes clearly between the three.

4

Draw the loanable funds diagram correctly for crowding out: label S, D₁, D₂ (shifted right), and show both the old and new equilibrium interest rate and quantity.

5

Use real-world context to earn application marks: reference how the size of the multiplier or the degree of crowding out varies by country (e.g. Japan's limited crowding out due to Bank of Japan bond purchases, or differing automatic stabiliser strength across welfare systems).
