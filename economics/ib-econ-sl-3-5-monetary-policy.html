What Is Monetary Policy?

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and the money supply by a country's central bank to influence the level of aggregate demand (AD) in the economy. It is one of the two main demand-side policies available to policymakers — the other being fiscal policy (Section 3.6).

Almost every economy has an independent or semi-independent central bank responsible for conducting monetary policy — examples include the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan, the Reserve Bank of India and the Bank of England. Central banks are typically given operational independence: politicians set the objective (usually a target inflation rate), but the central bank itself decides how to meet it, free from short-term political pressure. This matters because elected governments face an incentive to cut interest rates before elections to boost short-term growth — a "political business cycle" — even where this risks higher inflation later. Independent central banks are judged less likely to make this trade-off.

Monetary Policy
The use of interest rates and the money supply by the central bank to influence the level of economic activity, in particular the rate of inflation.
Central Bank
The institution responsible for a country's monetary policy, currency issuance, and (usually) the stability of the banking system. Distinct from a country's Treasury or Ministry of Finance, which controls fiscal policy.
Operational Independence
The central bank is free to decide how to meet its inflation target without day-to-day political interference, even though the target itself is usually set by government.
Policy Rate
The interest rate set by the central bank (also called the base rate, discount rate, or refinancing rate depending on the country) which influences the rates commercial banks charge households and firms.
★ REAL WORLD: Central Bank Independence Around the World
Central bank independence became the global norm from the 1990s onward. New Zealand's Reserve Bank was an early pioneer of formal inflation targeting (1990); the Bank of England gained operational independence in 1997; the European Central Bank was designed from its 1998 founding to be one of the most independent central banks in the world, explicitly barred from taking instructions from EU governments; the Bank of Japan Law was revised in 1998 to give the Bank of Japan greater formal independence from the Ministry of Finance. Not every central bank is independent, however — some governments retain direct influence over interest rate decisions (Turkey's central bank has faced repeated political pressure to cut rates even during high inflation — see the case study in Section 7), which critics argue makes those economies more vulnerable to politically-motivated policy and higher long-run inflation.

The precise design of "independence" varies considerably between countries — who sets the numerical target, who sits on the rate-setting committee, and how insulated the governor is from removal all differ:

The Goals of Monetary Policy

Central banks pursue several, sometimes competing, objectives through monetary policy. The IB syllabus identifies five core goals.

Low & Stable Inflation
Most central banks operate a formal inflation target (commonly around 2%). Price stability is usually treated as the primary objective because high or volatile inflation erodes real incomes, distorts price signals, and undermines confidence in the currency.
Low Unemployment
Monetary policy can be used to stimulate AD and support job creation when the economy is operating below full employment, although most central banks treat this as secondary to price stability.
Reduce Business Cycle Fluctuations
By adjusting interest rates counter-cyclically — cutting rates in a downturn, raising them in a boom — the central bank aims to smooth the amplitude of the business cycle rather than eliminate it entirely.
Promote a Stable Environment for Long-Term Growth
Low, predictable inflation and a stable interest rate environment make it easier for firms to plan investment with confidence, supporting long-run productive capacity (LRAS).
External Balance
Interest rate changes affect hot money flows and the exchange rate (see Unit 4), which in turn affect the current account balance — so monetary policy can be used, directly or indirectly, to help manage external balance.
Inflation Targeting: a framework in which the central bank publicly commits to a numerical inflation target (e.g. 2% per year) and adjusts its policy rate to hit it. Inflation targeting is credited with anchoring inflation expectations — if households and firms believe the central bank will act to keep inflation low, they build low inflation into their own wage and price-setting decisions, which itself helps keep inflation low and stable (a self-reinforcing effect).

Real versus Nominal Interest Rates

Interest rates are quoted in two ways, and confusing them is a common source of lost marks. The nominal interest rate is the percentage rate actually charged or paid, unadjusted for inflation. The real interest rate adjusts the nominal rate for the rate of inflation, and reflects the true change in purchasing power a saver or lender experiences.

Real Interest Rate ≈ Nominal Interest Rate − Rate of Inflation

This is known as the Fisher equation (approximate form). It matters because a nominal interest rate can look attractive while still leaving a saver worse off in real terms if inflation is high enough.

★ WORKED EXAMPLE: Calculating the Real Interest Rate
A bank offers a nominal savings rate of 6% per year. Inflation over the same year is 4%.

Real interest rate = 6% − 4% = 2%.

The saver's money grows by 6% in nominal (money) terms, but because prices have risen by 4%, their real purchasing power has only grown by approximately 2%. If inflation had instead been 7%, the real interest rate would be negative (6% − 7% = −1%) — the saver's money would buy less at the end of the year than at the start, despite earning positive nominal interest.
★ WORKED EXAMPLE (Paper 3, Reverse Direction): Finding the Nominal Rate or the Inflation Rate
A central bank wants savers to receive a real interest rate of 3%. Inflation is forecast at 5%.

Rearrange the Fisher equation: Nominal interest rate = Real interest rate + Rate of inflation.
Nominal interest rate = 3% + 5% = 8%.

The bank must set the nominal rate at 8% for savers to earn a real return of 3% given 5% inflation. Equally, Paper 3 may instead give the nominal rate and the real rate and ask for inflation: Rate of inflation = Nominal interest rate − Real interest rate — e.g. a nominal rate of 8% and a real rate of 3% implies inflation of 8% − 3% = 5%. Whichever two values you are given, rearrange the same triangle to find the third.
Exam Tip: always show your working for real interest rate calculations explicitly — state the formula, substitute the values, and give a one-sentence interpretation of what the answer means for savers or borrowers. A bare number with no formula or interpretation rarely earns full marks.

The Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanism

It is not enough to state that "a rate cut increases AD" — top-mark answers trace the full transmission mechanism: the complete chain of effects through which a change in the policy rate works its way through to real output and inflation. Central banks identify several distinct channels through which this happens, each with its own speed and reliability.

Fig: the policy rate feeds through five channels — market rates, asset prices, expectations, the exchange rate, and credit availability — into total demand and, ultimately, inflation.

Channel 1: Market Interest Rates — the Direct Cost of Borrowing

This is the most direct channel. Because commercial banks borrow from (and deposit with) the central bank, any change in the policy rate directly changes the cost of funds for commercial banks, which they pass on to their own customers.

1
Central bank cuts the policy rate

It becomes cheaper for commercial banks to borrow funds from the central bank.

2
Commercial banks cut their own interest rates

Banks lower rates on loans and mortgages, and lower savings/deposit rates, to protect their margins on the new, cheaper funding.

3
Cost of borrowing falls for households and firms

Mortgages, car loans and credit cards become cheaper; the return on saving falls, reducing the incentive to save rather than spend.

4
Consumption (C) and investment (I) rise

Households increase credit-financed spending; firms find more investment projects profitable at the lower cost of capital.

5
Aggregate demand rises

Higher C and I shift AD to the right, putting upward pressure on real output and, eventually, the price level.

Reverse chain (rate RISES): policy rate rises → commercial banks raise lending and savings rates → cost of borrowing rises → consumption and investment fall → AD falls → downward pressure on inflation.
Deeper Look — Two Competing Effects on Saving: a rate cut does not unambiguously reduce saving. The substitution effect says a lower return on saving makes spending today relatively more attractive than saving, so consumption rises. But the income effect works the other way for savers who are relying on interest income (e.g. retirees living off deposit interest): a lower rate means they earn less, so they may need to save more, not less, to reach a target income or nest egg, cutting current consumption. For most economies the substitution effect dominates for the average household, which is why the standard transmission story assumes consumption rises — but the income effect explains why the relationship is not perfectly reliable, especially in economies with a large population of savers depending on interest income.
★ REAL WORLD: Why the Speed of Pass-Through Differs Between Countries
The market interest rate channel does not work at the same speed everywhere. In economies where most mortgages are variable-rate or reset frequently (the UK and Australia are commonly-cited examples), a policy rate change reaches household budgets within weeks. In economies where most mortgages are long-term fixed-rate (common in much of continental Europe, North America, and Japan, where mortgage rates have stayed extremely low and stable for decades alongside the Bank of Japan's near-zero policy rate), the majority of existing borrowers are insulated from a rate change until they refinance or take out a new loan — so the same policy move takes far longer to feed through to aggregate demand. This is one reason central banks in different countries can see very different real-economy responses to similar-sized interest rate changes, and why examiners reward answers that note transmission speed depends on a country's financial structure, not just the size of the rate change.

Channel 2: Asset Prices and the Wealth Effect

Changes in the policy rate alter the pricing of financial and physical assets — bonds, equities, and property — affecting household wealth and, through the wealth effect, consumption spending.

1
Policy rate falls

Yields on newly-issued bonds fall, making existing higher-coupon bonds relatively more attractive.

2
Existing bond prices rise

Higher demand for existing bonds pushes their price up.

3
Equity and property prices tend to rise

Lower discount rates make future company profits worth more today; cheaper mortgages support demand for housing.

4
Household wealth rises — the positive wealth effect

Households who hold bonds, equities and property (directly or through pensions) feel wealthier.

5
Consumer confidence and spending rise

Discretionary spending increases, particularly on non-essentials and big-ticket items.

6
AD rises

Higher consumption reinforces the effect of the market rates channel.

Reverse chain (rate RISES): policy rate rises → new bond yields rise → existing bond prices fall → equity and property prices tend to fall → household wealth falls (negative wealth effect) → confidence and spending fall → AD falls.

Channel 3: The Exchange Rate — Hot Money and Net Exports

Hot money refers to short-term capital flows between countries driven by interest rate differentials. If a country's interest rate rises relative to others, its assets offer a better return for the same risk, attracting inflows and shifting demand for its currency.

1
Central bank cuts the policy rate

Domestic assets now offer a lower return relative to other countries.

2
Hot money flows out of the country

International investors sell the domestic currency to buy assets abroad offering a better return.

3
The domestic currency depreciates

Reduced demand for the currency in the foreign exchange market lowers its value.

4
Exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, imports dearer for domestic buyers

A weaker currency buys less foreign currency, so foreign buyers now need less of their own currency to purchase domestic goods.

5
Net exports (X − M) rise

Exports rise and imports fall, directly increasing AD = C + I + G + (X − M).

Reverse chain (rate RISES): policy rate rises → hot money flows in → currency appreciates → exports dearer for foreign buyers, imports cheaper at home → net exports fall → AD falls (partly reinforced by cheaper imports directly reducing the domestic price level).
The J-Curve Effect: the exchange rate channel is not immediate. Export and import contracts are often fixed in advance, so a depreciation can initially worsen net exports before trade volumes adjust — another reason monetary policy is said to work with "long and variable" lags.
★ REAL WORLD: The Yen Carry Trade
Hot money flows can move faster, and in larger volume, than the "real" trade and investment channels the transmission mechanism otherwise relies on. The clearest long-running example is the yen carry trade: because Japan held interest rates near zero (and briefly negative) for decades, international investors routinely borrowed cheaply in yen and invested the funds in higher-yielding currencies and assets elsewhere, keeping persistent downward pressure on the yen. When the Bank of Japan began signalling higher rates in 2024, investors who had funded these trades rushed to unwind them, causing sharp, rapid swings in the yen and global asset markets — a vivid illustration of how sensitive the exchange rate channel is to interest rate differentials between countries, not just to domestic policy in isolation.

Channel 4: Expectations and Confidence

Households and firms do not only react to today's interest rate — they form expectations about the future and adjust behaviour in anticipation, sometimes before any rate change has fully fed through the banking system.

1
Central bank cuts the rate and signals further cuts ahead

Communications (minutes, forward guidance, press conferences) shape expectations of the future path of rates.

2
Households expect lower future borrowing costs

Even those with existing fixed-rate loans may bring forward planned purchases.

3
Firms revise investment plans upward

Expecting cheaper future borrowing and stronger demand, previously marginal projects now clear the investment hurdle.

4
Confidence rises, spending rises today

The expectations channel can shift AD before the interest rate change has fully passed through the banking system, amplifying the other channels.

Reverse chain (rate RISES): central bank signals further rises ahead → households and firms expect higher future costs → spending and investment plans are revised down in anticipation → confidence falls → AD falls even before full pass-through.

Channel 5: The Credit Channel — Bank Lending and Credit Availability

It is not just the price of borrowing that matters, but its availability. This channel is often the most powerful during a financial crisis, when banks may restrict lending regardless of how low the policy rate falls.

1
Policy rate falls → banks' cost of funds falls

Wider margins give banks room to loosen lending standards without sacrificing profitability.

2
Banks loosen lending criteria

Lower credit score thresholds, smaller deposits required, less collateral demanded — more borrowers are approved.

3
Credit becomes more available

Households and firms who were previously rationed out of credit can now borrow.

4
Household and business borrowing rises

Consumer durables spending and small-business investment — both reliant on bank credit — increase.

5
AD rises

The credit channel reinforces the market rates channel by working through the availability, not just the price, of credit.

Reverse chain (rate RISES): banks' cost of funds rises → lending criteria tighten → credit availability falls (a "credit squeeze") → household and business borrowing falls → AD falls.
★ REAL WORLD: When the Credit Channel Breaks Down
During the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, interbank lending froze as banks became unsure which counterparties held bad debt. Even as central banks — including the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank — cut policy rates aggressively towards zero, many banks refused to lend, and rate cuts had far less effect on the real economy than usual. This breakdown of the credit channel was a key reason central banks turned to quantitative easing (covered in the HL extension) to inject money directly into the financial system, bypassing the broken banking channel. Japan had already experienced a version of this in the 1990s: after its asset-price bubble burst, many Japanese banks kept lending to insolvent "zombie" firms to avoid recognising losses on their own balance sheets, rather than extending credit to healthier new borrowers — a credit-channel breakdown that contributed to Japan's "Lost Decade" of stagnant growth and one reason the term "zombie" is now used more broadly to describe this failure mode.
Key Insight — Lags in Monetary Policy: different channels work at different speeds. The expectations and credit channels can act almost immediately; the market rates channel depends on how quickly existing loans reprice; the exchange rate channel is slowed by the J-Curve; and the asset price channel depends on market conditions. Central banks typically estimate the full effect of a rate change on inflation takes 12–24 months to work through all channels — meaning policy must be forward-looking, set today based on where inflation is forecast to be in one to two years, not where it is right now.

Evaluating the Transmission Mechanism — Does It Always Work?

Examiners reward candidates who go beyond describing the channels and evaluate how reliably the transmission mechanism actually operates. In practice, several things can weaken or completely break the links in the chain:

✓ Why the Mechanism Usually Works

Multiple reinforcing channels

Because five channels operate simultaneously, a weakness in one (e.g. a slow market-rates pass-through) can be partly offset by another (e.g. a fast-moving exchange rate or expectations channel).

Well-anchored expectations

Where a central bank has strong credibility, the expectations channel can shift behaviour almost immediately, speeding up the overall response.

Deep, competitive financial markets

In economies with well-developed banking and capital markets, rate changes are transmitted quickly and predictably to borrowers and savers.

✗ Reasons the Mechanism Can Fail or Weaken

"Pushing on a string"

Even with abundant cheap credit available, firms and households may refuse to borrow and spend if confidence is very low (e.g. deep recession, pandemic uncertainty) — you cannot force people to borrow just because it is cheap.

Already at the zero lower bound

If the policy rate is already close to 0%, there is little or no room left to cut further through conventional means, forcing central banks toward unconventional tools such as quantitative easing. Japan is the clearest example: the Bank of Japan held its policy rate near zero (and briefly below zero) for most of the period from the late 1990s to 2024, leaving conventional rate cuts unavailable for over two decades.

The income effect can dominate for savers

In economies with a large retired or saving-dependent population, a rate cut can perversely increase saving (to protect target income) rather than reduce it, weakening Channel 1.

Debt overhang

Heavily indebted households and firms may use the extra disposable income from lower repayments to pay down existing debt rather than to consume or invest, muting the effect on AD.

Squeezed bank profitability

Very low or negative rates compress the margin between what banks pay depositors and earn on loans; if this damages bank profitability enough, banks may lend less, not more, partially reversing the credit channel.

Overall, the transmission mechanism is best understood as a set of tendencies rather than a guaranteed, mechanical process. Its strength depends on the state of the economy (a downturn with very low confidence blunts it), the structure of the financial system (fixed vs. variable-rate lending, how developed capital markets are), and how much room conventional policy still has before hitting the zero lower bound.

Expansionary Monetary Policy — Closing a Recessionary Gap

Expansionary (loose) monetary policy involves cutting the policy interest rate (and/or increasing the money supply) to stimulate aggregate demand. It is used to close a deflationary/recessionary gap — a negative output gap where real GDP is below the full-employment (potential) level.

Fig: reading right-to-left — the economy starts at AD₂ (recessionary gap: Y₂ below potential Y*). An expansionary rate cut raises C and I, shifting AD back out towards AD₁, moving the economy from (P₂, Y₂) up to (P₁, Y*) and closing the recessionary gap.
1
Central bank cuts the policy rate

Commercial banks' cost of borrowing from the central bank falls.

2
Commercial banks cut lending and savings rates

Mortgages, loans, and overdrafts become cheaper; the return on saving falls.

3
Cost of borrowing falls for households and firms

Lower rates reduce the cost of credit-financed spending and reduce the incentive to save rather than spend.

4
Consumption (C) and investment (I) rise

Households increase spending on credit-financed goods; firms find more investment projects profitable at the lower cost of capital.

5
Aggregate demand shifts right (AD2 → AD1)

AD = C + I + G + (X − M); higher C and I shift the whole AD curve outward, from the recessed AD2 back towards AD1.

6
Real GDP rises and the output gap narrows

Firms respond to higher demand by increasing output and hiring more workers, moving the economy from Y₂ back towards potential output (Y*).

★ REAL WORLD: Global Rate Cuts After the 2008 Financial Crisis
Following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, central banks across the world — including the US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England and the European Central Bank — cut policy rates aggressively, in several cases to close to 0%, in an effort to stimulate collapsing aggregate demand. The Bank of Japan had already been living with near-zero rates for almost a decade by this point, following its own asset-price bubble collapse in the 1990s — Japan's experience is often studied as an early warning of what a prolonged recessionary gap combined with a near-zero policy rate can look like. When conventional rate cuts reached their practical floor, several central banks turned to unconventional tools such as quantitative easing (covered in the HL extension to this chapter).

Contractionary Monetary Policy — Closing an Inflationary Gap

Contractionary (tight) monetary policy involves raising the policy interest rate to reduce aggregate demand. It is used to close an inflationary gap — a positive output gap where real GDP is above the full-employment level and inflationary pressure is building.

Fig: the economy sits at Y1, above potential output (Yfe) — a positive (inflationary) output gap. Contractionary policy shifts AD left, back towards LRAS, moving the economy from (P1, Y1) down towards potential output and closing the inflationary gap.
1
Central bank raises the policy rate

Commercial banks' cost of borrowing from the central bank rises.

2
Commercial banks raise lending and savings rates

Mortgages, loans and overdrafts become more expensive; saving becomes more attractive.

3
Cost of borrowing rises for households and firms

Credit-financed spending becomes more expensive; the opportunity cost of spending rather than saving rises.

4
Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall

Households cut back on big-ticket, credit-financed purchases; fewer investment projects clear the higher cost-of-capital hurdle.

5
Aggregate demand shifts left

Lower C and I shift the whole AD curve inward, reducing inflationary pressure.

6
The price level falls back towards target and the inflationary gap narrows

Real GDP falls back towards the full-employment level, easing demand-pull inflationary pressure.

Compare with expansionary policy: the contractionary chain mirrors Section 4 exactly, but in reverse — a rate rise (not a cut) raises (not lowers) borrowing costs, which reduces (not increases) C and I, shifting AD left (not right) to close an inflationary gap (not a recessionary one). Examiners reward candidates who can run this chain confidently in both directions.

Case Study: The Volcker Disinflation, 1979–1982

The clearest real-world illustration of contractionary monetary policy taken to its most aggressive extreme is Paul Volcker's chairmanship of the US Federal Reserve. By 1979, US inflation had reached almost 13.5%, driven by two oil price shocks (1973 and 1979) and years of accommodative policy. Volcker, appointed Fed Chair by President Carter in August 1979, judged that only a severe, sustained monetary contraction could break the public's expectation that high inflation was here to stay.

1
The Fed shifts its operating target from the interest rate to the money supply

From October 1979, the Fed prioritised controlling the growth rate of bank reserves and the money supply directly, letting the federal funds rate move wherever necessary to hit that target — a deliberate break from smoothing interest rates.

2
The federal funds rate rises to historic highs

The federal funds rate peaked at almost 20% in 1981, and the prime lending rate charged to businesses briefly touched 21.5% — rates unprecedented in US peacetime history.

3
Borrowing collapses across the economy

Mortgage rates above 18% froze the housing market; small businesses and farmers reliant on credit were hit especially hard, with widespread bankruptcies.

4
The US enters a deep double-dip recession (1980, then 1981–82)

Unemployment rose to 10.8% by late 1982 — at the time the highest US unemployment rate since the Great Depression.

5
Inflation expectations break, and inflation falls sharply

Annual inflation fell from around 13.5% in 1980 to roughly 3.2% by 1983, and the credibility this established is widely credited with anchoring US inflation expectations for the following two decades — a real-world example of the "well-anchored expectations" strength discussed in Section 4.

Why Examiners Love This Example: the Volcker disinflation is a genuine, quantifiable trade-off between two macroeconomic objectives — it succeeded completely against the inflation objective while badly failing the unemployment and growth objectives in the short run. It is exactly the kind of case that supports a strong evaluative answer on "to what extent should a central bank prioritise price stability over growth and employment", because both the costs (a severe recession) and the benefit (durable low inflation for decades afterwards) can be stated with real figures.
Naming the Gaps: a deflationary gap (also called a recessionary gap) exists when actual real GDP is below potential/full-employment GDP — there is spare capacity and rising unemployment. An inflationary gap exists when actual real GDP is above potential GDP — the economy is overheating and inflationary pressure is building. Always state which gap a question describes before choosing between expansionary and contractionary policy.

Evaluating Monetary Policy

A strong evaluative answer weighs monetary policy's genuine strengths against its real constraints, rather than simply describing the mechanism.

✓ Strengths of Monetary Policy

Incremental and flexible

Interest rates can be adjusted in small steps and reversed quickly if circumstances change, unlike many fiscal policy measures which are harder to unwind.

Short time lags relative to fiscal policy

Decisions can be implemented quickly once made (no need for parliamentary approval of a budget), although the full effect on the real economy still takes time to feed through.

Set by an independent body

Where the central bank is independent, decisions can be made on economic rather than political grounds, which may improve credibility and effectiveness.

✗ Limitations of Monetary Policy

Limited scope near the zero lower bound

Once the policy rate is already close to 0%, there is very little room left to cut further, weakening the central bank's ability to respond to a severe downturn using conventional tools.

Low consumer and business confidence

If confidence is very weak, households and firms may not respond to lower rates by borrowing and spending more — cutting rates has little effect if nobody wants to borrow regardless of the price of credit.

Time lags still exist

Even though decisions can be made quickly, the transmission mechanism through the banking system and into spending decisions can still take many months to feed through fully.

Effectiveness Against Each Macroeconomic Objective

A top-mark evaluation goes further than generic strengths and limitations — it weighs effectiveness separately against each of the three objectives the syllabus names.

Promoting Growth
Effective when there is genuine spare capacity to absorb the extra demand (a negative output gap) and confidence is reasonably intact — cheaper credit then translates into real investment and consumption. Least effective when the economy is already near full capacity (a rate cut then mostly raises the price level rather than real output) or when confidence is so weak that lower rates fail to encourage borrowing.
Low Unemployment
Works indirectly — lower rates raise AD, and firms hire more workers to meet the higher demand. This channel depends on firms actually expanding output rather than raising prices, and can be slow if firms first respond by using existing spare capacity (labour hoarding) before hiring.
Low and Stable Inflation
Generally regarded as monetary policy's strongest objective — it is the one central banks are usually mandated to prioritise, and the one interest rate changes affect most directly and reliably through the transmission mechanism (Section 4). Even here, effectiveness is weaker against supply-side (cost-push) inflation, since higher interest rates reduce demand but do nothing to address rising costs of production.

Overall effectiveness therefore depends heavily on the starting conditions in the economy — how much room the central bank has to cut or raise rates, how responsive households and firms are likely to be, and whether the underlying problem is a demand-side or supply-side one.

★ REAL WORLD: When Monetary Policy Struggles Against Supply-Side Inflation
In 2021–2023, inflation surged across many economies due largely to supply-side shocks — pandemic-related supply chain disruption and a spike in global energy prices following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Central banks including the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England raised interest rates sharply to bring inflation back to target. Because the underlying shock was on the supply side, this response worked mainly by deliberately weakening demand (and, in several economies, slowing growth and raising unemployment) rather than by addressing the root cause of the price rises — illustrating the real trade-off between the inflation objective and the growth/employment objectives when a shock originates on the supply side. Japan provides an instructive contrast: with weaker domestic demand and a long history of low inflation, the Bank of Japan kept its policy rate near zero for much of this period even as other major central banks raised rates sharply, only beginning to raise its own rate in 2024 — a reminder that the same global supply shock does not call for the same policy response everywhere, since it depends on each economy's own starting position on the business cycle.

Case Study: What Happens When Independence Breaks Down — Turkey, 2021–2023

Section 1 introduced operational independence as a safeguard against a "political business cycle". Turkey's experience under President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan shows what happens when that safeguard fails.

★ REAL WORLD: Turkey's Unorthodox Rate Cuts, 2021–2023
Erdoğan holds the unconventional view that high interest rates cause, rather than cure, inflation, and repeatedly dismissed central bank governors who resisted cutting rates. Under governor Şahap Kavcıoğlu, the Central Bank of the Republic of Turkey (CBRT) cut its policy rate from 19% in early 2021 to 8.5% by early 2023 — even as annual inflation officially peaked at over 85% in October 2022 (independent estimates ran considerably higher). The Turkish lira lost roughly half its value against the US dollar over the same period, and real interest rates were deeply negative for savers throughout, directly illustrating the Fisher equation from Section 3: with inflation running far above the nominal policy rate, real returns were sharply negative, punishing lira-denominated savings. Following his re-election, Erdoğan reversed course in June 2023, appointing Hafize Gaye Erkan (later succeeded by Fatih Karahan) and finance minister Mehmet Şimşek, who raised the policy rate back above 40% by early 2024 in an orthodox attempt to restore credibility. The episode is a textbook illustration of why examiners reward "operational independence" as more than a definitional term — it is the mechanism that, when absent, allows political priorities to override the inflation objective, with a measurable cost in currency depreciation and eroded real incomes.

True or False

Choose True or False for each statement — the explanation only appears once you've answered.

1

Monetary policy is controlled by a country's government, not its central bank.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Monetary policy is controlled by the central bank. Government controls fiscal policy (spending and taxation).
2

An expansionary monetary policy involves raising interest rates.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Expansionary (loose) monetary policy involves cutting interest rates to stimulate AD. Raising rates is contractionary (tight) policy.
3

The real interest rate equals the nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. This is the (approximate) Fisher equation: Real rate ≈ Nominal rate − Inflation rate.
4

A negative real interest rate is impossible.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. If inflation exceeds the nominal interest rate, the real interest rate is negative — savers lose purchasing power even while earning positive nominal interest.
5

A recessionary (deflationary) gap means real GDP is below potential GDP.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. A recessionary/deflationary gap is a negative output gap — actual real GDP sits below the full-employment (potential) level.
6

To close an inflationary gap, a central bank should cut interest rates.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. An inflationary gap calls for contractionary policy — raising rates to reduce AD, not cutting them.
7

Lower interest rates increase both consumption and investment.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Cheaper borrowing increases credit-financed consumption (C) and makes more investment projects (I) profitable at the lower cost of capital.
8

Operational independence means the central bank sets its own inflation target with no government involvement at all.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. Typically the government sets the inflation target; the central bank has operational independence over how to meet it, not over the target itself.
9

One strength of monetary policy is that it is easily reversible compared to some fiscal measures.

Correct —Not quite — TRUE. Interest rate changes can be adjusted incrementally and reversed quickly, unlike certain fiscal measures which are harder to unwind.
10

Monetary policy always works quickly and reliably, regardless of business and consumer confidence.

Correct —Not quite — FALSE. If confidence is very weak, households and firms may not respond to lower rates by borrowing and spending — a key limitation of monetary policy.

The Golden Chain: Expansionary Monetary Policy to Close a Recessionary Gap

Economy has a negative output gap (recessionary/deflationary gap) → central bank cuts the policy interest rate → commercial banks cut lending and savings rates → cost of borrowing falls for households and firms → consumption (C) and investment (I) rise → aggregate demand shifts right (AD2 → AD1) → firms respond to higher demand by increasing output and employment → real GDP rises towards the full-employment level (Y*) → the recessionary gap narrows.

The Golden Chain: Contractionary Monetary Policy to Close an Inflationary Gap

Economy has a positive output gap (inflationary gap) → central bank raises the policy interest rate → commercial banks raise lending and savings rates → cost of borrowing rises for households and firms → consumption (C) and investment (I) fall → aggregate demand shifts left → demand-pull inflationary pressure eases → real GDP falls back towards the full-employment level → the inflationary gap narrows.

Key Exam Tips

1

Always identify the gap first: state clearly whether the economy has a recessionary/deflationary gap or an inflationary gap before choosing between expansionary and contractionary policy.

2

Draw the AD/AS diagram: label LRAS, SRAS, AD1 and AD2, the price level and real GDP axes, and shade or label the output gap being closed.

3

Trace the full chain: examiners want every link — policy rate → commercial bank rates → cost of borrowing → C and I → AD → real GDP/price level — not just "lower rates increase AD".

4

Show real interest rate calculations with the formula stated explicitly: Real rate = Nominal rate − Inflation rate, followed by a one-sentence interpretation.

5

Evaluate, don't just describe: for higher-mark questions, weigh strengths (flexibility, short lags, independence) against limitations (the zero lower bound, weak confidence, remaining time lags).

6

Use real-world context: reference actual episodes of central banks cutting or raising rates (such as the global rate cuts after 2008, or rate rises to combat post-pandemic inflation) to earn application marks.
