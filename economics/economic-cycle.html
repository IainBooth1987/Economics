Economic Cycle & Output Gap

Theory notes · Interactive cycle · Sorting activity · Exam practice — AQA A-Level Economics

The Economic (Business) Cycle

The economic cycle describes the recurring fluctuations in real GDP around its long-run trend. Economies do not grow at a constant rate — output expands and contracts over time, creating periods of boom and recession. Understanding the gap between actual GDP and potential (trend) GDP is central to macroeconomic analysis and to judging which government and central bank policy responses are appropriate.

Actual GDP

The real value of goods and services the economy actually produces in a given period. It fluctuates with the economic cycle, driven mainly by changes in aggregate demand in the short run.

Measured by ONS quarterly national accounts

Potential GDP (Trend Output)

The level of output the economy can produce sustainably without generating rising inflation, when resources are fully and efficiently employed. It grows over time as productive capacity expands through investment, technology and labour supply.

UK trend growth ≈ 2–2.5% a year before 2008; considerably weaker since

The Output Gap

The difference between actual and potential GDP, expressed as a percentage of potential GDP. It shows where the economy sits in the cycle and what inflationary or disinflationary pressure is present.

Output gap = (Actual GDP − Potential GDP) ÷ Potential GDP × 100
Trend Growth vs Actual Growth

Trend (Potential) Growth Rate

The average rate at which productive capacity expands over the long run. It reflects the supply side of the economy, not short-run demand.

  • Determined by growth in the labour force, the capital stock and productivity
  • Growth at the trend rate leaves the output gap unchanged — it is sometimes called the sustainable or non-inflationary growth rate
  • Supply-side policies (education, infrastructure, incentives to invest) aim to raise it
  • UK trend growth has been much weaker since the 2008 financial crisis — the productivity puzzle
  • Shown as an upward-sloping dashed line on the economic cycle diagram

Actual GDP Growth Rate

The rate at which real output actually grew in a period — driven by aggregate demand (C + I + G + X − M) and short-run supply conditions.

  • Cyclical — faster than trend in some periods, slower in others
  • Actual growth above trend: the output gap is moving upwards (a negative gap closes, or a positive gap widens)
  • Actual growth below trend: the output gap is moving downwards (a positive gap closes, or a negative gap widens)
  • Two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth = technical recession
  • Hit by demand-side shocks (confidence, credit, export demand) and supply-side shocks (energy prices, pandemics)

⭐ Examiner Tip — Growth rate is not the same as the gap

  • An economy can be growing and still have a widening negative output gap — if actual growth is below the trend rate.
  • The growth rate tells you which way the gap is moving; the sign of the gap tells you whether output is above or below capacity. Good answers use both.
  • Say which measure you mean: "GDP fell by 0.3%" is an actual growth figure; "the economy is operating 2% below capacity" is an output gap statement.
Positive vs Negative Output Gap

🟢 Positive Output Gap

Actual GDP exceeds potential GDP. The economy is operating above its sustainable capacity — resources are overstretched, creating inflationary pressure.
  • Unemployment below the natural rate; labour shortages emerge
  • Wage growth accelerates, raising firms' costs
  • Firms operating above normal capacity; high capital utilisation
  • Demand-pull inflation as spending outstrips supply
  • Import volumes rise as domestic firms cannot meet demand — the current account tends to worsen
  • Tax revenues rise with incomes, profits and spending, and welfare spending falls — the budget deficit narrows without any change in tax rates (automatic stabilisers)
  • Asset and property prices often rise rapidly
  • Supply chain bottlenecks and shortages appear
Likely policy: contractionary demand management

🔴 Negative Output Gap

Actual GDP is below potential GDP. The economy is operating below its sustainable capacity — spare resources exist, creating disinflationary (or deflationary) pressure.
  • Cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment rises above the natural rate
  • Wage growth slows; real wages may fall
  • Spare capacity — idle machinery and under-used workers
  • Downward pressure on inflation as demand is weak
  • Business confidence falls; investment declines (accelerator effect)
  • Consumer spending is weak; retail sales fall
  • Tax revenues fall and welfare spending rises — automatic stabilisers widen the deficit
  • Unsold inventories build up; firms cut output
Likely policy: expansionary demand management

Positive gap on an AD/AS diagram

AD and SRAS intersect to the right of LRAS: real GDP (Y1) is above full-employment output (Yfe). Firms are producing beyond sustainable capacity, so costs and prices rise until SRAS shifts left or AD falls back.

Negative gap on an AD/AS diagram

AD and SRAS intersect to the left of LRAS: real GDP (Y1) is below full-employment output (Yfe). Resources are idle and there is downward pressure on the price level. Keynesians argue the gap may persist without a policy boost to AD.

The Four Stages of the Economic Cycle

The stages describe the direction the economy is moving. Each stage passes through both signs of output gap — the gap only changes sign when actual GDP crosses the trend line.

📈
Expansion / Recovery
Actual growth > trend
  • A negative gap closes first as spare capacity is used up
  • Once actual GDP crosses trend, a positive gap opens and widens
  • Unemployment falls, usually with a lag
  • Confidence rises; investment accelerates (accelerator effect)
  • Inflation begins to pick up as the gap turns positive
  • Tax revenues rise; deficit narrows
🏔
Peak / Boom
Largest positive gap
  • Output furthest above potential
  • Actual growth has slowed back to the trend rate — that is why the gap stops widening
  • Unemployment at its lowest; labour shortages
  • Inflationary pressure greatest; wage-price spiral risk
  • Asset price bubbles can form
  • Central bank likely to have raised rates
📉
Contraction / Downturn
Actual growth < trend
  • A positive gap closes first — a slowdown, while output is still above capacity
  • Once actual GDP falls below trend, a negative gap opens and widens
  • If real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters: technical recession
  • Unemployment rises; redundancies increase
  • Spending and investment fall
  • Automatic stabilisers widen the deficit
🕳
Trough / Slump
Largest negative gap
  • Output furthest below potential
  • Actual growth has recovered to the trend rate — so the gap stops widening
  • Cyclical unemployment near its highest
  • Disinflationary pressure strongest
  • Confidence at its lowest
  • Base for the next recovery

What Moves the Economy Through the Cycle?

Demand-side shocks

  • Changes in consumer and business confidence ("animal spirits")
  • Credit booms and busts; changes in household debt and asset prices (wealth effects)
  • Changes in interest rates, taxes and government spending
  • Global shocks — a slowdown in trading partners cuts export demand
  • The multiplier and accelerator amplify an initial change in spending

Supply-side shocks

  • Energy and commodity price spikes raise costs and shift SRAS left
  • Pandemics and natural disasters disrupt production
  • Supply chain disruption and changes in trade arrangements
  • Some shocks reduce potential output too, not just actual output
  • A supply shock can combine falling output with rising inflation
Why the Output Gap Is Hard to Measure

Actual GDP is measured (and later revised). Potential GDP is never observed — it has to be estimated. This is one of the strongest evaluation points on the topic.

How it is estimated

  • Statistical trends — smoothing past GDP data to find its underlying path
  • Production function — estimating capacity from labour supply, capital stock and productivity
  • Survey evidence — business surveys of capacity utilisation and recruitment difficulties
  • Bodies such as the OBR, the Bank of England, the OECD and the IMF each publish their own estimates

Why that matters for policy

  • Estimates from different bodies can differ in size — and sometimes even in sign
  • Estimates are often revised heavily after the event as GDP data and trend estimates change
  • Policy set on a mis-measured gap can be mistimed and make the cycle worse
  • A supply shock lowers potential output too, so the gap may be smaller than the fall in GDP suggests
  • So central banks look at a wide range of indicators: wage growth, vacancies, inflation expectations, surveys

⭐ Examiner Tip — Evaluation points on the cycle

  • The cycle is not regular or predictable — shocks (financial crises, pandemics, energy price spikes) change the timing and depth of each stage.
  • Policy lags (recognition, decision, implementation, effect) mean policy can arrive too late and add to volatility.
  • Near the peak, objectives conflict: raising rates to curb inflation risks tipping the economy into recession.
  • Recessions differ in shape: V (sharp and brief), U (prolonged), L (stagnation), W (double-dip).
  • Because the gap is estimated, a strong judgement questions how reliable the figure in the extract is before recommending policy.

    02Explain how the data in Extract A show that the economy was in a slowdown before it entered a technical recession.
    Show answer guidance
    • From Year 1 Q3, actual growth (0.4%, then 0.1%) was below the 0.5% trend rate while output was still positive, so the positive gap was closing — a slowdown.
    • The gap fell from +1.4 to +0.2 while GDP was still growing or only just falling.
    • A technical recession needs two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth: Year 2 Q1 (−0.2%) and Q2 (−0.3%).
    • Data use must be explicit; a definition of technical recession on its own is not enough for a full answer.
    03With the help of an AD/AS diagram, explain why a positive output gap, such as the one in Country A during Year 1, is likely to lead to rising inflation.
    Show answer guidance

    Diagram: AD intersecting SRAS to the right of LRAS, real GDP above Yfe, price level P1 labelled. Better answers show AD shifting right from equilibrium at Yfe, and/or SRAS shifting left as costs rise.

    • Demand-pull: spending exceeds what the economy can sustainably produce, so firms raise prices.
    • Labour market: unemployment below the natural rate — skills shortages bid up wages (Extract A: highest wage growth for a decade), raising costs.
    • Cost-push follows: higher unit labour costs shift SRAS left, adding to the rise in the price level.
    • Firms running above normal capacity face rising marginal costs; bottlenecks raise input prices.
    • Expectations: persistent inflation can feed into wage bargaining — a wage-price spiral.
    • Use of data: CPI rose from 3.1% to 4.0% as the gap stayed above +1%.

    Strong answers form a developed chain of reasoning linked to the diagram and the extract, rather than a list of points.

    04Using the data in Extract A and your knowledge of economics, assess whether Country A's central bank should cut interest rates now that the output gap has turned negative.
    Show indicative content

    Arguments for cutting

    • The gap is negative and widening (−0.6%) with GDP falling — spare capacity will push inflation down.
    • Unemployment has risen from 3.7% to 4.9%; cyclical unemployment has real costs and can become structural (hysteresis).
    • Inflation is already falling quickly (4.0% → 2.6%); monetary policy acts with long lags, so waiting risks undershooting the 2% target later.
    • Transmission: lower rates reduce borrowing costs and saving incentives, supporting C and I, with multiplier effects; a weaker currency may help net exports.

    Arguments against / for waiting

    • CPI (2.6%) is still above target; cutting too early could damage credibility and lift inflation expectations.
    • The gap estimate is uncertain and has already been revised — the "negative gap" may be revised away.
    • If the slowdown partly reflects a supply-side fall in potential output, cutting rates would add to inflation rather than restore output.
    • Earlier rate rises may still be feeding through; cutting now could overshoot.
    • Low confidence may make households and firms unresponsive to lower rates; fiscal policy or automatic stabilisers may be more effective.

    Judgement: a supported conclusion might weigh the speed of disinflation against the risks to credibility, suggest a gradual cut, or make the decision conditional on wage growth and inflation expectations.
